# Derivations — the theory critics' findings (2026-10-08, before any data)

Three adversarial agents checked the draft predictions (`docs/protocol_draft.md`, as first written) against
`pinning/paper/main.tex`, with sympy and toy numerical examples, and without data. Their verdicts are reproduced
as returned; `PROTOCOL.md` section 2 states what was taken from them.

## housing position

### KEY (sign of dP/dq = minus sign of net housing position) — follows with conditions

**Derivation.** Minimal extension of App B: person i with log utility log g - chi n, owns l units of land, has goods-denominated non-labour income a (pensions, benefits, bonds), and needs housing h_w in work and h_e in exit, with exit also yielding home production s0. Work iff chi <= I = log(w + a + q(l - h_w)) - log(s0 + a + q(l - h_e)). Sympy: dI/dq = (l - h_w)/G_w - (l - h_e)/G_e. (a) App B case h_w = h_e = h, s0 = 0: dI/dq = w(h - l)/(A(A + w)), A = a + q(l - h) > 0. So sign(dP/dq) = -sign(l - h): the draft's claim holds here, as a pure income effect from concave utility. (b) Linear utility (the reduced form of Section 7): the participation threshold moves by d(G_w - G_e)/dq = h_e - h_w, and net holdings l cancel. Housing needed in both states therefore cancels exactly. The reduced form s(q) = max(s0 - q h_e, s_under) compared with w is App B only if h_e is read as exit's EXCESS housing over work (h_e - h_w > 0). That is the plot-based historical exit; it is doubtful for modern exit, where work usually requires living near jobs at higher rent. So the reduced form and App B are two different channels: a substitution term (h_e - h_w) and an income term (l - h) that add under log utility. (c) Dependency floor: exit at s_under with the host paying the housing, and work paying its own housing. Then dI/dq = -h_w/(w + a - h_w q) < 0, so participation FALLS with q. A net-short person who has a parental room is pushed toward exit, not enclosed. (d) Substitutable housing (Stone-Geary, necessity hbar; sympy-verified): dI/dq = -w(l - hbar)/(A'(A' + w)), A' = a + q(l - hbar). The relevant position is holdings minus NECESSITY housing, not minus occupied housing. With Cobb-Douglas housing (hbar = 0), renters do not respond at all and every owner, including owner-occupiers, responds negatively. 'Exactly hedged -> no effect' holds only with Leontief housing or with frictions that stop owners from downsizing or renting out. (e) Wage contamination: at a given margin, w/r = b gamma/(1 - a - lambda gamma) is fixed by technology, so the goods wage is w = v q. Then I = log(1 + v/(a/q + l - h)) and dI/dq = a v/(A(A + vq)) > 0 for every group with a > 0, whatever its net position. With a = 0 (all non-labour income in land, exactly App B) q has NO participation effect: everything scales with r. With partial pass-through epsilon in [0,1], the total effect is the net-position term plus epsilon times a positive wage term.

**What the model implies.** Holding the group's goods wage fixed, outside the dependency floor, and with housing a necessity (Leontief h or Stone-Geary hbar): dP_g/dq has the sign of (hbar_g - l_g), where l_g is land/housing held and hbar_g is necessity housing. Groups at the dependency floor (host pays housing in exit, own housing paid in work) have dP_g/dq < 0. The reduced-form enclosure term adds +f*(h_e - h_w)/G_e only where exit is more housing-intensive than work. When q rises with the goods wage passing through c(r), the net-position sign is replaced by the sign of goods-denominated non-labour income (positive for all groups with a > 0), with magnitude falling in A.

**Against standard models.** The net-position income effect is the standard housing-hedging logic of a life-cycle model (Sinai-Souleles hedging; house-price wealth effects by age and tenure, e.g. Campbell-Cocco). It is not distinctive. What is distinctive to the paper is (i) the substitution term h_e > h_w, which predicts a positive q effect even for groups hedged relative to necessity housing, (ii) the dependency-floor kink, which reverses the sign for net-short people with a host, and (iii) the technology-pinned w/r, which ties the goods wage to rent.

**What a contrary result refutes.** A failure of the net-position sign with the goods wage controlled refutes the concave-utility income-effect auxiliary (log utility, Leontief housing), not the paper's mechanism. The reduced-form mechanism is the substitution term h_e - h_w, and App B's mechanism is silent at linear utility. Only a finding that q does not move participation even for groups with no host, no goods-income pass-through, and exit more housing-intensive than work would bear on the paper's enclosure mechanism itself.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- goods wage held fixed when q moves (violated by the paper's own c(r) closure)
- housing is a fixed necessity (Leontief); with substitutable housing the position is relative to necessity housing hbar
- interior: A > 0 and the person is not at the dependency floor
- static, frictionless rental market: owners can realise long positions by renting out or downsizing
- q varies exogenously (supply-driven), not with a labour-demand boom
- no coresidence-in-work option

### P1 — follows with conditions

**Derivation.** From KEY (a): at fixed goods wage, beta_g proportional to f(chi*) * w (hbar_g - l_g)/(A_g(A_g + w)). Young renters (l = 0) are net short, so beta_young > 0. But only interior young count: those with a parental room satisfy KEY (c), dP/dq = -h_w/(w + a - h_w q) < 0, the same force that drives P2 coresidence. Older owner-occupiers are net long relative to necessity housing (KEY d), so beta_old < 0. They are hedged (beta_old = 0) only under Leontief housing and no downsizing. Wage contamination (KEY e): with pass-through the extra term a v/(A(A + vq)) is positive for both groups and larger where A is small (young). It therefore biases beta_old upward, against the 'old lower' half, and beta_young - beta_old upward, in favour of P1. The primary difference can pass even when net position is irrelevant. Cyclical confound: demand-driven rises in q coincide with labour-demand booms, and marginal groups (young) are more cyclical, which also produces beta_young > beta_old. The equilibrium margin response (participation up -> x* down -> w down) dampens all betas in common.

**What the model implies.** Conditional on the group's real wage (or the cycle: group unemployment or output gap) and using rent relative to goods CPI as q: beta_old <= 0, and beta_young > 0 only where young non-coresident renting dominates. The young's response should DECLINE in, and can turn negative with, the coresidence share and the rent-to-young-wage ratio. Primary: beta_young - beta_old > 0, AND beta_old <= 0 tested jointly, with beta_young interacted with the young renter share (positive slope) and with the coresidence share (negative slope).

**Against standard models.** Not distinctive as drafted. A life-cycle model with housing predicts the same signs: house-price and rent increases make prospective buyers and renters save and work more, and housing gains for older owners bring earlier retirement (Disney-Gathergood; Zhao; Li-Yao life-cycle effects). Cyclical sensitivity of marginal groups also yields beta_young > beta_old. The discriminating pieces are (i) the floor kink: the standard concave income-effect model says the young's response GROWS as the rent burden rises (A -> 0), while the model says it vanishes or reverses once dependency is the exit; (ii) flow versus capitalised price: in the static model the young respond to rents, not to house prices, whereas the life-cycle down-payment motive says prices.

**What a contrary result refutes.** beta_young - beta_old <= 0 does not refute the mechanism: the model itself predicts beta_young <= 0 where coresidence is the exit, and a positive old effect via wage pass-through. It refutes the auxiliary group-to-position mapping. A positive difference does not discriminate between the paper and the standard life-cycle or cyclical accounts. The only result here with real bite: the young's q response is not decreasing in the coresidence share or the rent burden (the floor kink is absent).

**Hidden assumptions.**
- 25-34s are mostly renters and not at the dependency floor (varies widely by country: owner shares among young are high in CEE and low in DE/CH)
- 55-64s are net long relative to necessity housing, not merely hedged
- q exogenous to labour demand; wage pass-through via c(r) controlled
- pension reforms and retirement-age changes controlled for 55-64
- the same q measure applies to renters (rent index) and owners (implicitly the price index)

### P3 — ambiguous sign

**Derivation.** Two-adult pooling household: utility log(A + w1 n1 + w2 n2) - chi1 n1 - chi2 n2, A = a + q(l - h), housing h needed in all four states. The second earner's exit is housed by the household, so her reduced-form substitution channel h_e - h_w is OFF: s is at the flat segment and q enters only through the household's net position. Given n1 = 1, the second earner works iff chi2 <= log(1 + w2/(A + w1)), so d(threshold)/dq = w2(h - l)/((A + w1)(A + w1 + w2)). Its sign is the HOUSEHOLD's net position: + if net short, 0 if hedged, - if net long (yes, it depends on being net short). The first earner's threshold shift is w1(h - l)/((A + w2 n2)(A + w2 n2 + w1)), larger whenever w1 > w2, because the second earner's base already includes w1 (concavity dilution). Simulation (joint four-option choice, a = 1, h = 1, w1 = 1, w2 = 0.7): with identical chi ~ U[0,1], renters give dP1/dq = +0.49 > dP2/dq = +0.31, hedged give 0/0, net long give -0.14/-0.09. beta_W > beta_M appears only when men's chi has a thin tail (P1 ~ 0.999: dP1/dq = +0.006 vs dP2/dq = +0.27). That is an auxiliary assumption about the density at the margin, not the mechanism. Wage contamination: pass-through raises w2 (an own-wage effect, positive on her elastic margin) and w1 (an income effect, negative for her). Net ambiguous, but the own-wage effect on an elastic margin favours beta_W > beta_M without any housing mechanism. Most prime-age couples are mortgagor owner-occupiers: hedged on rent flows under Leontief housing (beta = 0), or net long relative to necessity housing (beta < 0).

**What the model implies.** beta_W has the sign of the prime-age household's net position relative to necessity housing: positive only where renting couples are a large share. Primary: beta_W increasing in the prime-age renter share (interaction > 0), with beta_W near zero or negative where owner-occupation dominates. beta_W > beta_M is a composition statement that holds only where men's prime-age participation is near saturation. It is exploratory, not a model prediction.

**Against standard models.** Not distinctive. A unitary household with income effects (the added-worker effect; the larger income elasticity of married women's labour supply, Blundell-MaCurdy) predicts exactly beta_W > 0 and beta_W > beta_M when housing costs cut household income. Search and life-cycle models agree. The paper's distinctive channel (exit-specific housing, h_e > h_w) is switched off for a second earner housed by the household, so P3 tests only the standard income effect. A discriminating variant would contrast single women (own exit housing) with partnered women: under the reduced-form channel, singles respond more to q holding household income.

**What a contrary result refutes.** beta_W <= beta_M refutes only the auxiliary assumption about the density of distastes at the margin. The model with equal distributions predicts the first earner responds more. beta_W <= 0 where renters dominate would refute the concave income-effect auxiliary of App B, not the paper's reduced-form enclosure mechanism, which P3 does not engage.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- pooled (unitary) household budget
- households net short (renters); mortgagors are hedged against rent movements
- men's participation near saturation (thin-tail distribution of chi1)
- q not driven by labour-demand booms (women's participation is also more cyclical)
- the second earner's wage held fixed, or pass-through controlled

### Predictions the draft omitted

- Floor kink (most discriminating): the young's participation response to q falls with, and can turn negative at, a high rent-to-young-wage ratio or coresidence share (q x coresidence interaction < 0). The standard concave income-effect model predicts the opposite (the response grows as net income after housing goes to zero).
- Rents versus prices: in the static model young renters respond to the flow price (rent/goods CPI), not to capitalised house prices. Interest-rate-driven price rises with flat rents should move older owners (down) but not young renters, whereas the life-cycle down-payment motive says the young respond to prices. Include rent and price indices as separate regressors.
- Pension indexation: under wage pass-through (w/r pinned by technology), a rise in q raises participation of groups whose non-labour income is fixed in goods. Older people's beta should therefore be higher (more positive) where pensions are CPI-indexed than where they are wage-indexed. Testable from country indexation rules and distinctive to the technology-pinned w/r.
- Housing benefit: where out-of-work housing costs are publicly covered (the flat floor), the renter enclosure effect of q vanishes. It turns negative where housing benefit is withdrawn on entering work (effective h_w > h_e). Test q x housing-benefit generosity (OECD TaxBEN), expected interaction < 0.
- Wage-rent pinning: at an active margin w/r = b gamma/(1-a-lambda gamma) is set by technology. Supply-driven rises in q should therefore pass into goods wages, so the real wage in goods co-moves positively with rent/goods across country-years, conditional on productivity.
- Singles versus partnered: the reduced-form exit-housing channel bears on people whose exit needs separate housing. Under it, single prime-age women's participation responds more to q than partnered women's, holding household income; under the pure App B income effect the difference runs only through net position.
- Placebo or sign test for the cycle: q's effect should survive controls for group unemployment or the output gap and long differences. The cyclical-confound reading predicts all betas share the sign of the q-cycle correlation, while the model predicts beta_old <= 0.

## dependency floor

### P2 — follows with conditions

**Derivation.** Minimal extension: a young person picks one of four arrangements, with utility log(c) + theta*[lives independently] - chi*[works]. Work and live independently: log(w - q h) + theta - chi. Work and live with parents: log(w - tau) - chi, where tau is the contribution to the parents. Independent exit: log(s0 - q h) + theta. Exit at the floor: log(s_under). Living with parents beats living independently while working iff log(1 - q h/w) + theta < log(1 - tau/w), so the work-side switch happens at q_WP = w(1 - e^{-theta})/h. The dependency floor beats independent exit iff q > q_enc = (s0 - s_under e^{-theta})/h, which is the paper's eq. (exit) with an independence premium added. Both thresholds are crossed from below as q rises, so the share living with parents rises with q on BOTH margins, among workers and among the exited. A numerical check (scratchpad derive/youth_floor.py, wages heterogeneous on [1, 1.6]) gives a coresidence share rising monotonically from 0 to 1 as q goes from 0.15 to 0.5. The workers' switch depends only on q h/w, so the correct shifter is the rent burden r h/w, i.e. the inverse of the paper's wage-rent ratio, not q = r/p alone. Condition from the paper's own text ('the host's capacity to share inherits the host's rent bill'): parents must be able to supply the room at below its market rent. If parents rent, or would let or downsize the room, hosting costs them q h too, and the family gains nothing from coresidence beyond scale economies. The sign then depends on the parents' net housing position: parents who own outright (net long) get richer when q rises and can host more; renting parents get poorer and can host less.

**What the model implies.** Within country over time (country and year fixed effects), the share of 25-34-year-olds living with parents rises with the rent burden: a rent index relative to an all-age wage or to the goods CPI (the paper's q = r/p). Do not use the youth wage as the denominator. The rise is larger where the parent generation mostly owns outright (measured before the sample), and may be zero or negative where the parent generation mostly rents. Youth unemployment is not controlled in the primary specification, because the model says q moves it (it is a mediator). A version controlling for it is a secondary robustness check.

**Against standard models.** Not distinctive. Standard household-formation and life-cycle models (Ermisch-type models of the independence-versus-housing-cost choice; Kaplan 2012 on moving back home as insurance) predict the same positive slope of coresidence on rent relative to income. The draft correctly does not claim distinctiveness. The only part with any edge is the interaction with the parents' tenure (host's budget), and a unitary family model predicts that too. Discriminating power is low; this is a consistency check.

**What a contrary result refutes.** A null or negative slope, with q measured as rent/goods-CPI or rent/all-age wage, refutes the auxiliary claim that the dependency floor in modern economies works through the parental home (the paper's 'often a parent's spare room'). It does not refute the wage or replacement-cost mechanism. A negative slope confined to places where parents rent is CONSISTENT with the paper (the host's rent bill), so the tenure split must be registered in advance, or it becomes an excuse after the fact.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- Parents' spare room has an opportunity cost below market rent (illiquid, not let, not downsized)
- Parents' hosting capacity does not fall with q faster than the young's gain from coresidence rises (true for outright-owner parents, possibly false for renter parents)
- Coresidence and work are compatible (jobs within reach of the parental home); otherwise q moves participation instead of coresidence
- The draft's 'q (rent relative to the young's wage)' conflates r/p with r/w_young; a youth-wage denominator makes q move with youth labour demand (a demand shock lowers w_young, raises measured q and raises coresidence via lost income, so the correlation appears without the rent channel)
- Reverse feedback: higher coresidence lowers housing demand and so lowers r, biasing the slope toward zero

### P4 — not implied

**Derivation.** The paper's model has no search state. Participation is binary: work iff w >= s(q), or iff chi <= log(1+v/A) in App. B. By construction every non-employed person is in 'exit', so the model says nothing about how non-employment splits into unemployment versus inactivity. Signing P4 requires adding search, and then the sign depends on which search variant is chosen, not on anything in the paper. (A) Endogenous search intensity or participation (Pissarides ch. 5/7 with search effort; Garibaldi-Wasmer 2005): the parental floor raises the flow value of non-employment whether or not one searches. That lowers the gain V_E - V_N, fewer people search, and the inactive share of the non-employed RISES, the same sign as P4. (B) Fixed search with a reservation-wage margin: the floor raises the reservation wage, lengthens unemployment spells and swells the unemployment stock, so the inactive share FALLS. The scratchpad check (derive/search_comp.py) gives an inactive share of non-employed of 0.81, 0.89, 0.94 under (A) and 0.77, 0.73, 0.67 under (B), as the floor value b rises from 0.3 to 0.5 to 0.7. So the draft's statement that 'a search reading predicts the opposite sign' is true only of variant (B). The standard modern search model with a participation margin predicts the SAME sign. The aggregate test also cannot discriminate, for three reasons. (1) Reverse causality and composition: aggregate coresidence = sum over statuses of status share times coresidence rate, and inactive youth (lower income, longer spells) coreside more than unemployed youth, so a higher inactive share mechanically raises coresidence in every model. (2) Institutional confounding: a state floor conditional on job search (m_e conditional on activation, App. conditionality) both pays for independent living (lower coresidence) and makes the non-employed report search (higher measured unemployment). A family floor carries no search condition. Floor provider and conditionality are bundled, so a cross-country correlation picks up welfare regime, not q. (3) The model's own content about the floor is about q: at the floor, s is flat in q, and q taxes only the independent work option. That is the place to look.

**What the model implies.** Replace P4 with a q-shifter test of the floor asymmetry ('host pays in exit, self pays in work'). Run country-year data with country and year fixed effects, on 25-34 (or 20-29). Outcome: the employment rate, and separately the share of the population that is inactive and not in education. Shifter: log(rent CPI / goods CPI) (the paper's q), or rent over the all-age average wage, lagged one year. Interact it with a measure of floor availability F_c fixed BEFORE the sample: the pre-sample coresidence share of 25-34, or the outright-ownership share among 50-64. Prediction in the floor regime (q above q_enc, i.e. youth independent exit already unaffordable): d(employment)/dq is lower where F_c is high, beta(q x F) < 0. Where F_c is high the slope is <= 0, strictly negative only if a material share of jobs cannot be reached from the parental home. Where F_c is low, the slope is > 0 (enclosure of a priced independent exit). The coresidence response to q is larger where F_c is high. The model is refuted if the employment slope at high F_c is significantly positive and no smaller than at low F_c.

**Against standard models.** As drafted: not distinctive, and the stated contrast is wrong. Standard search with a participation margin predicts the same positive inactive-share/coresidence correlation, and composition and reverse causality produce it in any model. The revised q-shifter test discriminates against the unitary/life-cycle model with symmetric housing costs, which predicts a non-negative income-effect slope (rent makes the young work more, attenuated by family insurance but never reversed). Note that the model does not predict a monotone q x F interaction everywhere. Below q_enc, a high F_c makes the slope MORE positive, because living with parents absorbs the rent hit on the work side while exit is still priced. Scratchpad derive/youth_floor2.py: d(participation)/dq at q = 0.2, 0.35 is 0.58, 0.85 for F = 0.2 and 0.81, 1.18 for F = 0.8; past q_enc (q = 0.55, 0.7) it is 0.77, 0.71 versus -0.34, -0.28. The test therefore requires stating in advance that the sample is in the floor regime. The revised test does NOT discriminate against search or spatial models where taking a job means moving out (Kaplan-type models with coresidence insurance). These share the asymmetry, and the paper's flat floor is that asymmetry, not something unique to it.

**What a contrary result refutes.** For the draft P4: a negative correlation refutes nothing in the paper (it could be search variant B or welfare-regime confounding), and a positive one confirms nothing (composition, search variant A). For the revised test: a significantly positive employment-q slope at high floor availability, as large as at low availability, refutes the paper's mechanism that the floor makes s flat in q (eq. exit's max with s_under) as the operative margin for the young. It does so given that measurement is clean. A null at high F_c is consistent with the model if work and coresidence are compatible, so only a positive slope refutes. A failure of the low-F_c positive slope refutes the enclosure channel (Prop. exit (iii)) for the young.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- The paper's non-employment is 'exit'; mapping exit to the LFS 'inactive, not in education' requires that the inactive do not want a job at the going wage. The LFS 'wants work but not seeking' (discouraged or potential labour force) category is search failure in either model and should be excluded or reported separately
- The floor regime holds: q > q_enc for most non-employed youth (independent exit unaffordable without income)
- State youth out-of-work support is not rent-indexed (a housing allowance makes s flat in q independently of the family)
- Some jobs are not reachable from the parental home (location), needed for a strictly negative rather than zero slope at the floor
- Q varies within country for reasons other than youth labour demand. Booms raise rents and youth employment together, biasing the slope upward (against the model, so a negative estimate is conservative). A youth-wage denominator biases it downward, toward the model, and must be avoided

### P8 — follows with conditions

**Derivation.** The paper has no schooling choice. The eras section says 'the baseline model does not separately solve for the two wage schedules or for investment in training', and the skill-premium paragraph only says training costs discipline the premium. Minimal extension: a fifth option, school, with value log(m_s + family transfer - q h_s * [student lives independently]) + beta*Delta, where Delta is the expected gain from moving to a trained worker's schedule (nearer the wall). (1) The entrant's wage w falls (an entrant task automated, or a slack youth market): the value of work falls, while school and exit values are unchanged, so enrolment rises. This is the standard opportunity-cost channel, and the sign follows. (2) q: school is an exit with a housing requirement. If students live independently at market rent and grants are fixed cash, a rise in q lowers the value of school one-for-one, exactly as eq. (exit) lowers s, so enrolment FALLS with q (enclosure of the school exit). Where students live with parents or in subsidised housing, there is no effect. (3) Delta: if AI automates entrant tasks while trained tasks stay at the wall (scarcity price), Delta rises and enrolment rises. If AI makes trained tasks contestable (the paper's AI configuration), Delta's sign is ambiguous, so the model does not sign the AI-era enrolment response through the premium. (4) Paid exit: a grant conditional on enrolment acts like m_e in App. conditionality, restricted to school, so a more generous grant raises enrolment. The draft's measure is flawed: the youth unemployment rate is U/(E+U) among youth in the labour force. A rise in enrolment for unrelated reasons (reform, cohort size) changes the composition of who remains in the labour force and moves that rate, so cause and measure are entangled.

**What the model implies.** Within country over time, the 20-24 enrolment rate rises when entrant labour-market prospects fall. Prospects are measured by prime-age (25-54) unemployment, or the 20-24 employment-to-population ratio of the previous year, not the contemporaneous youth unemployment rate. Model-specific addition, registered separately: enrolment falls with q (rent/goods CPI) where students mostly live independently at market rent, and is insensitive to q where students mostly live with parents. The interaction is signed: beta(q x independent-student share) < 0.

**Against standard models.** The opportunity-cost prediction is not distinctive: it is countercyclical enrolment from human-capital theory (Betts and McFarland 1995; Dellas and Sakellaris 2003), and the draft rightly labels it a consistency check. The q-interaction is weakly distinctive. A credit-constrained human-capital model also predicts that higher living costs lower enrolment, but it has no reason to tie this to the student housing arrangement in the same way as to the non-employed floor (P2/P4). Jointly with the revised P4, it tests whether the same flat-floor structure governs both forms of exit.

**What a contrary result refutes.** A null or negative response of enrolment to prime-age unemployment refutes only the imported opportunity-cost auxiliary, not the paper's mechanism, which does not model schooling. A positive or null response of enrolment to q where students live independently refutes the extension's claim that school is an exit priced by the non-produced input, i.e. that eq. (exit) applies to education as it does to non-employment.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- School's housing need is met at market rent when students live independently; grants are not rent-indexed
- Prime-age unemployment proxies entrant prospects (the AI-era entrant-specific shock in P5 would break this proxy, since it hits 20-24 but not 25-54)
- The premium Delta is not moving in the opposite direction over the same period; AI-era contestability of trained tasks makes this uncertain
- Enrolment is measured consistently across countries (ISCED tertiary plus upper-secondary completion; apprenticeships counted as employment in some LFS and as education in others)

### Predictions the draft omitted

- CONFLICT WITH P1: for young people whose exit is the parental floor (q > q_enc), the model gives d(participation)/dq <= 0, not > 0. The exit value is flat at s_under, and q only taxes working while living independently: d/dq[log(w - qh)] = -h/(w - qh) < 0. It is zero if working while living with parents is costless. The enclosure sign (+) holds only where independent exit is still priced (App. B log case: -h/(w - qh) + h/(s0 - qh) > 0 since s0 < w). So P1's beta_young > 0 for 25-34 is signed only for low-coresidence, low-floor settings. In high-coresidence countries the model predicts the youth slope shrinks to zero or turns negative. P1 should be registered conditionally on the youth floor regime, or it is a test the model can lose for reasons the model itself predicts.
- Kink test: the youth participation response to q should switch from positive to <= 0 as q crosses q_enc = (s0 - s_under)/h_e. Country-year version: with country and year fixed effects, the q-slope of 25-34 employment is decreasing in the lagged coresidence share, and is <= 0 above a pre-specified coresidence threshold. Below q_enc, a high floor availability makes the slope MORE positive, because coresidence absorbs the rent hit on the work side. A linear q x F interaction is therefore signed only once the sample is known to be in the floor regime.
- Independent non-employment is priced out: the share of non-employed 25-34 living independently (not with parents) falls with q. Where youth out-of-work support includes a rent-indexed housing allowance (state carries q), the fall is muted. This is a direct test of the max(.) in eq. (exit), albeit not distinctive against budget-constraint models.
- The host's budget carries q: the coresidence response to q is larger, and the youth participation response more negative, where the parent generation owns outright (net long, richer when q rises), than where it rents (host's rent bill rises). Use pre-sample outright-ownership among 50-64 as the moderator; it is predetermined, unlike coresidence.
- Shifter discipline across P2/P4/P8: use q = rent CPI / goods CPI (or rent / all-age wage), lagged, never rent / youth wage. A youth-wage denominator correlates mechanically with youth labour demand and manufactures the predicted signs. Drop 'holding youth unemployment' as a primary control in P2: it is a mediator in the model.
- Conditionality composition (App. conditionality): where the out-of-work floor is state-provided and conditional on job search, youth non-employment should show up as measured unemployment. Where it is family-provided and unconditional, it shows up as inactivity. This is the honest reading of the P4 cross-country correlation: a consistency check on Delta = m_w - m_e, not evidence for exit-versus-search.
- Household formation (Future Work): the rate of first independent household formation and first births among 25-34 fall with the rent burden. This is not distinctive (standard housing-cost models predict it) but is in the paper's stated agenda and testable with country-year data.

## schedule aging pensions

### P5 — follows with conditions

**Derivation.** Minimal extension: two worker types, an entrant E and a trained worker T, each with its own schedule gamma_g(x), as in the eras figure. The paper says this figure is interpretive and that the baseline does not solve the two schedules. In the post-industrial configuration, E's margin is interior on the contestable stretch: w_E = c*gamma_E(x*_E). T's margin is at the wall: w_T is a scarcity price, so w_T < c*gamma_T(x) on every task T holds that has just become contestable. Model AI as a fall in gamma near both margins (gamma_M rises). For E, Prop. margin(i)-(ii) gives an immediate first-order effect: at fixed c, dlog w_E = dlog gamma_E(x*_E) < 0, and x*_E moves right. With lambda > 0, c also falls (the closure), which deepens the cut. For T, nothing moves until the falling c*gamma_T(x) on the newly contestable tasks crosses the scarcity wage w_T. That gap is a buffer, so T's wage and employment respond with a delay or not at all. 'Entrants first' therefore follows. But in this competitive static model, an employment fall for E needs a participation response: w_E falls toward s_E (the parental dependency floor, or schooling as exit, P8). The model has no unemployment and no hiring margin, so its first-order prediction concerns the entry WAGE (w_E/w_T falls). The fall in employment is second-order and goes through inactivity and enrolment. The right exposure measure is the entrant's own margin: the AI exposure of 20-24-year-olds' jobs minus that of 25-54 (or 35-54), each computed from the age-specific pre-2023 occupation mix (ISCO 3-digit with an AIOE, GPT or ILO-genAI index). A country's overall clerical share is not that measure, because it raises exposure for both groups. Window: annual data for 2023-2025 give 2-3 post years. Firms' adoption was low in that period. The 2022-23 rate shock hit youth hiring (youth employment is 2-3 times as cyclical). Services-heavy, high-clerical countries differ in cyclical exposure, and post-COVID normalisation was still running. With about 30 countries the design is badly underpowered, and a null is uninformative.

**What the model implies.** Conditional on AI having lowered machine-relative capability on the tasks entrants hold (the auxiliary claim), and holding the cycle fixed (interact youth cyclicality with the output gap; show an event study with 2015-2019 pre-trends): from 2023 on, the entry wage and the employment rate of 20-24 relative to 25-54 fall more where the pre-2023 exposure GAP (age 20-24 minus age 25-54, from age-specific occupation mixes) was larger. The employment fall shows up as inactivity or enrolment more than as unemployment where coresidence is available. Trained (high-schooling, 35-54) workers in exposed occupations show no relative fall in the window. Register this as descriptive or low-power, with a minimum detectable effect stated in advance. A null is not evidence against the model.

**Against standard models.** Not distinctive in sign. Standard models predict entrants first for other reasons: adjustment on the hiring margin (firms stop hiring rather than fire), employment protection and insider-outsider wage setting, seniority rules, and firm-specific human capital. The canaries-style evidence for the US (young workers in AI-exposed occupations) already reads the pattern this way. The model's only weakly distinctive pieces: (a) the wall buffer, under which trained workers in the same exposed occupations are untouched until a threshold and then flip at once, a nonlinearity rather than a proportional effect; (b) the youth margin running into inactivity, schooling or coresidence rather than unemployment. Piece (b) partly reflects the static model having no unemployment rather than a real prediction. Sign risk: if AI mainly raises novices' gamma_L (evidence that AI tools help novices most) or replaces TRAINING (making entrants able to do trained tasks), the model predicts the trained worker's scarcity premium is hit first, and entrants could gain. So the sign depends on whether AI acts as gamma_M (a substitute) or as a substitute for training.

**What a contrary result refutes.** A contrary result (no relative youth decline, or the experienced hit first) refutes only the interpretive placement in the eras section: that AI's first gains land on the entrant's contestable stretch and that trained workers sit behind a wall. It does not refute the closure, Prop. margin, or the exit-pricing mechanism. A null in 2023-2025 refutes nothing, given lags and power.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- AI acts as gamma_M rising (machine substitute) on the entrant's tasks, not as a novice-biased gamma_L rise or a substitute for training
- The trained worker's wage is a scarcity price strictly below the post-AI replacement value (the wall buffer)
- The youth employment fall goes through participation, since the static model has no hiring or unemployment margin
- The 2023-2025 youth cycle (rate shock, post-COVID normalisation) is uncorrelated with the exposure gap after controls
- AI capability had diffused into production by 2023-2025

### P6 — follows with conditions

**Derivation.** Model aging as a rise in the retired share rho: people who hold land, occupy h and consume goods but never work. In Appendix B, n_S = N(1-rho)F(log(1+v/A)), while n_D(x) = (S/b)[lambda + (1-a)(1-x)/J(x)] does not depend on rho. Equilibrium n_D(x*) = n_S(v(x*)) gives dx*/drho = (dn_S/drho)/(n_D' - n_S'v') > 0, because dn_S/drho < 0, n_D' < 0 and n_S'v' >= 0. Machines take the lowest-gamma contestable tasks (the routine stretch, if that is where the margin sits), and w = c*gamma(x*) rises. With lambda > 0, c also rises: dc/dgamma = lambda*b*r/(1-a-lambda*gamma)^2, which dampens the shift. Role of slope, at fixed output (Prop. margin(ii)): dN_F/dx* = -Y/gamma_L does not depend on gamma'. With inelastic participation, dx* is the same whatever the slope and dlog w is proportional to gamma'(x*). With elastic participation, the induced wage rise draws remaining workers in, which offsets part of the shift. That offset is larger where the schedule is steep. So FLAT means a large move in x* (much automation) and a small wage rise; STEEP means a small move in x* and a large wage rise. A numerical check in the Appendix B example (rho 0 to 0.1) confirms the signs: x* and v both rise, and dlog v is about 0.001 for a flat schedule versus 0.006 for a steep one. Other aging channels push the same way or blur the test. (1) Demand for care (H) rises, pulling labour off the contestable stretch: the routine share falls without any automation. (2) Older owners are net long in housing and occupy more space per head, so q rises. That encloses the young (higher youth participation), partly offsetting the supply shift.

**What the model implies.** In the model, a fall in labour supply for a given machine technology raises x* (machines take more contestable tasks) and raises the wage at the margin. The split between the two depends on the local slope: where the schedule is flat the response is mostly automation (a fall in routine employment share), where it is steep mostly wages. Testable with country-year data: (a) the sign: aging (the ratio of 56+ to 21-55, or the change in the old-age dependency ratio) goes with a faster fall in the routine employment share AND a non-falling relative wage of routine-task workers; (b) the mediation claim: conditional on the wage-to-machine-price ratio (w/c, e.g. manufacturing hourly compensation over an investment-goods or robot price index), current aging has no further effect on automation.

**Against standard models.** The sign is NOT distinctive. It is Acemoglu-Restrepo's ('Demographics and Automation', 2022), already tested across countries with robots and automation-technology imports and found positive. A pre-registered P6 in this form largely replicates a published result. Where the accounts differ: (1) AR have directed innovation and forward-looking investment, so ANTICIPATED aging raises automation. The paper's model is static and moves along a given, globally available schedule, so only CURRENT scarcity, priced through w/c, matters. The model does not forbid an anticipation effect (durability is set aside), so a positive anticipation effect is consistent with the model's silence rather than contrary to it. Test (b) is the closest thing to a discriminating test, and it is weak, because w is endogenous. (2) Against a demand-composition (Baumol or aging-demand) account, in which the routine share falls because demand moves to care: that account predicts routine relative wages FALL with aging, while the margin account predicts they rise. Testing the joint sign of employment share and relative wage separates them. (3) The slope interaction is distinctive but not testable with aggregates, because no country-year measure of gamma'(x*) exists.

**What a contrary result refutes.** Aging going with a slower fall (or a rise) in the routine share, together with rising routine relative wages, contradicts the supply-shift comparative static of Prop. margin(ii). That mechanism is shared with every task model, so the result would not discriminate. A falling routine share with falling routine relative wages refutes only the reading that the margin drives the share (demand composition dominates), not the model. Measurement: routine occupational shares (ISCO 4/7/8/9) mix task location with industry composition, and the routine stretch is assumed to be where x* sits.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- Aging is a labour-supply shift (retirees consume and hold land but do not work), not a fall in N
- The margin x* sits on the routine stretch, so the newly automated tasks are routine
- The machine price c is common across countries (traded machines), so cross-country variation runs through w
- Care-demand (H) and housing (q) channels are controlled for, or acknowledged as reinforcing or offsetting
- The static model: anticipation effects are outside the model, not ruled out by it

### P7 — follows with conditions

**Derivation.** Conditionality appendix: the worker compares w + m_w with s + m_e, so the compensated participation margin depends only on Delta = m_w - m_e. An exit-conditioned pension (earnings test, or the requirement to stop working to claim) has m_e > 0 = m_w, so Delta < 0: the reservation wage rises and participation falls. A pension that can be drawn while working (no earnings test) has Delta = 0: no compensated effect, only an income effect. In the Appendix B log form, work iff chi <= log((A + m + v)/(A + m)), which falls in m, so participation still falls, and the effect per unit is larger where A is small (renters, the net short). An earlier statutory age makes m_e available at 60-64, so it lowers participation through both terms. If a delayed claim raises later benefits actuarially fairly, the present-value Delta is about zero and only the income effect remains. So the replacement-rate LEVEL is not the model's object. The object is Delta, the implicit tax on continued work (net accrual), plus pension wealth through the income effect.

**What the model implies.** Participation at ages 60-64 falls in (i) the implicit tax on continued work at those ages: the exit-conditioned benefit net of accrual, or the earnings-test strictness interacted with replacement; and (ii) pension wealth (the income effect). Per unit of replacement, the effect is larger where the benefit is exit-conditioned (earnings test, or early-retirement or disability routes that require leaving work) than where it can be combined with work. Optional, depending on functional form: the effect is larger where non-labour income after housing is lower. Treat as a consistency check.

**Against standard models.** Not distinctive. This is Gruber-Wise ('Social Security and Retirement Around the World'): participation of older workers falls with the implicit tax on continued work, and the life-cycle model predicts the same sign through wealth and incentive effects. Delta is the model's restatement of that implicit tax. The q x replacement interaction (bigger effects for the net short) rests only on the log form and the density of chi, so it is not a robust model implication.

**What a contrary result refutes.** A contrary sign refutes participation step (iii) itself, that people compare w + m_w with s + m_e. Every labour-supply model shares that step, so a contrary result would almost surely reflect measurement: OECD theoretical net replacement rates describe a full-career worker at the NORMAL age, not the out-of-work payment available at 60-64 (early-retirement, disability and unemployment-to-retirement routes), and they ignore earnings tests and accrual. A null with the replacement-rate level refutes only that proxy.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- The measured replacement rate is exit-conditioned (Delta < 0) or large enough for the income effect to show
- No offsetting effect from a later statutory age raising expected lifetime work
- Health and the cohort mix at 60-64 are held fixed

### S1 (sex over the eras) — not implied

**Derivation.** The model has no sex. It assigns effects by TASK LOCATION (contestable stretch, margin, wall or H) and by EXIT VALUE s_g. A sex prediction therefore exists only after mapping each sex's pre-period task mix onto the schedule. Eras: industrial (physical tasks contestable) compresses the premium on physical tasks, which is Galor-Weil's brawn-erosion story. But early mill entrants were often women and children, so the model does not say men lost first. Computing (clerical contestable): women's clerical employment was hit, yet women's participation kept rising as they moved into professional work and care. AI: clerical, admin and customer service (women-heavy) are exposed, while care is H (women-heavy) and Baumol concentration with sigma_H < 1 raises H's expenditure share. Physical trades (men-heavy) are less exposed to generative AI. The net sign for women is therefore exposure (negative) plus care demand (positive), which is ambiguous without the two shares. The enclosure channel (P3: q up raises the second earner's participation) is separate and model-specific.

**What the model implies.** The precise country-year form: 2023-2025 change in (women's minus men's employment rate, 25-54) = a*(women's minus men's pre-2023 AI-exposure share, from sex-specific occupation mixes) + b*(women's minus men's care (H) employment share) x (growth in demand for care, e.g. aging) + controls, with a < 0 and b > 0. It shares P5's power and lag problems. For the long run, the model says only that a group's participation falls where its task stretch is reached and its wage nears its exit value. Sex is not a model primitive.

**Against standard models.** Not distinctive. Standard task models (Acemoglu-Restrepo on tasks and inequality; Autor-Dorn) predict exposure-weighted losses by group, and Galor-Weil and Goldin's U-shape explain the long-run rise in women's participation. The only model-specific piece is the q x sex interaction already in P3.

**What a contrary result refutes.** A contrary result refutes only the mapping from sex to task location (measurement), not the model.

**Hidden assumptions.**
- Sex-specific occupation mixes measure location on the schedule
- Care is H (human-required) in practice, not just by assumption
- Women's and men's exit values move together over the window

### Predictions the draft omitted

- P6 discriminating version: aging should come with a NON-falling (rising) relative wage for routine-task workers alongside the falling routine employment share. A demand-composition (Baumol or aging-demand) account predicts the routine relative wage falls. The joint sign separates the margin account from demand shifts; the employment share alone does not.
- P6 mediation (weak but the only test that separates the model from AR): the static model says aging affects automation only through current w/c. Conditional on the wage-to-machine-price ratio, current aging has no further effect, and anticipated aging needs durability or expectations the paper sets aside. Register any anticipation effect as 'not contradicted', not as support.
- P5 primary outcome should be the ENTRY WAGE relative to the experienced wage. In the competitive model a fall in gamma at the entrant's margin hits w_E at first order, while employment responds only through participation (inactivity, enrolment, coresidence). That gives a P5/P4 link: where coresidence is available, the post-2023 youth employment fall shows up as inactivity or enrolment more than as unemployment.
- P5 wall nonlinearity: trained workers in the SAME exposed occupations show no relative change until c*gamma_T falls below their scarcity wage, then a discrete flip. Over 2023-2025 that predicts a null for 35-54 high-schooling workers in exposed occupations, which is checkable and slightly distinctive against proportional-exposure models.
- P5 sign risk to pre-register: if AI raises novices' gamma_L or substitutes for training, the model predicts the trained worker's scarcity premium (skill premium within exposed occupations) falls first. State which reading the test assumes.
- P7 sharpened: compensated effect only through Delta = m_w - m_e. Exit-conditioned benefits (earnings tests, early-retirement or disability routes that require leaving work) should lower 60-64 participation more per unit than benefits that can be combined with work. In-work benefits (EITC-type, Delta > 0) should raise low-wage participation and, in the sloped regime, lower the pre-transfer wage (partial incidence).
- Aging x housing: older owners are net long and occupy more space per head, so aging raises q, which by P1/P3 raises young and second-earner participation and partly offsets the aging supply shift. A country-year cross-check: in aging countries, rent-to-young-wage and coresidence rise faster.
- Power and window: the 2023-2025 tests (P5, S1) should state a minimum detectable effect in advance and use quarterly LFS data if possible. A null is labelled 'uninformative about the mechanism', not 'against'.
