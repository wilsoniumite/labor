Pinning the Wage to Scarcity and Technology

Replacement, exit, and the rents of non-produced inputs in a task economy
Stella Wilson
KTH Royal Institute of Technology · Skandinaviska Enskilda Banken (SEB) · thmwi@kth.se
Working draft — August 2026. Views are the author's own and do not represent those of KTH or SEB. Written in collaboration with Claude (Anthropic); see the AI-use note at the end.
Abstract. In a task model the wage is priced against its machine substitute, which is the cost of the machine adjusted by the difference in productivity at equilibrium, w = c·γ(x*). We price that substitute on its own production: free entry gives that it depends on replacement costs, human labor costs and cost of raw materials. c = ac + λw + br. Thus as machine production sheds labor, replacement cost resolves into rents on non-produced inputs — land, sites, energy. Margin and recursion together pin the wage to scarcity and technology: w = γ(x*)·br/(1 − a − λγ(x*)). Machine production takes time as well as inputs: build lags mark up replacement cost and decide who holds the value automation releases while capacity catches up. We price market exit from the same inputs: leaving the labor market still requires housing, space, and energy, so the same rents lower the outside option. At uniform machine capability, real wages diverge by what they are measured in: in machine-made goods the wage is pinned by absolute human productivity; against housing, space, and energy it falls without bound. In the U.S. since 1964 these two measures now diverge with a factor of 4.8: the same average paycheck buys nearly four times the durables or a fifth less shelter. From the model we derive a taxation and benefit system: a tax on the rents of non-produced inputs funding a uniform per-person transfer. It distorts no production decision and changes no work–exit choice, up to an income effect. We find that taxed in full, U.S. site rents would fund one-third of a per-person subsistence floor today, up from a twentieth in the 1950s.

1. Introduction

Suppose a firm can perform a task with a worker or with a machine. For the tasks where the firm is indifferent, the worker's wage is tied to the cost of the machine substitute. That mechanism is established and used by the factor-price condition at the task margin in Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018), with the same threshold logic in Zeira (1998) and, for tasks that move abroad rather than to machines, in Grossman and Rossi-Hansberg (2008). This paper examines that mechanism with two questions around that margin.

What prices the substitute? A machine is itself produced — from other machines, labor, energy, sites, minerals, permissions. Its rental is an equilibrium object. Follow that price backward through production and it terminates in inputs whose supply does not expand with production itself: land is the permanent example; a grid connection, a mineral right, or a fabrication bottleneck can hold the same position over shorter horizons. While machine production still employs labor, part of the machine's cost will be the wage itself, and thus the substitute will remain relatively expensive. As machine production sheds labor, that cost component is reduced, and the replacement price resolves increasingly into terminal rents.

What does time add? Machines — capital — take time to be produced. Capacity is paid for before it produces output, a fabrication plant runs years from ground-breaking to first wafer, a power interconnection longer. And, whilst the capability to use capacity can jump overnight, the capacity itself cannot. Waiting is priced at the interest rate; building is a lag, one number per input. An input is terminal over any horizon shorter than its build lag, and land is classically the input with infinite lag.

What prices the alternative to working at all? A worker sells an hour when the wage beats the best alternative use of that hour. Life outside the market wage still requires somewhere to live, food, energy, warmth, and usually access to a household. The value of exit is therefore also a price — and it depends on the same scarce inputs that anchor the machine's cost. When machine-made goods cheapen while housing and energy do not, the exit option deteriorates in exactly the units that matter to the person weighing it.

This paper closes both prices around the labor margin:

wage supply → machine substitute → the machine's inputs → rents on non-produced inputs

on the labor demand side, and

wage demand → exit → the inputs of unwaged life → rents on non-produced inputs

on the labor supply side. The wage sits in an interval, with replacement cost, the opportunity cost for firms paying wages, above and exit value, the opportunity cost for workers performing tasks, below, and both endpoints of these opportunity costs are formed partly in the market for non-produced inputs. Technological change moves both at once. It changes what workers can do relative to machines at final tasks and it changes how much labor the machine's own production employs. Crucially, it also changes the relative price of the scarce inputs that both chains terminate in.

We can illustrate this dynamic with the extreme scenario. Suppose machine capability becomes uniform across tasks. Thus machines are at rough parity with humans everywhere, and there exists no task where the human competitive advantage is large. In that limit every standing account of the wage becomes degenerate: there is no marginal-product schedule to climb, no bargaining surplus to split, no scarcity of labor for population to price. What remains is a simple set of constraints: the wage in machine-made goods is pinned by absolute human productivity and cannot fall (a point proved by Caselli and Manning (2019)), while the wage against housing, space, and energy falls to near zero as the capability gap closes, because that is where the value released by automation settles. The real wage forks by the composition of goods and labor used in production, a process which is visible in U.S. measurements in Section 10.

Applying the task margin model of Acemoglu and Restrepo and adding a price recursion mechanism based on the input-output logic of Leontief (1936) and Sraffa (1960) we are able to derive a closure of the wage model. We price the machine rental from its own production recursion and substitute it into the task margin, so the wage is expressed in terminal rents and the transition rides on a measurable parameter, the labor content of machine production. We price the outside option in the same relative price, so both endpoints of the bargaining interval move on one variable. From those two closures we derive the fork and its fiscal completion, a re-derivation of George (1879): a rent tax funding a uniform transfer, the pair that continues to function in the limit.

The paper is one economy read at two horizons. The environment is dynamic throughout — capacity is a stock, built to a recipe, arriving J periods after it is paid for — and the main text reads it at rest, in coefficient notation, where every claim is checked algebra. The model in motion is developed once, in Appendix E: the transition after a capability shock, its windfalls and their decay, each result carrying its label, proposition or numerically verified, and a machine-checked equivalence (Proposition E.1) tying the two readings so the text at rest never leans on the transition. The floor, the fork, and the fiscal sections cite E's results where they bite.

Section 2 surveys how current theory tries to pin the wage. Sections 3–6 build the model at rest: the margin, the recursion with its build recipe, the user cost of build time, the floor. Section 7 states the fork as a ratio of coefficients. Section 8 reads the long record as three configurations of the schedule. Section 9 states the fiscal completion, its horizon theorem, and the stabilizers as quantity protections. Section 10 measures. Sections 11–12 draw the implications for artificial intelligence and conclude. Everything supporting but not essential to the model — the model in motion, the closed-economy identity, the fiscal system away from the limit, human-essential tasks, the numerical methods, the notation table — is in the appendix.

2. What standing accounts pin the wage to

Accounts of the aggregate wage differ less in their mechanisms than in where they stop. Each family below determines the wage relative to a quantity that labor itself helps produce, or leaves it inside an interval whose endpoints it takes as given, or determines its growth rate while not fixing its level. We do not offer the model of Sections 3–6 against them; we offer instead the objects at which they terminate.

2.1 Marginal product

The textbook answer sets the wage equal to the marginal product of labor. As a statement about competitive equilibrium it is correct, and this paper's Proposition 1 is an instance of it: w = c·γ(x*) (using our notation, see Section 3) is the marginal product, evaluated at the margin the machine alternative sets. What the identity does not supply is a level: the marginal product is a function of the capital stock and the technology, two variables constantly changing. Applied work usually proceeds under Cobb–Douglas, which requires fixed factor shares and an aggregate capital measure. This makes it fragile when considering times of rapid changes to the factor shares. Likewise the aggregate capital measure becomes uncertain when the price of capital is the underlying factor analysed (Sraffa 1960; Samuelson 1966). In growth theory the wage's growth rate is pinned along a balanced path, but Uzawa (1961) shows the balanced path requires technical change to be labor-augmenting in form — and the form of technical change is precisely what is at issue under automation.

2.2 Search, matching, and bargaining

The search-and-matching framework (Diamond 1982; Mortensen and Pissarides 1994; Pissarides 2000) places the wage between the worker's outside option and the value of the match, dividing the surplus by a bargaining parameter. The framework is explicit that the level is not determined within it: two endpoints and a split, and it supplies none of the three.

Shimer (2005) showed that the calibrated model generates almost none of the observed volatility — in U.S. data the vacancy–unemployment ratio is roughly twenty times as volatile as labor productivity, while the model makes them comparable. Hagedorn and Manovskii (2008) resolve the puzzle by setting the value of non-market activity close to productivity, so that the surplus workers and firms split is small; Hall (2005) resolves it with rigid wages; and Ljungqvist and Sargent (2017) show that these and the other proposed resolutions operate through one channel, the fundamental surplus — "an upper bound on the fraction of a job's output that the invisible hand can allocate to vacancy creation" — which every successful calibration makes small, and whose leading deduction from productivity is the value of non-work. The central calibration debate of modern macro-labor is, in other words, a debate about the level of an object the framework treats as a parameter.

We price both endpoints. Sections 4 and 5 price the upper wall (what the firm's machine alternative costs, including the cost of building it, and why); Section 6 prices the lower wall with both lives' land priced, and shows the exit comparison is q-invariant under symmetric housing — what survives is a rigidity claim and a funding claim. The two walls are formed partly in the same market, so they can move together, and in the automation limit, close.

2.3 Task and assignment models

The task framework (Zeira 1998; Acemoglu and Autor 2011; Acemoglu and Restrepo 2018, 2022) determines the wage at the threshold task and is the direct ancestor of what follows. Its equilibrating force is the creation of new labor tasks: displacement met by reinstatement (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2018; Autor 2015). In each version the machine rental and the outside option enter as parameters; the wage is passed through the threshold rather than determined at it. This paper closes both parameters, and formalizes the new-task margin instead of shutting it: Section 3 states the condition — the support of new tasks' relative productivities collapsing toward γ̄ — under which reinstatement stabilizes the transition and the flat limit still arrives. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2026) add institutional wage wedges and show that automation is directed at the tasks where wedges are largest; Section 9.3 states that result in this notation. Cross border trade is treated similarly: a tradable task stays domestically only while the wage beats both the machine rental and the foreign wage, so the foreign wage is a second machine rental with a different cost composition. Offshoring is however still only a temporary condition: once the machine undercuts the foreign worker, the task flips onward to machines wherever they sit.

2.4 Institutional accounts

Efficiency wages (Shapiro and Stiglitz 1984), monopsony (Manning 2003), and firm-level rent sharing (Card, Cardoso, Heining, and Kline 2018) explain why individual wages depart from a common base and why the dispersion moves. In the notation below they are accounts of a multiplicative wedge on particular tasks. The model takes them as given at that layer and focuses instead on the common base.

2.5 The classical accounts

The wage was pinned to land and to the cost of subsistence before it was pinned to anything else. Ricardo (1817) priced land at the margin and the wage at its natural price, Malthus (1798) supplying the mechanism; the machinery chapter added in 1821 concedes that machinery can injure the laboring class. Marx priced labor power at its cost of reproduction and read enclosure — the legal extinction of common access to land — as the manufacture of a labor supply. Lewis (1954) pinned the modern-sector wage to what the traditional sector affords — the closest antecedent to Section 6, differing in that his traditional sector is exhausted by absorption rather than priced away by rent. Polanyi (1944) recorded the institutional half of the same event. George (1879) drew the fiscal consequence this paper's Section 9 derives inside the model. These accounts were not refuted. The premise they shared — that the human advantage over tools varied little from task to task — stopped holding, and the conclusions lapsed with it. Section 8 reads the classical case as the flat configuration of the schedule built below.

2.6 Automation and the wage

Korinek and Stiglitz (2019) pose the below-subsistence scenario this model resolves through exit; Korinek and Suh (2024) is the closest formal predecessor on wage collapse under transformative AI, differing in where the collapse ends — here at non-produced factors rather than accumulated capital — and in the treatment of the outside option; Appendix E runs their scenario, capability arriving before capacity, inside this model and finds the release-day wage move depends on which margin the capability extends. Susskind (2017) and Sachs and Kotlikoff (2012) reach technological immiseration through other channels; Moll, Rachel, and Restrepo (2022) route automation's gains to wealth-holders; this model names the terminal claimant, and Section 5's interest identity prices their channel inside the recursion — machine wealth earns the world rate on installed capacity and its build pipeline, and the identity fixes that income's ratio to rent in closed form. Aghion, Jones, and Jones (2019) supply the strongest stabilizer — expenditure concentrating on whatever remains human-essential — granted a theorem in Appendix D. Caselli and Manning (2019) prove real wages cannot fall in terms of machine-made goods; Section 7 concedes the theorem and locates the fall elsewhere. Hémous and Olsen (2022) endogenize automation's direction.

2.7 Where the model meets data

Three empirical literatures serve below as instruments. Factor shares: the labor share's decline is documented and global (Karabarbounis and Neiman 2014), and its capital-side counterpart resolves substantially into housing — which is to say land (Rognlie 2015; Knoll, Schularick, and Steger 2017; Piketty and Zucman 2014). The long wage record: real wages measured against the cost of a subsistence basket — welfare ratios in the sense of Allen (2001), extended for England by Clark (2005) — are the same object this paper refers to as the floor, computed at the household level; Section 8 describes that record. Payroll incidence: under Proposition 1 the share of a payroll tax borne by workers maps to the inverse slope of the schedule at the margin, so the quasi-experimental incidence literature (Gruber 1997; Kugler and Kugler 2009; Saez, Schoefer, and Seim 2019) is, in this model, a repeated measurement of the schedule's flattening; Section 10 specifies that assembly — existing estimates compiled onto one pass-through definition — as open work.

3. The model: tasks and the margin

The environment is dynamic throughout — machine capacity is a stock with a build lag, stated in full in Appendix E — but the main text reads it at rest, and Proposition E.1 there licenses the static notation used here as exactly its steady state.

Tasks. Producing one unit of the final good requires a continuum of tasks x ∈ [0,1], each in equal amount (Leontief aggregation over tasks): the good's unit cost is the sum of its tasks' unit costs. One human-hour completes γL(x) units of task x; one machine-hour completes γM(x). Relative human productivity is

γ(x) = γL(x) / γM(x).

A machine-hour rents for c — a parameter here, the subject of Section 4. Institutional wage wedges — union premia, licensing rents, efficiency wages — are set aside until Section 9.3.

People and land. N identical workers each supply one hour or exit to an outside option worth s — the value of the best life available without selling labor. s is a parameter here and the subject of Section 6; it presupposes somewhere to live. Land is fixed in total, heterogeneous in quality, with the worst parcel's reservation rent zero.

Labor, at wage w, holds task x when it is the cheaper input: w/γL(x) ≤ c/γM(x), i.e. w ≤ c·γ(x). Relabel tasks so γ increases, and assume the relabeled schedule is continuous and strictly increasing where labor is employed (flat stretches are handled in Appendix A). Competitive assignment then produces a threshold x*: machines take [0, x*), labor takes [x*, 1] (Figure 1).

Proposition 1 (the task margin). (i) In any equilibrium using both inputs, w = c·γ(x*). (ii) The wage's sensitivity to labor supply is governed by the slope of γ at x*: a steep schedule makes labor demand inelastic and the wage unresponsive; as the schedule flattens toward a constant γ̄, labor demand becomes perfectly elastic at c·γ̄ and the wage is pinned there regardless of how many or few workers exist. (iii) No equilibrium wage sits below s while anyone works: if the clearing wage would fall below s, workers exit until the wage recovers to s (possible only where the schedule has slope) or all have left.

Proof. (i) If w > c·γ(x*), the marginal task and, by continuity, tasks just above it are cheaper by machine; firms drop labor there and the unemployed underbid. If w < c·γ(x*), tasks just below x* strictly favor labor and bidding raises w. Only equality is stable. (ii) Absorbing more hours requires labor to win more tasks, and a firm concedes a task only when the wage falls relative to that task's machine cost; the required fall is proportional to the slope. At a flat schedule no wage above c·γ̄ employs anyone and every wage at c·γ̄ employs everyone willing. (iii) is the participation condition applied to (i) and (ii). Existence and uniqueness of (w, x*) are Lemma A.1. ∎

The condition is an indifference: the worker has no cost advantage at the marginal task; what is being priced is relative productivity. If c = $10 and γ(x*) = 4, the marginal worker can be paid $40 while leaving the firm indifferent. Let machines improve uniformly by 25%, so γ(x*) falls to 3.2: the same worker's ceiling is $32 — a 20% cut delivered entirely by someone else's machine improving at her task, with no human getting worse at anything. That arithmetic holds c fixed while machines improve, and machine progress also cheapens machine-made goods.

The new-task margin. Tasks are also created. Let new tasks arrive with relative productivities drawn from a distribution whose support's upper edge collapses toward γ̄ as capability generalizes, and let labor hold whatever arrives above the margin. Then reinstatement keeps the margin at the support's edge, the wage's land claim exceeds its flat-limit value at every finite date, and the flat limit still arrives — a transitional stabilizer, not a rescue. (Computer-checked; the empirical record of task creation is Autor, Chin, Salomons, and Seegmiller 2024.) The paper's limit statements need the support to collapse, not the margin to be shut; where later sections treat the margin as shut, it is this condition asserted, on our reading of history and the U.S. measures.

4. The ceiling: what prices the machine

Proposition 1 prices labor off the machine; this section prices the machine off its recipe. Suppose one unit of machine services is produced with three kinds of inputs: a units of machine services themselves (a < 1: machines net-reproduce); λ labor-hours — the fabrication, operation, maintenance, and installation the machine sector still buys; and b units of non-produced services — sites, energy, ore — renting at r, the rent of the sites production uses (parcels differ in quality; Appendix B specifies the schedule). As a price equation this is the one-sector version of the input-output cost systems of Leontief (1936) and Sraffa (1960): with free entry and constant returns, the rental equals unit cost,

c = a·c + λ·w + b·r.

The first term is recursively embodied machine content; the second is the wage bill inside the substitute; the third is the claim on the inputs at which the recursion terminates. Call an input terminal, informally, when its supply does not expand through the production process itself on the horizon under study; the propositions say "non-produced." Land is the permanent case. Energy capacity, transmission, minerals, and fabrication bottlenecks can occupy the position for years at a time and then leave it as they are completed or resolved; Section 4's results hold over whatever horizon the classification holds, and our long-run claims use the permanent case.

A machine is not only produced; it is produced slowly, and from a second recipe. Building one unit of capacity takes aI units of machine services, λI labor-hours, and bI units of non-produced services, all paid when the build starts, with the first service arriving J periods later (Section 5). Recipes are named for the activity they feed; the operating recipe, being the default, goes unmarked. The build price is a price, not a rental,

pK = aI·c + λI·w + bI·r,

the same form as the operating condition with different numbers. Free entry cannot equate a one-time price to a per-period flow; what it equates is the build price to the present value of the rentals a unit of capacity will earn. In steady state that condition compresses into a single factor, the user cost with gestation uK = (ρ+δ)(1+ρ)J−1, which Section 5 derives; the rental then covers the operating recipe and the capitalized build recipe together:

c = a·c + λ·w + b·r + uK·pK.

Proposition 2 (replacement closure). Let the task margin hold, w = c·γ(x*), and let machine services be priced by free entry against both recipes, c = ac + λw + br + uKpK with pK = aIc + λIw + bIr. If D := 1 − a − λγ(x*) − uK(aI + λIγ(x*)) > 0, then c = θc·r and w = θw·r with

θc = (b + uK·bI) / D,  θw = γ(x*)·θc.

The wage is a claim on terminal rents, scaled by relative productivity at the margin and amplified by the recursion. On the viable set both prices rise in λ, in λI, and in γ(x*). At zero build recipe the closure is c = br/(1 − a − λγ(x*)).

Proof. Substitute the margin into both recipes: c·[1 − a − λγ(x*) − uK(aI + λIγ(x*))] = (b + uKbI)r. Multiply by γ(x*) for the wage. The comparative statics on the viable set are ∂θc/∂γ(x*) = θc(λ + uKλI)/D, ∂θc/∂λ = γ(x*)θc/D, and ∂θc/∂λI = uKγ(x*)θc/D, all positive; setting aI = λI = bI = 0 returns the static closure. ∎

The viability condition has an intuitive reading: a + λγ(x*) + uK(aI + λIγ(x*)) is the produced content of one unit of machine services once its labor is priced at the margin and its build recipe is carried at the user cost — direct machine content, the machine-equivalent of its labor, and the same two objects inside the build. If that reaches one, the sector cannot deliver services at any finite price. Build time tightens the condition: a costlier wait makes more configurations non-viable.

The formula separates two kinds of automation that are usually discussed as one.

Task automation lowers γ(x*): machines improve at tasks near the assignment margin, so a marginal human hour replaces less machine service. This is the automation of the task literature.

Recursive automation lowers λ, and now also λI: fewer human hours are required to operate and maintain machine services, and fewer to build the capacity behind them. This removes the wage bill from the substitute's own cost base — operating and build recipe alike — and cheapens the substitute directly.

Either change lowers the wage supported by a given terminal rent. And they interact: with λ > 0, task automation also cheapens the machine, because it cheapens the machine sector's own labor; the build recipe carries the same cross-effect on its own, so it survives even at λ = 0 through λI. A worked instance, showing the recursion's amplification: at (a, λ, γ(x*), b, r) = (0.5, 0.1, 3, 0.2, 1), the recipe gives c = 1 and w = 3, and one checks 1 = 0.5·1 + 0.1·3 + 0.2·1. Now let recursive automation run λ from 0.1 to 0, holding everything else fixed: c falls to 0.4 and the wage falls to 1.2 — a 60% cut from removing one tenth of an hour of labor from the machine recipe, because that tenth of an hour, priced at the margin wage, was 30% of the recipe's cost. In the limit λ → 0,

c → b·r/(1 − a),

and the replacement price contains no wage at all: its reproducible components are internal to the recursion, and what remains in unit cost is the claim on the terminal input. A worked extension of the same instance shows what build time adds: keep the operating dials and give the machine a build recipe (aI, λI, bI) = (0.1, 0.2, 0.02) at (ρ, δ, J) = (0.05, 0.10, 3), so uK = 0.165. The land coefficient of a machine service rises from θc = 1 to 2.41: carrying the build recipe at the user cost more than doubles the land content of the substitute at these dials, before any task gets automated. (Appendix A carries the matrix version for many machine types and several terminal inputs, and the capitalized corner forms.)

Ideas are the boundary case. An idea's reproduction requires approximately nothing of anything, so its competitive price is approximately zero; any positive price on an idea is institutional scarcity, chosen — and chosen for a reason Romer (1990) made canonical: at marginal-cost pricing, ideas with fixed creation costs are not created. Institutional rents are therefore real, revocable, and policy-elastic; the taxonomy of Section 7's corollary keeps a column for them without confusing them with the physical kind.

5. Build time and the wage of waiting

Capital in this model is time, in two senses. Waiting time: the interest rate ρ prices the interval between paying for inputs and receiving output, Sraffa's reduction of every produced price to dated terminal services summed at interest — Appendix A's capitalized closures are that reading, and the interest they add is the wage of waiting. Build time: capacity ordered today serves J periods from today, and no price can shorten the queue inside the period. This section derives the one factor that carries both into the steady state, then names the parameter the paper turns on.

The user cost, derived. A unit of capacity started now costs pK, paid at the start; its first service arrives J periods later, undepreciated; its mass then wears at δ per period. Free entry equates the build price to the present value of the net rentals the unit will earn. In steady state the rental margin is constant and the geometric sum collapses, leaving one condition: the margin equals uK·pK, with

uK = (ρ + δ)·(1 + ρ)J−1.

This is Jorgenson's user cost, interest plus wear, marked up by compound interest over the build wait; the exponent is J−1 rather than J as a consequence of the timing just stated, and the derivation is computer-algebra-checked rather than asserted, because the off-by-one is the classic error. The corners locate the factor's two ingredients: at J = 1 it is ρ+δ (and Appendix A's capitalized closures are corner cases of the build recipe); at ρ = 0 it collapses to δ, since with no price on waiting a nuclear plant and a shed differ only in wear and J drops out; at δ = 0 it is a pure waiting cost. Wear and waiting are separable, and J compounds only the second.

Direct and total coefficients. Proposition 2 wrote every price as a coefficient on the rent. The direct coefficients (b, bI) are the land a process uses; the total coefficients θ are the land a price resolves to, in the sense of Leontief's total requirements: θc for a machine service, θw = γ(x*)·θc for the wage, and for any good j using mj machine-hours, lj labor-hours, and bj land directly, θj = mjθc + ljθw + bj. Section 7 reads real wages as ratios of these numbers.

Horizon-terminality is a parameter. Call input j terminal over horizon h when new capacity cannot arrive within it: Jj > h. What Section 4 called terminal informally is a build lag read against a horizon. Transformer capacity with a ten-year queue is terminal over five years and produced over twenty; a fabrication bottleneck is terminal for the years its build runs; land is the input with J = ∞, terminal at every horizon, and that is its classifier — not a sector label, a lag no price can shorten. Appendix E's transition results are ordered by this parameter.

The interest identity. Interest income is competitive and real, and the recursion sizes it. In steady state machine owners' net income is ρ times the market value of machine wealth WK: installed capacity at the value of its remaining rentals, plus the builds in progress at compounded cost — the gestation float. At J = 1 machine wealth is exactly pKK and the income exactly ρpKK; Appendix A states the ledger and the closed form of WK, and the algebra is computer-checked. Because the stock is limited by the land that operates and builds it, the identity fixes the ratio of interest income to aggregate rent; at the worked instance of Section 4 the ratio is 0.07. Ownership does not move the steady state: free entry makes every vintage zero net present value, so a stock financed at the world rate — domestic owners with full-value debt, or foreign owners outright — passes nothing to household income at rest, and the convention only decides who books the windfalls off it (Appendix E).

6. The floor: what prices exit

The task margin says what a firm will pay. A worker accepts when the offer beats the value of the best alternative — the outside option s of search theory, the reservation value of participation models. In most labor models s is a parameter, and for short-run questions that is exactly right. For long-run technological change it conceals a price, and the concealment matters, because the empirical debate of search and matching is changed significantly on this object's level.

Exit is a consumption problem. A person who leaves the labor market lives on household production, family, savings, transfers, informal work, a plot — and every one of those arrangements uses housing, space, energy, food, and care. Field evidence prices the components: the value of non-work time and household production is substantial and heterogeneous (Mas and Pallais 2019), and the calibration that resolves the volatility puzzle sets the aggregate outside option high for exactly this reason (Hagedorn and Manovskii 2008). If the inputs of unwaged life become expensive relative to goods, the value of exit falls with no change in anyone's capabilities.

Model the exit life directly. Let q = r/p be the price of non-produced services relative to the goods price p (the machine-made good is the numeraire below). An independent exit arrangement yields a keep s0 in goods units and requires he units of land services — quality-adjusted, so no particular parcel is needed. If no plot is affordable, exit degrades to a dependency floor s̲ < s0: living on someone else's land, at someone else's sufferance — in the modern economy, most often a parent's spare room. So

s(q) = max( s0 − q·he ,  s̲ ),

falling one-for-one with q·he until the dependency floor binds; from there, further rent increases find nothing in the exit bundle left to price. The functional form is deliberately minimal; the only essential property is ∂s/∂q ≤ 0, and the caveat is that an exit provisioned with machine-made goods adds a falling goods term — the conclusion stands wherever the exit bundle keeps a positive non-produced share, which is the same condition within the fork of Section 7.

Proposition 3 (priced exit). (i) While suitable parcels lie idle — parcels on which the keep s0 is attainable — the marginal such parcel rents at zero: the exit plot is free and s = s0. An unpriced margin of this kind is what "a commons" is, economically: idle land, family access, public provision — any arrangement supplying the scarce input below its scarcity rent. (ii) Once no suitable parcel lies idle, the exit plot pays the ruling rent and s = s(q) above. The trigger is land scarcity, not machine capability: the mechanism operates in the sloped regime (γ still sloped at the margin), where the evidence lives. (iii) For workers with ∂s/∂q < 0, a rise in q shifts labor supply outward: participation weakly rises, and by Proposition 1(ii) the equilibrium wage weakly falls where the schedule has slope (at a flat schedule the wage is pinned and only participation moves).

(iv) Both lives priced. A working life also uses land, hw units, so the full comparison is w − q·hw against max(s0 − q·he, s̲). With hw = he the rent cancels from the independent branch identically, and technology moves participation only through the wage. Part (iii) is therefore a rigidity claim and a funding claim, not a symmetry: the exit that collapses is the one tied to a specific plot while the working life can economize on land, and the exit that survives automation is the one funded by a land claim — a source with terminal coefficient θe, so s0 = θe·r holds its ratio to rent while a wage-funded keep inherits θw and shrinks with it.

Proof. (i) Any positive rent on the marginal parcel is undercut by an idle neighbor of reservation rent zero. (ii) With no idle neighbor, the exit plot must outbid other uses for the marginal suitable parcel, whose rent is now positive; the keep is s0 − q·he until s̲ binds. (iii) A lower reservation value moves the participation step down: more workers accept at any wage; the wage response is Proposition 1's comparative statics. (iv) Subtract: (w − qh) − (s0 − qh) = w − s0, free of q; the funding statements are Proposition 2's statics applied to the source's coefficient. On the dependency branch the cancellation needs the floor life to occupy the same land; a land-free floor leaves q·hw on the work side. ∎

The historical signs run opposite ways. In the enclosure era the exit held the plot and the working life held a tenement: he > hw, and rising rents crushed exit first. In the modern configuration work is location-rigid — the job market prices the metro, the exit can leave it — and the asymmetry can flip sign for renters while staying put for anyone whose fallback is a specific spare room. The claim the model keeps is (iv)'s: what automation does to the outside option runs through the funding source's coefficient and the bundle's rigidity, not through rent as such, and the one outside option whose coefficient cannot shrink is a claim on rent itself — Section 9's dividend. Appendix E adds the clock: during a buildout the erosion arrives with the capacity, not the announcement.

A parcel rents at zero because little can be done with it — which is why the truly idle margin supports no exit either. Free and livable together is a particular historical configuration: the commons proper, and its closing is the enclosure read below. When no suitable parcel idles, the floor s̲ is not land-free but otherwise-funded, in three modern forms: dependency — a room supplied out of someone else's wage or ownership, an unpaid transfer from the employed to the exited; public provision — the out-of-work payment of Appendix C.1, unconditional in few places and thin where it exists; and tolerated use — living on land one does not hold, its price paid in eviction risk rather than rent. Each is a commons in the degraded sense: the scarce input supplied below its rent, from someone's budget, the state's, or the law's forbearance. None is insulated from q: the host's capacity to share inherits the host's rent bill, so the flat segment at s̲ is an upper bound, and Appendix C.4's race is run while the fallback itself deteriorates.

This is the model's reading of enclosure. Close the idle margin — by law, as in the historical enclosures, or by price, as when housing scarcity does the same work parcel by parcel — and three things happen at once: the exit value falls, participation rises, and the wage falls at given technology. Enclosure manufactures labor supply. Marx said so as history; Lewis (1954) built the two-sector version, with the traditional sector's per-head product as the modern sector's wage anchor; Polanyi (1944) documented the institutional campaign. The model adds the price: the outside option falls one-for-one with q·he, which makes the mechanism measurable in rent-to-wage ratios, household formation, and coresidence. Appendix C.4 prices the race between this margin's closing and the arrival of a funded replacement.

6.1 The interval, closed

Assemble the two sides. The wage sits in an interval: at the top, replacement cost c·γ(x*), with c priced by the recursion; at the bottom, the exit value s(q). Terminal scarcity enters twice:

r → c(r) → labor demand,  q = r/p → s(q) → labor supply.

The two channels can pull the nominal wage in opposite directions: a higher terminal rent makes the substitute more expensive, which supports the wage; the same rent erodes exit, which expands supply and undercuts it. Which dominates is a parameter question. What is not ambiguous is the location of the walls: both are formed, in part, in the market for non-produced inputs, so the whole interval migrates when that market moves. In the language of Section 2.2: the fundamental surplus is productivity net of the outside option, and here both terms are prices; the same rent sits inside each. A calibration debate about where the walls are is, in this model, a measurement of the scarcity market.

Automation narrows the interval from both ends. Task automation and recursive automation lower the ceiling toward machine parity; the same progress raises q (Section 7), which lowers the floor toward dependency. As the schedule flattens the interval closes entirely: labor demand becomes perfectly elastic at c·γ̄, there is no surplus left to split, and the accounts defined inside the interval — bargaining shares, efficiency-wage premia, monopsony markdowns — lose their comparative statics not because they were wrong but because their object has vanished.

Heterogeneity. Real people carry personal schedules and personal outside options. Every statement above holds worker-type by worker-type: each type's wage sits in its own interval, so heterogeneity lifts the mean above the floor. Floor statements throughout refer to the bottom of the support, not the average. (Existence and uniqueness of the equilibrium, including the joint determination of (w, c, x*) given the land market and the participation step, is Appendix A.)

7. The flat-capability limit: the real-wage fork

Now take the limit: γ(x) uniform at γ̄ across tasks — the configuration toward which broad automation travels, and the one where, at the market wage, labor and machines cost the same at every task: machine parity. The limit removes assignment heterogeneity. Let L̄ = ∫dx/γL(x): the hours one human would need to complete the whole task list alone — the inverse of absolute solo productivity, a technological constant invariant to machine quality. Producing one unit of the good entirely by machine takes γ̄L̄ machine-hours, so p = c·γ̄·L̄, and at parity the wage is w = c·γ̄.

In coefficient language the fork is a ratio of two numbers on one scale. The real wage in any good j is w/pj = θw/θj: the rent cancels, and what remains compares the wage's land content with the good's. Caselli–Manning is the case θj proportional to θw — goods made by the same machines and labor as everything else — where the ratio is invariant to γ(x*), λ, and J together. The fork is the case θj bounded below by direct land content bj > 0, where w/pj ≤ θw/bj and automation sends the numerator to zero against a fixed floor. The gradient runs from ideas at θ = 0 through goods and energy to pure location at θ = 1; housing, energy, and food sit between the endpoints, placed by their land content rather than their sector label, and the classifier behind the placement is the build lag of Section 5 — what cannot be built at any horizon anchors the scale.

Proposition 4 (the real-wage fork). In the flat-capability regime, with machine services priced by the recursion of Section 4:

(i) The wage in machine-made goods is w/p = 1/L̄. Machine quality and the machine rental cancel: however good machines get, an hour buys what an hour alone could make.

(ii) The relative price of non-produced services is

r/p = (1 − a − λγ̄) / (b·γ̄·L̄),

rising as recursive automation removes labor from the recipe (λ ↓) and as the capability gap closes (γ̄ ↓), and diverging as γ̄ → 0 or b → 0. With the build recipe the same display holds with 1 − a − λγ̄ replaced by D and b by b + uKbI, equivalently r/p = 1/(γ̄L̄θc), and the divergence claims are unchanged. The divergence is not an artifact of fixing the recipe: at λ = 0, even substituting machines for land inside the recipe as b = b0(1−a) leaves r/p = 1/(b0γ̄L̄), bounded as a varies and still diverging as the gap closes.

(iii) Against a consumption bundle with expenditure share α on non-produced services — housing, siting, energy, food-acreage — the geometric index P = p1−αrα gives w/P = (1/L̄)·(p/r)α: the real wage falls without bound along the divergent margins if and only if α > 0, and is bounded at 1/L̄ when α = 0.

Proof. (i) w/p = cγ̄/(cγ̄L̄). (ii) Substitute c = br/(1−a−λγ̄) into r/p = r/(cγ̄L̄); the derivative in γ̄ is −(1−a)/(bγ̄²L̄) < 0, and the λ-derivative is −1/(bL̄) < 0, so both automation margins raise the ratio; the limits are elementary. (iii) is substitution into the index. ∎

Part (i) concedes the strongest objection to every technological-immiseration story: Caselli and Manning (2019) prove that new technology cannot lower the real wage in terms of the goods machines make, and the model agrees — that ratio is pinned by absolute human productivity, not machine quality. The objection is answered by splitting the consumption basket by reproducibility. Subsistence is not made of machine-made goods alone: shelter, space, energy, and the acreage under food carry irreducible non-produced content, and part (ii) says that is exactly where the value released by automation settles. The squeeze, when it comes, arrives through rent, not through the paycheck: purchasing power over what machines make is preserved; purchasing power over the ground under it is not. The fork is the model's central observable, it is already visible (Section 10, Figure 3), and its welfare bite turns on a measurable elasticity — if households could substitute away from non-produced services the fork would be a curiosity; the climbing shelter share of low-income budgets says which branch is live (Appendix B states the CES version, which reads the elasticity's sign off the shelter shares).

The concession also extends to the path, not only the endpoint. In Appendix E's sequence economy the goods wage never moves after a capability shock: Caselli–Manning holds in motion as well as at rest (numerically verified). What moves is the rent, and on a precise clock — frozen for exactly the build lag, then stepping to its higher level (Proposition E.2) — with several produced inputs passing the released value down the ladder of their lags until it settles on land (numerically verified). So "where the value settles" carries a timetable, set by the build lags, and the wage's land claim falls with the buildout under every shock tested; whether the goods wage dips or rises on release day is a diagnostic Section 11 takes up.

Corollary (terminal income). Parity does not erase the wage: at uniform capability labor still earns c·γ̄ per hour, and part (i) fixes what it buys. The wage goes to zero only in a stricter limit: γ̄ → 0 — machines not merely equal but unboundedly better — or parity below the outside option, so that labor exits. In that limit, with λ → 0, positive competitive factor income persists only on inputs outside the production recursion, plus institutional rents law maintains, transitional rents while capacity builds, and interest. In the one-terminal-factor closure with land the accounting balances exactly: every dollar of final spending resolves through the machine sector's zero-profit condition into site rent, and national income equals aggregate land rent as an identity. At parity, with labor still present, the identity weakens: all non-wage income is site rent (Appendix B states both cases; the wealth data lean this way — Rognlie 2015). The distribution of claims on that rent is then the whole distributional question, which is Section 9's subject.

8. History as three configurations of one schedule

The model reads the long record as three configurations of the relative-productivity schedule (Figure 2) — and, in motion, as three settings of the quantities Appendix E varies: the speed of the capability change, the build lags of the era's machines, and the labor content of making them.

Pre-industrial: the floor binds. With machines scarcely present, nearly every task is a labor task; the schedule is compressed and the binding boundary of the wage interval is the lower one, which is a land price. Wages sit near the exit value, the exit value tracks access to land, and demographic adjustment supplies the long-run equilibration — the world Ricardo and Malthus wrote down, and the one the new long-run estimates describe: pre-industrial English real wages strongly shaped by population and land, with productivity growth beginning well before wages escaped Malthusian pressure (Bouscasse, Nakamura, and Steinsson 2025). The classical account is one configuration of our model.

Industrial: the ceiling rises. The industrial revolution split capability violently and unevenly: engines far better than muscle at physical tasks, useless at cognitive ones. The schedule became enormously dispersed and steep — and, by Proposition 1(ii), the wage rose and gained insulation, because a steep schedule makes labor demand inelastic. Meanwhile machine production itself remained labor-intensive: in the recursion, both γ(x*) and λ supported the wage. The escape was neither immediate nor smooth — real wages grew slowly in the early decades while the capital deepening ran ahead — Engels' pause (Allen 2009; Crafts 2022), which Appendix E's speed-times-lag experiment reproduces in kind: a fast capability change against the era's long-gestation engines parks the released value on capital in place for the length of the crossing — and the model adds a joint reading to test rather than a settled claim: the wage's escape from the land-priced floor and land's departure from the production function are one reconfiguration, so the long factor-share and welfare-ratio series (Allen 2001; Clark 2005) should show them as one event. Section 10 lists that assembly as open.

Post-industrial: the flattening begins. Computerization flattened the simple-cognitive region of the schedule — the routine-task evidence of Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) and the polarization it produced (Autor and Dorn 2013) are the cross-sectional trace. AI is the candidate generalization of the same flattening, and it adds the margin computerization barely touched: λ, the labor inside the machine sector itself. Section 11 takes this up and states what would count against it.

9. The fiscal completion: rent tax, uniform dividend, and the horizon

9.1 The steady-state pair

The corollary reduces the distributional question to ownership: in its limit, a household without claims on terminal rents has no market income at all. The policy question is what instrument can move that distribution while the wage thins as both tax base and eligibility criterion. Two properties, each verified separately, meet in one pair of instruments.

Let terminal factors be indexed by j with fixed stocks Tj and competitive rents rj, and write R = Σj rjTj. Consider a tax at rate τR on pure ownership rents of these factors, with proceeds returned as a uniform per-person transfer d = τRR/N, paid identically in and out of work.

Proposition 5 (the limit welfare theorem). In the flat-capability limit with terminal factors fully employed:

(i) Production efficiency. The tax changes no factor supply (the stocks are fixed) and leaves gross rentals to allocate the stocks across uses, so production and adoption margins face unchanged social opportunity costs — the fixed-factor analogue of production-efficiency logic (Diamond and Mirrlees 1971). The tax base cannot contract in response to being taxed: a rent tax capitalizes into the asset's price and leaves the rental flow unchanged.

(ii) No participation wedge. The transfer enters both sides of the participation comparison — work iff w + d ≥ s + d — and cancels: the work–exit price w − s is untouched, so the compensated participation response is zero as an identity, and what remains is an income effect, which the program-evaluation evidence finds modest but not zero, larger for secondary earners (Hoynes and Rothstein 2019). Conditional instruments are different by design, not by size: a transfer paid only in work, or only out of it, moves the reservation wage through the substitution channel by design. The distinction is visible in the one universal case run at scale: the Alaska dividend — the state's annual universal cash payment from resource revenues — produced no detectable fall in aggregate employment, with part-time work up 1.8 percentage points (Jones and Marinescu 2022) — evidence consistent with the identity.

(iii) Implementation. Person i with ownership shares ωij receives (1−τR)·ΣjωijrjTj + τRR/N, which sums to R at every τR: as τR runs from zero to one the division of terminal income moves continuously from the inherited distribution to the equal one, with production efficient and no margin moved at any point on the path. What the second welfare theorem promises through lump-sum transfers that no tax system has ever had (Arrow 1951), the pair delivers along this one-parameter family with instruments that exist — because in the limit, terminal rent is the economy's one real lump sum.

Proof. (i) Fixed supplies do not respond to a tax on pure rents; users face gross rentals either way. (ii) d appears on both sides of the comparison; subtraction removes it. (iii) The sum is (1−τR)R + τRR = R given Σiωij = 1; no term moves a margin by (i) and (ii). ∎

First, the contrast that makes the pair necessary rather than merely available: a wage-funded transfer in the same limit is a closed loop — with labor demand perfectly elastic a wage tax at rate τw cannot pass to firms, so at full participation disposable income is (1−τw)cγ̄ + τw·cγ̄, identically cγ̄ at every rate — and with partial participation it is a self-undermining spiral (Appendix C.2 carries the full funding taxonomy, and C.1 the conditionality results: transfers keyed to work leak into lower wages or induce make-work, depending on the regime; transfers keyed to its absence deepen traps mechanically as wages compress). Second, the lineage: the efficiency of taxing fixed land is George (1879); the affinity with the Henry George theorem of Arnott and Stiglitz (1979) is real but distinct — there, aggregate land rents finance local public goods at optimal city size; here, terminal rents are the surviving base for redistribution at the automation limit. Third, the scope: uniqueness claims are within the model's instrument set, and away from the limit the pair is not sufficient: the rent base is still growing, and interim revenue must come from the transition's own bases — Appendix C's subject and a sequel's. What survives every regime, however, is the pair's directional property: the tax base that cannot flee, funding the one transfer that moves no margin.

9.2 The fiscal horizon

The transition adds a clock to the pair. Appendix E prices two kinds of windfall: quasi-rents on capacity in place, bounded by build windows, and the permanent settlement on land. Each is a candidate tax base, and the build lag sorts them.

Proposition 6 (the fiscal horizon). (i) A one-time levy on the quasi-rents of capacity in place enters no entry condition: the stock is sunk, the windfall was unanticipated, and no margin moves — the levy is lump-sum on the window. (ii) The same tax anticipated on new capacity is not: a rental tax at rate τK expected by entrants scales the user cost to uK/(1−τK), raising every θ and deterring the build. (iii) An input with lag Jj is therefore a clean base on stock in place over its own window only, and taxing it carries a time-consistency problem: the expectation of reintroduction is itself the anticipated tax of (ii). (iv) Land is the base with no entry margin at any horizon: the rent tax appears in no production price — θc, θw, and pK/r are free of τR identically. The algebra of (ii) and (iv) is computer-checked.

Proof. (i) is the definition of sunk: entry conditions price future units from future rentals, and a levy on past units appears in none of them. (ii) entrants require the after-tax margin to cover the user cost, so the pre-tax margin scales by 1/(1−τK); substitution into Proposition 2 raises every coefficient. (iii) applies (i) and (ii) to an input whose window closes. (iv) the stocks are fixed, so no entry condition exists for the tax to enter; inspection of the closed forms shows no τR term. ∎

The precedent shelf is the old-versus-new-capital literature: taxing existing capital while exempting new is the efficient confiscation of Auerbach–Kotlikoff and Judd, and its known instability — the promise not to repeat it is hard to keep — is exactly (iii). The pair of Proposition 5 escapes the instability because its base has no window to close: on land, the distinction between stock in place and stock to come never exists. On the transition itself, the ordering is practical: windfall levies on the buildout's quasi-rents are efficient precisely while the build windows of Appendix E are open, and convert into (ii)'s distortion the moment they are expected to persist; the durable instrument is the one the limit already selected.

9.3 Stabilizers as quantity protections

Three possible stabilizers are granted as preserving real wages if γ tends to γ̄, however none of them exist without deadweight. Human-essential tasks can persist by preference or by law — Appendix D grants the mechanism its theorem and shows the rescue is distributionally hollow where it operates (aggregate labor share saved, median wage not). Institutions can reserve work for people outright; that is a real instrument, and it is quantity protection rather than price protection. And supply expansion — building, densifying, powering — directly loosens the terminal constraint; it is the one lever that attacks q itself. Each carries a time signature in Appendix E's sense: a legal reservation holds until repeal, a build lag set by politics; a verified-human premium holds while verification does; and supply expansion is itself a build, the one lever whose J works for the wage rather than against it.

The wedge result behind that distinction is Acemoglu and Restrepo's (2026). Let a task pay a premium over the base wage — a union premium, a licensing rent, an efficiency wage — as a multiplicative wedge µ(x) > 1. Labor then holds the task only while w ≤ c·γ(x)/µ(x): allocation runs on the wedge-deflated schedule, a premium raises its own task's flip priority one-for-one, and automation is directed at the best-paid versions of each job — their targeting result, with the documented losses concentrated in the 70th–95th within-group percentiles. Any protection that operates through the price of labor therefore finances its own replacement; protection survives only in quantity form — a scope-of-practice rule extended to software, an enforceable automation ban, a mandated human in the loop. Under pressure, protection converts from price form toward legal form, and adoption at legally blocked tasks turns punctuated: nothing moves while the saving from flipping runs below the cost of confrontation; past it, everything the rule covers flips at once.

10. Measurement

Two objects are measured here. Every measured series below was computed under a set of classification variants with medians and min–max bands reported across the rule grid; sources and construction are in the data note. Every measured moment binds to steady-state coefficients; nothing below leans on Appendix E's transition numerics.

The fork. The same average U.S. paycheck, indexed at 1964, buys nearly four times the durables and a fifth less shelter (Figure 3): a near-fivefold fork between the machine-made and land-priced denominators, opening as machine capability spread. This is Proposition 4, the wage in mostly machine-made goods in one series and in mostly land-priced services in the other.

The floor's coverage. If the state captured every dollar of site rent and mailed each person an equal check, what fraction of one person's subsistence would it buy? Appendix C.3 derives this coverage ratio κ inside the model; Figure 4 measures it: aggregate site-rent flow (land residuals from the Federal Reserve's Z.1 accounts — market value minus replacement-cost structures — under capitalization-rate variants, with the housing-services flow as a lower-bound member) against population times a costed subsistence bundle. κ = 0.33 [0.18–0.59] in 2025, up from roughly 0.05 in the 1950s — the same q that erodes the wage and the exit value has been raising the replacement base for seventy years, as Proposition 4(ii) says it should. Two caveats. Every land measure used is a lower bound, so the truth likely sits above the band's middle. And κ's upper bound is physical: coverage can never exceed the land-per-head ratio.

The fiscal exposure. On the same accounts, 0.72 [0.66–0.81] of U.S. consumption financing is wage-linked and 0.68 of tax revenue is wage-exposed (2025) — the state taxes precisely the base the technology erodes; the full ledger is Appendix C's table and Figure 5.

Three possible interpretations of existing measures (1) The slope from incidence: under Proposition 1, worker-borne payroll-tax shares map to the inverse slope of the schedule at the margin; the quasi-experimental estimates — ranging from effectively full shifting onto wages (Gruber 1997) through partial (Kugler and Kugler 2009: formal wages fall 1.4–2.3% per 10% payroll-tax rise) to limited short-run shifting with firm-side responses (Saez, Schoefer, and Seim 2019) — assembled chronologically on a common pass-through definition, would read the schedule's flattening as a time series. (2) λ: the labor content of machine production is an input-output object — the labor share of the machine-producing and machine-operating sectors, and the resolution of a unit of machine-intensive output into wages versus terminal rents after recursively removing intermediates, the empirical counterpart of the matrix recursion c = Ac + Λw + Br of Appendix A. Recursive automation appears as this series falling. (3) The long record: welfare ratios (Allen 2001; Clark 2005) against long-run land-share series, spanning the two regime changes of Section 8, testing the joint reading flagged there.

The rival for the shelter leg. Zoning and supply restriction are the standing explanation for expensive housing: an institutional restriction is a chosen scarcity, and loosening it would move q. Two replies to that explanation. The durables leg of the fork owes nothing to housing policy, and its rise is the larger share of the divergence. And the model's claim is about where value migrates given whatever stocks are scarce — chosen scarcity is still scarcity, and its rents still land where Section 7 says; what zoning changes is how much of the scarcity is elective, which matters enormously for policy and not at all for the accounting. The discriminating evidence is cross-sectional, the fork's slope against local regulation and local exposure.

11. Implications for artificial intelligence

AI is the first technology that very plausibly moves both automation margins at once. It lowers γ(x*) wherever models reach parity at final tasks — and it lowers λ, because software generation, chip design, datacenter operation, logistics, and research are themselves the machine sector's labor. The strong scenario of this paper requires both empirically: capability closing faster than reinstatement replenishes it, and the machine-production network shedding its own labor. If neither happens, the flat limit is a curiosity. If both do, the direction of travel is the one derived here: the wage's ceiling and floor converge, and the released value settles as rents on sites, power, water, minerals, and whatever law elects to protect — not on the reproducible capital, whose price the recursion competes away, except transiently, for the length of a build window. The prediction for the AI buildout is therefore a dated sequence, ordered by J, not a list. Model weights are ideas, copiable and distillable (distillation: cheaply training one model to imitate another): θ = 0, no durable claim at all. Chip capacity is the short-lag input, power and interconnection the long-lag input, and the waterfall result (T2, numerically verified in Appendix E) says their quasi-rents should decay in that order, each on roughly the timescale of its own build lag, with the land under and around the buildout the permanent residue. The release-day diagnostic of Appendix E rides along: where a model release extends capability to new tasks, the goods wage moves at the release and the land-denominated wage with the buildout (T5, the conjecture Appendix E splits by shock type). And the sequence is falsifiable in place: compute and power quasi-rents that fail to decay on the timescale of their build lags — or that settle on the reproducible stock rather than the sites behind it — count against the model, in addition to the two requirements above.

12. Conclusion

One timing fact changes the policy conclusion: the same relative price q that lowers the wage and erodes the exit option also raises the value of the base that could replace both. Coverage of a rent-funded floor has climbed from 0.05 to one-third in seventy years because the same rising rent that opens the fork is the coverage ratio's numerator. Necessity and feasibility travel together on one parameter, while the fiscal system keys 0.68 of its revenue to the base that is thinning. The pair of Proposition 5 is where that mismatch resolves: a tax whose base cannot contract, funding a transfer that moves no margin, delivering redistribution without deadweight exactly where every wage-keyed instrument goes degenerate. Whether the economy arrives at the limit is an empirical question that is hard to measure at the scale of every good and policy system, yet what to do if it does is not an open question, and the instruments are old. The transition mechanics — which bases bridge the crossing, at what deadweight, on what timetable — are priced in Appendix C and developed in a sequel.

This gives us the final empirical question that likely must be answered soon: is the economy's price recursion becoming less wage-linked and more scarcity-linked, and is the same scarcity eroding the value of exit? The transition adds a third: are the buildout's quasi-rents decaying on the timescale of their build lags?

Appendix A. The environment and assignment equilibrium

The economy below generalizes the model of Sections 3–5; the main text is the case with every extension absent — one machine type, no reserved tasks, no government — and each appendix relaxes one restriction. Notation is defined here once, and the machine sector's general form — many machine types, durability — is stated here as well. Every result is proved in the configuration it states, with the remaining restrictions in place; existence for the unrestricted economy is not claimed.

Tasks and technology. A final good requires a continuum of tasks x ∈ [0,1] in equal amounts (Section 3). One human-hour completes γL(x) units of task x, one machine-hour γM(x); relative human productivity is γ(x) = γL(x)/γM(x). A set H of measure |H| ≥ 0 is closed to machines — by capability, preference, or law (Appendix D).

Machines. Machine services are produced. With one machine type, a unit takes a units of machine services, λ labor-hours, and b units of non-produced services renting at r (Section 4). With many produced inputs, c is the vector of their prices, A the produced-input requirements matrix, Λ the labor requirements, B the requirements of terminal inputs with rent vector r; free entry gives c = Ac + Λw + Br, hence c = (𝟙−A)−1(Λw + Br) provided the spectral radius of A is below one (𝟙 the identity matrix, since I is investment) — the many-good statement of net reproduction. The scalar text carries the full logic: produced-input prices pass backward until anchored by factors outside the recursion and, during transition, by the labor still embodied in produced inputs. The task margin selects which machine type is the binding alternative at x*: the one minimizing delivered task cost, and the threshold condition holds against that minimum. Free entry prices every produced input at unit cost.

Durability, time preference, and the capitalized corners. With one-period building and time preference ρ (the interest rate, distinct from the rent r), the λ = 0 recursion becomes c = br(1+ρ)/(1 − a(1+ρ)), finite iff a(1+ρ) < 1; with wear at rate δ, the user-cost recursion gives c = br(ρ+δ)/(1 − a(ρ+δ)). With λ > 0 the same closures hold with the recipe's labor priced at the margin: c = br(1+ρ)/(1 − (1+ρ)(a + λγ(x*))) and c = br(ρ+δ)/(1 − (ρ+δ)(a + λγ(x*))), finite iff (1+ρ)(a + λγ(x*)) < 1, respectively (ρ+δ)(a + λγ(x*)) < 1. Read as Sraffa's reduction: every produced price is dated terminal services summed at interest, and the added term is the wage of waiting, competitive and real. These are corner cases of the build recipe of Sections 4–5 — a machine whose whole recipe is build (no operating inputs) capitalized at uK, with uK(J=1) = ρ+δ and uK(J=1, δ=1) = 1+ρ reproducing the two displays. (All computer-algebra-checked.) Horizon-terminality is formalized in Section 5: input j is terminal over h iff Jj > h, land at every horizon; the propositions hold per horizon, and our permanent-case claims use land alone.

Non-produced inputs. Terminal stocks are fixed: Tj at rent rj where several matter (Section 8), land the permanent case — parcels of heterogeneous quality, reservation rent zero at the idle margin, rent schedule r(z) over parcels z (Appendix B). Land serves machine production and households: housing, and the he units an independent exit requires, and the hw units a working life requires.

Appendix B. The land-only closure

Take Appendix A's economy to the full limit: γ flat at γ̄, λ = 0, ρ = 0, one terminal factor (land, homogeneous here; the heterogeneous case below), preferences at σ = 1 — Cobb–Douglas with share α — the stock T jointly held and split between production use TP and housing TH.

Proposition B.1 (closure and conservation of rents). (i) Equilibrium exists with unique real allocation: TH = αT, TP = (1−α)T, final-good output Y = (1−a)(1−α)T/(bγ̄L̄). (ii) The entire goods bill is production-land rent and national income is aggregate site rent identically: pY = rTP, pY + rTH = rT. (iii) Market clearing reproduces the cost-side relative price r/p = (1−a)/(bγ̄L̄): demand side and cost side agree on the same number.

Proof. Gross machine services solve X = γ̄L̄Y + aX, so X = γ̄L̄Y/(1−a) and production land demand is bX. Substituting p = brγ̄L̄/(1−a) gives pY = rbX = rTP: goods spending passes through the zero-profit machine sector and accrues, all of it, to production land. Land being the only primary factor, household income is rT; Cobb–Douglas shares give rTH = αrT and pY = (1−α)rT; clearing pins Y and dividing returns (iii). ∎

Dissipation is migration, by construction: the obvious escape — automation's value dissipating into consumer surplus at collapsing prices — is blocked, because prices do collapse and the identity holds; what collapses is p relative to r, never rent's claim on the flow of spending. Heterogeneous parcels: with a rent schedule over parcels in use and reservation rent zero at the idle margin, rT reads ∫r(z)dz over parcels and nothing changes: the proposition requires only that spending, wherever it goes, crosses a site. Labor still present at parity: the identity weakens to "all non-wage income is site rent," GDP = N·cγ̄ + rT, recovering the full identity as γ̄ → 0. At ρ > 0 the accounting gains the interest flow of Section 5's identity — domestic product is rT + ρWK in the labor-free limit — and the statements here are the ρ = 0 reading. Feedback: a rent-funded floor is spent partly on land services, lifting r and the base rT — favorable in direction, distribution-dependent in size; claimed as tendency only.

General σ. With the general elasticity σ of Appendix A's preferences between goods and land services, the land share of expenditure becomes

α(q) = αq1−σ / (αq1−σ + 1 − α),

and as automation sends q up (Proposition 4(ii)): α(q) → 1 for σ < 1 — complements; housing takes the whole budget and the fork binds — while α(q) → 0 for σ > 1 and substitution defuses it. The climbing shelter share of low-income budgets is the σ < 1 signature.

Appendix C. The fiscal system in the sloped regime

The main text proves what survives the limit; this appendix carries the transition results, plus the measured ledger — Appendix A's government in full, in the sloped regime. Everything below passed the same check discipline as the main text.

C.1 Conditionality

A transfer program is Appendix A's pair (mw, me): what it pays per period in work and out of it. The worker compares w + mw against s + me, so the margin sees only Δ = mw − me and the reservation wage is s − Δ (cash in exit also lifts the exit value itself — an income effect the evidence puts small; Hoynes and Rothstein 2019). An in-work benefit (Δ > 0) recruits participation: in the sloped regime part of the benefit leaks into lower wages through the margin — the Speenhamland result — the 1795 poor-relief wage subsidies read as depressing wages — rebuilt without employer power — and at machine parity it induces work whose replacement value is below the outside option: society loses s − cγ̄ per hour of employment so preserved. An out-of-work benefit (Δ < 0) raises the reservation wage and taxes the first hour; as parity compresses the wage distribution toward the floor, the mass of workers inside any fixed benefit-withdrawal (phase-out) band rises mechanically — the trap worsens with no rule change. The uniform transfer is the unique Δ = 0 instrument: zero compensated participation response as an identity. The three failure modes are one result read at three values of Δ.

C.2 Funding, and the incidence dial

At the limit, a payroll-funded transfer is the closed loop of Section 9; away from it, with labor-demand elasticity εD (mapped by Proposition 1(ii) to the inverse slope at the margin) and participation elasticity εS, workers bear εD/(εD+εS) of a payroll tax, and exactly that fraction of a wage-funded transfer is circular: taken from the net wage, handed back as the transfer. The regime is therefore measurable: every payroll-incidence estimate is a reading of the schedule's slope, which is Section 10's assembly (1). The land tax's properties (base cannot contract; capitalizes into price, not flow) hold at every point of the transition, not only at the limit — which is why the pair of Proposition 5 is close to a no-regret instrument: its non-distortion never depended on the limit arriving.

C.3 Feasibility: the coverage ratio

Per person, subsistence is a bundle (gs, hs) of goods and land services costing Ps = pgs + r·hs; full capture of site rent funds rT/N per head; coverage at full capture is κ = rT/(N·Ps) = qT/(N(gs + q·hs)), strictly increasing in q toward the physical ceiling T/(N·hs), with κ ≥ 1 iff q ≥ q* = N·gs/(T − N·hs) — a finite threshold that exists iff T > N·hs. The fiscal constraint is a land constraint in disguise: the floor is fundable exactly when the direct plus embodied land content of N bundles fits in the land there is. Measured: Section 10's Figure 4 (0.05 → 0.33 [0.18–0.59], 1953–2025), with the ceiling caveat stated there. Worked instance (a = 0.6, b = 0.2, γ̄ = 1.8, L̄ = 1, so q = 10/9; T = 100; unit bundle): fifty people are covered with room to spare (κ = 20/19); eighty must wait for q to reach 4; one hundred twenty are never covered.

C.4 The race with enclosure

Proposition 3's independent exit ends where rising rents exhaust the keep, at qenc = (s0−s̲)/he; the fundable floor arrives at q*. The natural floor outlasts the replacement's arrival iff qenc ≥ q*, i.e. iff N ≤ qencT/(gs + qenchs) — a crowding condition (60 people in the worked instance, where qenc = 1.5). Otherwise a gap opens — commons gone, floor not yet fundable — and transition instruments are rescue, not optimization. Two closing observations: the uniform transfer's cancellation survives any s(q), and past qenc the guaranteed floor is s̲ + d — the transfer replaces the commons rather than supplementing it, billed at exactly the he·qenc the enclosure took, funded from inside the rT the enclosure created.

C.5 The mix on the way down

Until κ reaches one, a full floor needs revenue beyond site rents, and the two reaching instruments miss opposite bases: a land tax reaches every site rent, saved or consumed, but no bottleneck profit or royalty; a uniform consumption tax reaches whatever finances consumption — including exactly those non-site rents — but its wage-financed slice is a payroll tax in disguise. With φC the wage-financed share of consumption (φG, in the table, its tax-revenue counterpart), filling the rent base first is optimal (its marginal deadweight is zero at every rate), the consumption tax covers the shortfall, and minimized deadweight is proportional to φC(1−κ)² — the product of two measured series, 0.72 and 0.33 today, giving 0.32; at (0.50, 0.60) it would be 0.08. Both series are moving the right way, the consumption tax is scaffolding by construction, and in the corollary's limit the bases merge (all consumption resolves into rent income by B.1) and the mix question disappears.

Appendix D. Human-essential tasks

Let Appendix A's set H have positive measure |H|: tasks machines cannot hold — by capability residue, by verified preference for human hands, or by law — with H-wage wH. Let machines grow unboundedly better at every task outside H: γ(x) → 0 there, the stricter limit of Section 7's corollary confined to the tasks machines can hold. The machine rental c stays pinned by the recursion; what vanishes is the per-task cost of machine work, c/γM(x) = c·γ(x)/γL(x). Aghion, Jones, and Jones (2019) raise the strongest stabilizer this paper faces: Baumol concentration — expenditure concentrating on the sector whose costs refuse to fall (Baumol 1967), here the human-essential set — can preserve labor's share indefinitely. Proposition D.1 grants the mechanism its theorem and makes its premise explicit.

Proposition D.1 (Baumol concentration). (i) Any good whose task list contains H has unit cost tending to |H|·wH/γL as γ → 0 outside H: labor's share of its cost tends to one. (ii) Under CES substitution σH between H-content and H-light substitutes, the expenditure share of H-content tends to 1 if σH < 1, to the taste weight if σH = 1, and to 0 if σH > 1. (iii) On the σH < 1 branch with three categories (machine-made goods, land services, H-labor), end-state expenditure splits between the two non-collapsing categories — land services and H-labor — and labor's aggregate share tends to the H-share of that split. Our baseline is exactly the H = ∅ edge of this result.

Proof. (i) H-tasks cost |H|·wH/γL whatever machines do; each task outside H costs c·γ(x)/γL(x), and the recursion keeps c bounded as γ falls (Proposition 2), so the remainder vanishes with γ. (ii) is Appendix B's share display with q read as the relative price of H-content: the costlier category's share rises with its relative price iff σH < 1, and by (i) that relative price diverges. (iii) A H-free good's price vanishes by (i) at |H| = 0, so for σH < 1 its share vanishes, leaving the displayed split; setting the H taste weight to zero returns the baseline. ∎

Two anchors, and a hollowness result. Credence (the buyer cannot verify provenance): preference-based membership binds only if provenance can be verified: suppose buyers carry a taste premium for verified human work, verification catches a false claim with probability v, and a caught claim pays a penalty f.

Lemma D.2 (the fraud bound). A premium on verified human work is sustainable only up to v·f/(1−v): at any higher premium, false claiming is profitable in expectation and entry by fakes competes the premium away. The bound rises in v and in f, diverges as v → 1, and collapses to zero as f → 0 for every v < 1.

Proof. A false claimant earns the premium with probability 1 − v and pays f with probability v; expected profit is nonpositive iff the premium is at most v·f/(1−v). The comparative statics and limits are elementary. ∎

With price-only enforcement (f → 0) the bound collapses for every v < 1: certified-human markets unravel through fakes, not robots. H's durable core is what law reserves plus what co-presence verifies — being in the room, where faking is impossible rather than merely punished. Superstars: within H, the broadcastable fraction ψ of demand concentrates on top performers (Rosen 1981) while only the co-present remainder is rationed by human capacity.

Lemma D.3 (superstar concentration). Let the broadcastable fraction ψ of H-expenditure accrue to a set of performers of measure zero, and let the co-present remainder be shared uniformly across the H-workforce. The mean H-income is unchanged, and the median H-income is (1−ψ) times the mean.

Proof. Total H-income equals H-expenditure whatever its division; the stars carry the fraction ψ on measure zero, every other worker receives the uniform share (1−ψ) times the mean, and a measure-zero top moves no quantile below the top. ∎

Wherever H employs a minority the economy-wide median worker holds no H-task at all, and on the σH < 1 branch the aggregate labor share can persist, even approach one, while the median wage collapses to the floor: aggregate rescue, median collapse. The Baumol case also raises the floor's price: care and teaching put human hours in the subsistence bundle. Extend C.3's bundle with ns hours of H-service and coverage becomes κ = qT/(N·(gs + ns·wH/p + q·hs)) — below the H-free ratio at every q, and further below as the H-hour's price rises.

E.1 The sequence economy

This appendix sets Appendix A's environment in motion; the moving parts are these. Capacity Kt is installed at the start of t; builds started at t arrive at t+J: Kt+1 = (1−δ)Kt + It+1−J. Services cannot exceed capacity, and when capacity binds the service price clears the market, leaving a net rental per unit of stock,

πt = ct − a·ct − λ·wt − b·rt,

the quasi-rent that was invisible at rest because free entry ties it there to the user cost. Entry is forward-looking: builds start whenever the build price is covered by the present value of the rentals the unit will earn, It ≥ 0 with equality of price and present value wherever building is positive. At t = 0 an unanticipated permanent capability improvement arrives — a fall in γ̄ in the flat case, a shift of the schedule in the sloped case — and the path is perfect foresight thereafter. Households consume current income each period; builds are financed externally at the world rate, and the ownership convention decides only who books the surprises on capacity financed before the shock (the baseline books them to households; the steady state is convention-free by the interest identity's zero-profit logic).

Clearing, incomes, and conventions. Periods t = 0, 1, …; goods numeraire each period. Capacity: Kt installed at the start of t; builds It started at t, paid at the start, arrive at t+J: Kt+1 = (1−δ)Kt + It+1−J; the first service is undepreciated and wear runs post-install — the timing behind uK's exponent. Services Xt ≤ Kt, with the net rental πt = ct − act − λwt − brt zero at slack capacity. Entry is the complementarity It ≥ 0 with pK,t ≥ PV of the unit's rentals, equality where building is positive. Households consume current income with the Cobb–Douglas split; builds are financed externally at ρ, so goods clearing carries the net export of rentals less build inflows, and Walras closes as an identity — household income at rest is wNa + rT exactly (the interest identity's zero-profit logic), and off rest the domestic convention adds the surprise on pre-shock vintages, (πt − πold)·St with St the surviving pre-shock mass, to household income; the foreign convention books it abroad. The income convention is a real choice off rest — it moves housing demand — and the baseline is domestic.

E.2 The steady-state equivalence

Proposition E.1 (steady-state equivalence). Constant paths of the sequence economy satisfy exactly the coefficient system of Sections 4–5: the rental margin equals uKpK, prices are θcr and θwr, replacement investment is δK, and goods clearing holds as an identity with the external-finance flows included. Conversely, the coefficient system's solution extends to a constant sequence satisfying entry, the capacity law, and every market. The identity is machine-checked in both directions, symbolically in the flat case and on a worked sloped configuration.

The equivalence is what licenses the paper's architecture: Sections 4–7 never leaned on the transition, and nothing below re-proves the steady state. Existence for the sloped sequence system is not claimed in general; the flat case has a closed form, and the numerical paths are validated in E.4.

Regimes and existence. The flat case has three steady-state regimes: all-labor (entry unprofitable), interior (the closed forms of the text), and an operation-bound corner where machine-operating labor absorbs the workforce and the wage decouples upward from γ̄c; the interior region is genuinely narrow in (T, γ̄, J), the worked dials sit inside it at both shock endpoints, and the solver checks interiority and participation every period rather than assuming them. Existence for the sloped sequence system is not claimed; the flat transition has Proposition E.2's closed form at bI = 0, and numerical paths elsewhere are validated in E.4. Proposition E.1's equivalence is the bridge: constant sequences are exactly the coefficient system, checked symbolically in the flat case and on the worked sloped configuration, whose build→0 limit reproduces this appendix's static instantiation.

E.3 The flat transition in closed form

Proposition E.2 (the frozen rent). Let the schedule be flat, let build recipes use no land (bI = 0), let the interior regime hold at both endpoint steady states, and let the post-shock capacity target exceed the depreciated inherited stock. After an unanticipated permanent fall in γ̄:

(i) The land rent is frozen at its old level for exactly J periods, then jumps to its new steady-state level: the old pipeline maintains the stock, the goods wage is γ̄-invariant, so nothing in land clearing can move until new capacity physically arrives.

(ii) The quasi-rent is a rectangle: πt sits at one elevated value on the window, then at the new user cost; the window premium is b·(rnew − rold) per unit.

(iii) Steady-state Tobin's Q is not 1 but (1+ρ)J−1 — installed capacity carries the gestation float over a queued build — and the impact value of the installed stock exceeds that benchmark by the discounted window premium: Q0 − (1+ρ)J−1 = (1 − qqJ)·b(rnew − rold)/((ρ+δ)pK), with qq = (1−δ)/(1+ρ).

Proof. Computer-checked algebra on the stated conditions: the pipeline holds K at its old level for J periods while w = γL is unchanged, so the old land-clearing identity survives the shock unchanged on the window; entry from t = 0 delivers from t = J, where the new steady state's clearing pins the rent; the Q statements are the geometric sums of the rectangle. ∎

The sentence version: the rent stands still for exactly as long as it takes to build the machine that will raise it. What an owner of capacity in place holds on release day is the build lag itself, priced.

E.4 The solver, validated before use

The transition paths above are solved numerically away from the closed form, and this subsection states how and why the computations deserve belief. The solver is a damped fixed point on the investment path under the entry complementarity — building nonnegative, and wherever it is positive the build price equals the unit's present value. Given a path, capacity follows the law of motion with the pre-shock pipeline included; each period's remaining objects then solve in closed form (the flat split is linear; the sloped margin is the positive root of a quadratic, and of a linear equation on a capped stretch); land clearing is linear in the rent once the income convention's surprise term is included; present-value gaps then update investment, with adaptive damping and the tail clamped at the terminal steady state.

Acceptance is a ladder, run in order. First, the exact benchmark: on Proposition E.2's configuration the solver must reproduce the closed-form frozen-rent path; it does, to a maximum rent deviation of 25 parts in a trillion. Second, horizon insensitivity: extending the horizon by half moves the overlapping path by less than one part in ten trillion. Third, endpoints: every experiment starts and ends on steady states that reproduce the computer-algebra targets to one part in a billion, and the build-recipe-to-zero limit reproduces this paper's static instantiation to the same tolerance. Only after the ladder passes does the solver touch the sloped case. Interiority, viability, and participation are checked at every period of every path and reported, never assumed; the delivery-cycle ripples visible in the figures are equilibrium objects, stable in amplitude across solver tolerances, not iteration noise. One solved subtlety is reported rather than hidden: rentals inside the shortest build window of the two-input experiment are a transfer among sunk owners that the equilibrium does not pin down; the figures display the stated convention. E.5 Windfall, waterfall, and speed times lag

The windfall (T1, numerically verified; Figure 6). At the worked dials with bI > 0 and the domestic convention, the shock delivers Q0 = 1.209 against the steady-state benchmark 1.103: owners of capacity in place gain, in present value, about a tenth of replacement cost per unit. The goods wage never moves — Caselli–Manning holds on the path, not only at rest — while the wage's land claim drops at the release, sits below its old value at every subsequent date, and settles at the lower steady state. The path is not step-by-step monotone: the investment surge's own land demand and the windfall income front-load the rent slightly at release, and delivery cycles ripple it; the claims above are the envelope statements, and the ripples are equilibrium objects, stable across solver tolerances. Of the value the shock releases, land takes almost all of it: the present value of the windfall on capacity in place is 0.08 against a land-value gain of 3.80 at these dials. The windfall is real, bounded by the build window; the permanence belongs to the input no window can deliver.

The waterfall (T2, numerically verified; Figure 7). With two produced inputs in fixed proportions — build lags J = 1 and J = 5, think chips and power — composite capacity is frozen at the long input's inherited level until its first post-shock deliveries, and each input's rental is pinned by its own entry margin from its own lag onward. Excess rents therefore survive on an input exactly over its remaining build window: at the run's dials the short input's excess is gone after the first period, the long input holds an elevated rental through its window and returns to user cost on schedule, and the land rent settles at its permanently higher level when the long input arrives. Value passes down the ladder of build lags and stops at J = ∞. One indeterminacy belongs in the open: rentals inside the shortest window are a pure transfer among owners of sunk capacity — allocations and the rent path are invariant to how that window's excess is split, and the model does not determine the split.

Speed times lag (T3, numerically verified; Figure 8). The windfall on capacity in place scales with the speed of the capability change and with the gestation lag, monotonically in both across the full grid; the fast-shock, long-lag corner is roughly twenty times the slow, short one at these dials. Read as regimes: slow automation is Georgist — value accrues to land almost as it is released — while fast automation with long build times is Piketty for the duration of the crossing, a transient in exactly the sense that it is priced by the window.

E.6 The sloped path: which shock moves the wage

The sloped wage path (T5, a conjecture the numerics split; Figure 9). The conjectured scenario — capability arrives, capacity lags, the goods wage dips on impact and recovers as capacity is built while the land wage falls throughout — is the Korinek–Suh transition run inside this model, and the numerics decide it by shock type. Under frontier extension, the sloped analogue of a γ̄ fall — the schedule capped from above, new tasks becoming automatable without the old ones cheapening — the goods wage falls at the release (by 3 percent at the run's dials) and the cap then pins it: the buildout sweeps tasks at a constant margin, Caselli–Manning on the capped stretch, with no recovery phase; the land claim falls with the buildout and stays below its old value at every date. Under efficiency deepening — the whole schedule scaled down, already-automated tasks cheapening — the release-day move reverses: existing capacity stretches further, the margin advances at once, and the goods wage rises on impact. What survives of the conjecture at every configuration tested is its land half; the goods-wage dip is real but its recovery is not, and the sign of the release-day move is a diagnostic for which kind of capability arrived. The falsifiable statement stands: AI's wage effect in land units arrives with the buildout, not the model release.
