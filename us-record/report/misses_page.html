How bad are the misses?

None of the misses breaks the fit to the five series. Two of them shrink a lot once the data are read like for like: the waterline and the coverage ratio. The real one is the machine chain, about twice its measured size, and most of it has an easy fix. The main case runs on a 2% return, so depreciation takes almost all of capital's income. Fitted with the chain's measured size as a sixth target, the return comes out at 7.4%. NIPA's split of capital income implies about 8%. The chain then falls to 22% of pay (15–18% measured) and replacement to 22% of gross output (16%), and the five targets fit as well as before (2.02 against 2.00).

What the return cannot fix is the ceiling. The task line still sits on the machine chain's ceiling, so a machine's cost holds no fixed rents, against at least the 18% that is imported content in the record. Tested directly (section 3), a one-good model cannot leave the ceiling. Holding fixed rents at the record's floor raises the five targets' misfit from 2.0 to 8.5. The record's wages seem to need the fast clock that makes goods cheap, and in a model with one good that clock drives land out of every machine. Durables apart is the next test.

The misses, one line each

The machine chain

Mostly the return; the rest is structural

Three checks fail together because they are one fact. The fit matches θV, pay's share of a machine's cost, at about 0.49. It also puts the task line on the machine chain's ceiling from about 1970, where land's share of a machine's cost is exactly the distance to the ceiling, so it falls to almost nothing. Machine services then take the other 51%. With the required return at 2% and depreciation at 5.3%, 73% of the rent on machines is depreciation. NIPA puts consumption of fixed capital at 16% of GDP and the net operating surplus at 26%, so in the record depreciation is about 39% of capital's income.

The data are sound here. The chain's share is BEA's input–output tables traced through the full chain, and the model has one wage, so its hours share and pay share are the same thing. The record's pay share is 18% at US cost shares and 15% for domestic labour. θV, which the fit targets, is read on the domestic basis, so 15% is the stricter comparison.

The easy part of the extension is the return. The five targets alone barely mind it: the best fit with the return free is at 4.6% (1.90), and 2% to 7% all fit about as well. Walked up one number at a time, the fit stalls just above 5%. Fitted jointly with the chain's measured share as a sixth target (1997–2023, tolerance 0.02 like θV), the return comes out at 7.4% and the five targets' misfit at 2.02. The chain falls to 22% of pay in 2023 and replacement to 22% of gross output. NIPA's split implies about 8%: depreciation's rate times the surplus over depreciation. At a fixed 2%, the same sixth target costs the five targets dearly (6.45, mostly labour's share).

What the return does not touch is the ceiling. Every fit sits on it after about 1970, so fixed rents stay near zero in a machine's cost. On the domestic basis, the record's machine is 47% pay, 11% depreciation, 4% taxes, 19% surplus and 18% imported content. Calibration (c) counts imported content and the surplus above a normal return as fixed rents, about 0.3 in all. Raising the return also squeezes the site out of the basket: 0.2% of spending at 7.4%, against 1.4% at 2%.

Off the ceiling?

Not with one good

The forward model's calibration (c) reads fixed rents of about 0.3 in a machine's cost, so its multiplier, 1 + θV/σV, is near 2.6. The main case has 0.001 and a multiplier near 500. There the wage moves almost independently of capability, which is how the fit matches the wage paths while the task line barely moves.

To see whether the record allows anything else, the main case was refitted with a floor under fixed rents in 1997–2023, the return free. The five targets' misfit rises from 2.00 with no floor to 3.85 at 3%, 6.40 at 10% and 8.53 at the record's 18.5%. Both starts agree at every floor, and every series gets worse. Without a target on the chain's size, the return falls to zero and the chain grows to about half of all pay. With the chain's size and the band both as targets, eleven of fourteen starts reach one fit. There the five targets' misfit is 11.1 and the return 9.5%, and fixed rents still sink to 11% by 2023.

That fit is telling all the same. Everything about machines lines up with the record: the chain at 18% of pay, replacement at 17% of gross output (NIPA: 16%), and the waterline at 1.9-fold (broad equipment: 2.3-fold). What breaks is the wages, labour's share and employment. In one good, the facts about machines and the facts about pay cannot both hold.

So in a model with one good, the record's wages need the knife edge. The likely reason is that the clock that makes goods cheap must make every machine cheap in land. In the record, the fast progress sits in computers and durables: the waterline's computer measures and the durables wage show it.

The waterline

Mostly the data as used

The waterline is the production worker's wage over the price of machines, quality adjusted, from 1999. Its median mixes four computer-price measures (they rise 7.6 to 15.2-fold to 2023) with two broad-equipment measures (2.3-fold). The model's machine is every machine: equipment, software and structures. Broad equipment is the fair comparison. Against it the model's 1.25-fold is short by a factor of 1.9, not 6. The shortfall that remains has the same cause as the chain: with the task line on the ceiling, the marginal task hardly moves, and the wage over a unit of machine capability moves only as the marginal task does.

The coverage ratio

Partly the comparison, partly real

The report compared the record with the model's land rent on all land. The record counts housing land only: rent flows times a land share, or the Fed's household land values. In 1950 the main case's machines held 72% of the land, and 4% by 2024. On housing land the model's ratio rises 2.3-fold from 1953 to 2024; the report's "falls by a fifth" was the wrong basis.

The record's median rises 6.7-fold, but it leans on the Z.1 land-value members. These are capitalized at bond yields, swing sixfold within a decade, and are flagged unreliable after 1995 in the measure's own spec. The steady rent-flow member rises 4.3-fold. So the gap is 2.3 against 4.3. It is real and moderate: with land fixed and the basket's site share nearly flat, rent per person cannot grow as fast as recorded.

Housing's share of spending

A tension in a set value

The levels do not compare: housing in the record is structures and site, while the model's basket holds site alone. At a land share of a third to a half, site would be 4% to 8% of spending; the main case has 1.4%. The trend is informative. The record's share rises 44%; the model's site share 8%.

The trend comes from the basket's elasticity. B sets it at 0.875 from the share against shelter's price over durables'. Read against everything but shelter, the price B now uses for goods, the same regression gives 0.636. That steepens the site share's rise to 25% and costs the fit 14% (2.28). Cobb–Douglas, the other way, fits 5% better. The record's housing share wants goods and site to be complements; the five targets lean the other way. Choosing one price basis settles which reading the model takes.

Out of sample

Expected for a backward model

Fitted to 1950–1995 alone, the main case fits those years at 1.25 and misses 1996–2024 by 21.6, mostly on the wage over shelter and labour's share. Fitted to 1950–1985 the miss after is 16.2. The short fits run the clock at 10.6% to 14.7% a year against 6.8% with all years. The drivers' paces are not constant, and the record pins them loosely: with the wave, any steady pace from 2% to 9% a year is within 10% of the best fit. As an account of the record this is not fatal. It does mean the clock's pace is not yet a number to carry forward. Driving the clock from a measured series, broad equipment prices for one, would fix that and test the waterline in the same stroke.

What to try next

The machine chain is the miss that matters. Calibration (c)'s route has now been tried: the return fitted with the chain as a target works, but holding fixed rents at the record's level does not, in one good (section 3).

  1. Durables apart, first. A second good whose capability clock runs faster, served by the same machines and the same labour and land markets. The durables wage rises 6.2-fold against 2.0-fold for everything but shelter, and one good cannot carry both. If the fast progress lives in durables, the rest of the economy's machines can keep their fixed rents. The test of section 3 is then rerun on two goods.
  2. Calibration (c)'s targets, kept. The chain's share of pay and the band for fixed rents stay as checks, and become targets once a model can meet them.

Questions for you before building either:

  • For calibration (c): should the share of fixed rents be a target, with a band from the record, or set outright as calibration (c) sets it? Should the return be fitted with the chain as a sixth target, as here, or set from NIPA's split (about 8%)? And which basis for the chain and θ_V: domestic labour (15%, as θ_V is read now) or US cost shares (18%)?
  • For durables: two goods with their own clocks but one labour market and one land market, the basket holding both at constant utility? Or durables as a share of the goods basket with its own price index and no machines of its own?
