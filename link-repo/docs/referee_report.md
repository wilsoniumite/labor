# Referee Report: "The Link: Wages, machines, and what remains"

**Recommendation: major revisions.** The paper contains a genuinely interesting organizing idea, one elegant lemma, and an unusually honest empirical section. It also contains a central comparative static that ignores the paper's own general-equilibrium logic, dismisses in one paragraph the mechanism its principal sources exist to model, and states its policy results only in a limit whose distance from the present economy is never measured. I detail these below in descending order of severity. The author writes clearly, which makes the problems easy to locate; this is meant as a compliment and should be taken as one.

## Major comments

**1. The real-wage objection (the signature example assumes its conclusion).** The paper's emblematic calculation — machine improves, ρ falls from 5 to 2, wage falls from $50 to $20 — holds the machine rental c fixed while machine productivity rises. But c is endogenous by the paper's own Proposition 3, and technological progress that improves machines also cheapens them. Worse for the author: in the corner regime the final-good price is p = c·∫dx/γ_M, so the real product wage is w/p = ρ̄/∫(1/γ_M)dx — the c cancels, and uniform machine improvement *raises* the real wage even at full parity. If machines dominate humans uniformly, the real wage converges to the worker's absolute productivity valued at machine-era prices: bounded, not zero. This is the Caselli–Manning (2019) "robot arithmetic" point — cheaper machine-made goods cannot immiserate everyone in terms of machine-made goods — and the paper never confronts it. The collapse claim is rescuable, but only by being restated: wages collapse *as a share* of output, and the *absolute* real wage collapses only against the consumption components whose prices do not fall with γ_M — precisely the land-content components (housing, energy, food-acreage) into which Proposition 3 says value migrates. The subsistence constraint binds through the land-priced part of the bundle. This is, I suspect, what the author believes; it is not what the paper says, and the current example is rhetorically effective and analytically indefensible. Required: define the real wage against a bundle with non-produced content σ, prove the collapse for σ > 0, and concede the σ = 0 case to Caselli–Manning.

**2. Reinstatement is waved at, not answered.** The paper's fixed task space is defended in three lines of Section 9 while its two most-cited sources are the authors of the canonical model of endogenous new-task creation (Acemoglu–Restrepo 2018), in which reinstatement is a derived equilibrating force, not an assumption to discard. The paper's "reservoir" argument — that inventing new labor tasks presupposes latent human advantage, which is the link — is the right *shape* of an answer, but it is asserted, not modeled. Either (a) add a directed-task-creation margin and show the reservoir condition formally, or (b) present the fixed-task case explicitly as the pessimistic branch of a stated dichotomy, with the empirical displacement-vs-reinstatement balance since 1987 as the reason for taking that branch seriously. Currently the paper does neither and will be dismissed by exactly the readers it most wants.

**3. Everything of policy relevance is a limit theorem, and the limit's distance is unmeasured.** Propositions 4 and 5 hold in the corner regime; the paper concedes that in the linked regime "standard redistribution works roughly as advertised." So the policy payload is conditional on a regime the economy has never occupied, and the paper offers no metric for proximity, no rate, and no finite-elasticity versions of the results. The shell-game identity in 5(i) should be restated with finite labor-demand elasticity — pass-through and partial futility as continuous functions of the schedule's slope — so the reader can see how much of the theorem survives at, say, present elasticities. Same for the 4(ii) leak. Limits are fine; limits with no continuity statement invite the suspicion that the results are knife-edge.

**4. The positioning vacuum, and one substantive omission inside it.** There is no related-literature discussion. Korinek–Stiglitz appears only as a floor-resolution footnote; Korinek–Suh (2024), Susskind's technological-unemployment model, Sachs–Kotlikoff, Moll–Rachel–Restrepo (2022) on automation and the returns to wealth, and Caselli–Manning are absent. Most substantively: Aghion–Jones–Jones's Baumol argument — if any tasks remain human-essential, their expenditure share can grow without bound and labor's aggregate share can persist — is a direct counterforce to the corner regime and deserves an answer, not silence. The paper's human-premium discussion gestures at this but treats it as a taste question; Baumol makes it a cost-structure question, which is harder to dismiss.

**5. Ideas at zero price proves too much.** "Any positive price for an idea is institutional scarcity, chosen rather than natural" is Romer's problem stated as if it were a solution. At marginal-cost pricing, ideas with fixed creation costs are not produced at all; the institutional scarcity is constitutive of the idea sector's existence, not an optional distortion. The paper's conclusion (IP rents are policy-elective) survives, but the sentence as written will read as naive to anyone who teaches growth.

**6. "Necessity" equivocates.** Proposition 5(iii) concludes that funding from rents on non-produced factors is "the only column with anything in it," but the paper's own taxonomy leaves three columns non-empty in the corner: physical rents, institutional rents, and (temporarily) transition rents, with VAT as an indirect instrument on the first. The necessity claim is therefore base-*class* necessity, within which the LVT is distinguished — uniquely non-contractible base, per 5(ii) — rather than instrument necessity. Say that. As written, the proposition claims more than its proof delivers.

**7. The empirics test motivation, not propositions.** Section 7 is the paper's most careful work, and the rule-grid honesty is commendable. But nothing in it tests Propositions 1–5; it measures the exposure the propositions make alarming. The declining λ_C series is directionally consistent with the mechanism and nothing more. Relatedly, the funding attribution behind the four-way split is incidence by pro-rata assumption; the paper says so once and then labels a channel "transfers from ownership taxes" as though the assumption were a finding. Keep the measurements; lower the labels' confidence by one notch; and say plainly which predictions in Section 8 constitute the model's actual test surface.

## Minor comments

- **Figure 1 is internally inconsistent.** The axis says tasks are "ordered by effective human edge ρ̃(x)," but the plotted ρ̃ is non-monotone (the wedge pockets dip). If ordered by ρ̃ it cannot dip; the figure is in fact ordered by raw ρ. Fix the label and add one line noting the relabeling used for the threshold argument.
- Proposition 1's proof invokes continuity at the margin; state the (relabeled) monotonicity and continuity of ρ̃ as an assumption.
- The rationing convention (participation evaluated at the base wage) is fine but should cite the queue logic where it is introduced, not in Section 9.
- LVT invariance should acknowledge the assessment problem (separating site value from improvements) and the collateral effects of capitalizing the tax into land prices; these are the standard practical objections and their absence reads as unfamiliarity.
- Predictions 5, 8, and 9 lack magnitudes or horizons; 9 is a political-economy claim in an economics list. Sharpen or relabel.
- Figure 3's "machine parity = 18" line has no derivation in the example's numbers; give it one or drop the number.
- The purchased-commons and "George resumed" sentences are effective rhetoric; a hostile reader will use them to classify the paper. Consider fencing rhetoric into the introduction and conclusion.

## Summary for the author

The deflation lemma is genuinely nice; the stratigraphy-plus-targeting synthesis is a real contribution of framing; the U-shape postdiction is well used; the measurement section's band discipline is better practice than most published work in this area. But the paper currently leads with an example that its own model contradicts (Major 1), waves off the strongest objection (Major 2), and proves its headline policy results only where it cannot be checked (Major 3). All three are fixable, and the fixed paper is considerably stronger than the current one — which is the only reason this report is as long as it is.
