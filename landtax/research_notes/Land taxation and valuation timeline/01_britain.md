# Britain (mainly England; Scotland and Wales where they differ): land and property taxation and the valuations behind it, 1692 to 4 November 2026

How to read these notes
- Confidence labels on key claims: **[P]** = confirmed in a primary source (statute or explanatory notes, Hansard, tax or valuation agency publication, archival catalogue, official statement). **[S]** = secondary source only (journalism, think tank, local history, or a search-result snippet I could not open). **[U]** = unresolved or unverified in this session. I give it as a lead, not a finding.
- Research conditions: the shared web-search budget ran out partway through the work. After that I could only open URLs I already had or that sit on official sites (historic Hansard, legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, gov.wales, parliament.scot). Several key sites returned HTTP 403 (House of Commons Library, publications.parliament.uk, irishstatutebook.ie, ifs.org.uk). As a result, some well-known facts (especially on the 18th-century land tax, 19th-century rating law, the Uthwatt Report and the dates when the 1947 and 1976 taxes started and ended) are listed under **Gaps** as unverified, not under findings. See the Access log at the end.
- Valuation is the thread through every section. For each tax the notes ask: what was valued (land alone, or land plus buildings), on what basis (area, annual rent, capital or sale value, "development value"), by whom, how often values were updated in law and in practice, and what happened when the values aged, were frozen, were challenged in court or were abandoned.

## 1. Merge-ready dated timeline (1692 to 4 November 2026)

### Takeaway
Over 330 years Britain tried several land or property value taxes. The 1692 land tax froze into fixed quotas. The 1909–10 land value duties were backed by a full national valuation and repealed in 1920. Snowden's 1931 land value tax was never levied. Three post-war betterment taxes were abolished by the next government. Domestic property taxation itself kept outliving its valuations: the last domestic rating revaluation in England and Wales was in 1973, council tax in England and Scotland still uses 1991 values, and in October 2026 Wales postponed again a revaluation it had written into law.

### Inferences
- The same pattern recurs: the valuation is set up, values age, and governments find revaluing politically costly (1692 quotas, the 1973 rating lists, council tax since 1991, Wales in 2026). The exceptions are non-domestic rates, which have been revalued fairly regularly since 1990, though with postponements, and the planned five-yearly HVCTS revaluation.
- Twentieth-century land value taxes died twice of administration and law (1909–20 through court rulings and cost, and the 1931 tax suspended before valuation began) and three times of changes of government (1947/53, 1967/71, 1976/85).

### Gaps
- The exact dates of several transitions could not be confirmed from primary text this session: community charge start in England and Wales (widely given as 1 April 1990), abolition of the development charge (widely given as November 1952, with the Town and Country Planning Act 1953), DLT start (widely given as 1 August 1976) and DLT abolition (widely given as disposals from 19 March 1985). All are **[U]**. See section Gaps below.

## 2. The 1692 Land Tax: first assessment, fixed quotas, stale values, Pitt's redemption (1798), compulsory redemption and abolition (1963)

### Takeaway
The land tax started in 1692 as a tax on assessed annual values of land, personal estate and offices. From 1698 each county and borough had a fixed quota, so the 1690s valuations froze and became irrelevant to the total. In 1798 Pitt made the charge perpetual and redeemable for a lump sum. Unredeemed land tax was made compulsorily redeemable from 1950 and abolished on 25 March 1963. In effect, a valuation from the 1690s stayed the legal basis of a charge for about 270 years.

### Cited Findings
**What was taxed and how it was first assessed**
- The first assessments (1692–3) were made under "An Act for granting to their Majesties an aid of four shillings in the pound for one year" (4 W. & M. c.1). It reached "real estate and personal property", and named local commissioners for every English and Welsh borough and county to oversee assessment and collection. **[P]** — [LMA catalogue via AIM25](https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/land-tax-commissioners-6). The same catalogue entry says the local officials for each county were listed in the annual Acts until 1798. The extraction tool rendered them as "assessors"; historically the Acts named commissioners, who appointed assessors, so this detail is **[U]**.
- Bedfordshire Archives describes it as "a new tax on personal estate, public offices, and land to raise money for the war against France". **[P, county archive]** — [Bedfordshire Archives](https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/Using_Our_Collections/Collection-Guides/Maps-and-Land-Records/LandTaxRecords.aspx)
- TNA describes the land tax as "based on a 1692 valuation and voted annually until 1798". **[P]** — [TNA C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030)

**How it hardened into quotas**
- The tax was levied "as a pound rate between 1693 and 1696". In 1697 it was a four-shilling assessment with a poll tax. "From 1698 to 1798 each county or borough was given a fixed sum to collect." **[P]** — [AIM25/LMA](https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/land-tax-commissioners-6)
- Plain meaning: Parliament still voted a nominal rate (so many shillings in the pound), but each locality's total was fixed. Local commissioners and assessors shared out that fixed sum among owners. Revaluing land therefore changed nothing for the Exchequer. (Interpretation of the AIM25 description.)

**How stale the assessments became**
- Assessments recorded each parish's annual rentals, owner and occupier names and the sum assessed. Bedfordshire Archives notes that the annual rentals were frozen at 1690s levels or often left out entirely. Property descriptions appear only from about 1825, and occupiers are rarely recorded before 1772. **[P, county archive]** — [Bedfordshire Archives](https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/Using_Our_Collections/Collection-Guides/Maps-and-Land-Records/LandTaxRecords.aspx)
- From 1780 to 1832 paying land tax on freehold was evidence of the county voting qualification, so Clerks of the Peace kept duplicates. **[P]** — [Bedfordshire Archives](https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/Using_Our_Collections/Collection-Guides/Maps-and-Land-Records/LandTaxRecords.aspx)

**Pitt's 1798 redemption scheme**
- In 1798 "the amount voted for that year was made a perpetual charge, subject to redemption or purchase". **[P]** — [TNA C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030)
- "An Act of 1798 allowed property to be exonerated (i.e. freed from tax) by payment of a lump sum." After this, assessment books split the sums into "exonerated" and "not exonerated". After 1832 exonerated property no longer had to be listed, so later assessments cover a shrinking share of owners. **[P]** — [Bedfordshire Archives](https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/Using_Our_Collections/Collection-Guides/Maps-and-Land-Records/LandTaxRecords.aspx)
- From 1806, Acts allowed land tax to be removed from property of ecclesiastical and charitable bodies with incomes under £150 a year, up to £6,000 a year in total. **[P]** — [TNA C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030)
- Redemption terms varied over time and "by 1950 stood at twenty-five times the annual assessment". **[P]** — [TNA C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030)

**When and why it ended**
- "The Finance Act 1949 introduced compulsory redemption in certain circumstances", from 1 April 1950, for example when property changed hands. **[P]** — [TNA C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030). Bedfordshire's local records "cease in 1949 when compulsory redemption was introduced prior to the abolition of the tax in 1963". **[P]** — [Bedfordshire Archives](https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/Using_Our_Collections/Collection-Guides/Maps-and-Land-Records/LandTaxRecords.aspx)
- "The Finance Act 1963 abolished all unredeemed land tax with effect from 25 March of that year." **[P]** — [TNA C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030)

**Records**
- Land Tax Redemption Office records run 1798–1964: registers (IR 8), deeds (IR 20), exonerations (IR 21), parish books (IR 22), quotas and assessments (IR 23), redemption certificates (IR 24), miscellanea (IR 25). **[P]** — [TNA C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030). IR 23 is described as covering 1798–1914. **[S, search snippet of TNA catalogue C9361]** — [TNA C9361](https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C9361)

**Per-tax summary (land tax)**
- What was taxed: land (on assessed annual rental value), plus personal estate and public offices at first. **[P]**
- Who valued: local assessors under commissioners named in each annual Act. **[P]**
- How often values were updated: in law, assessments were annual until the quota system (1698). In practice the rentals were frozen at 1690s levels. **[P]**
- Rate: 4s in the pound (a nominal 20%) in 1692. Later rates and yields were not verified this session. **[P for 1692; U for later]**
- What happened to the valuations: they became irrelevant to the national total once quotas were fixed. The charge then hardened into a perpetual, redeemable, debt-like burden on particular parcels until compulsory redemption (1950) and abolition (1963). **[P]**

### Inferences
- The land tax is the textbook case of an apportioned (quota) tax. Fixing each county's total took the pressure off revaluation, and local distributions froze. That made it possible in 1798 to treat the tax as a fixed annuity on each property and sell it back to owners. That treatment is itself evidence that the charge no longer followed value.
- Anyone who had not redeemed by 1963 was still paying a charge rooted in a 1690s assessment, about 270 years old. This is an inference from the 1692 and 1963 dates.

### Gaps
- **[U] Not verified this session:** yields (e.g. the often-quoted figure of about £2 million a year at 4s in the 1690s, or roughly £0.5 million per shilling of rate); the size and distribution of county quotas, including the claim that northern and western counties were under-assessed relative to the south-east; the price terms of Pitt's 1798 redemption (widely described as payable by transferring government 3% stock yielding more than the tax); the land tax's share of total revenue over time; and Scotland's separate "cess". Standard sources are W. R. Ward, *The English Land Tax in the Eighteenth Century* (Oxford University Press, 1953) and J. V. Beckett, "Land Tax or Excise: the levying of taxation in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century England", *English Historical Review* 100 (1985). Neither was consulted (no search budget left; likely paywalled).
- The TNA research guide on land tax (if one exists separately) and the archived TNA talk "The land tax 1692–1963" could not be opened (redirect to the UK Government Web Archive, then 403).

## 3. Local rates: how "rateable value" was assessed, by whom, and how often revaluations really happened (19th century to 1990)

### Takeaway
Rates were a tax on occupiers based on the annual rental value of land and buildings together, not on land alone. By the 1970s valuation officers of the Inland Revenue prepared the rating lists in England and Wales, using open-market rental value under the General Rate Act 1967. In practice revaluations were irregular. The 1973 revaluation came about ten years after the previous one, and I found no evidence of a later domestic revaluation in England and Wales before domestic rates were abolished. Much of the 19th-century and inter-war legal history could not be verified this session.

### Cited Findings
- **Who valued and on what basis (1970s):** the 1973 revaluation was carried out by "the valuation officers of the Inland Revenue Department". They had to assess each property on its open market rental value under section 19 of the General Rate Act 1967, and were "now revaluing all properties for 1st April, 1973". The minister said the Secretary of State "has no power to intervene" in individual valuations. **[P]** — [HC Deb 24 Nov 1971](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1971/nov/24/rate-assessments-revaluation)
- **Frequency in practice:** in February 1973 the minister said the lists were prepared "in accordance with the provisions of the General Rate Act 1967". He contrasted this with the previous government, under which revaluation "had not been postponed for 10 years", implying the previous revaluation was in about 1963. **[P]** — [HC Deb 14 Feb 1973](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1973/feb/14/rating-revaluation)
- **Effects of revaluation:** in the same exchange a Labour MP claimed the 1973 revaluation implied industrial "derating" worth about £250,000 to Ford, with the shortfall falling on domestic ratepayers in Havering and Barking. The minister said "Material assistance has been given to mitigate the pressure on domestic ratepayers". **[P, as claims made in debate]** — [HC Deb 14 Feb 1973](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1973/feb/14/rating-revaluation)
- **1950s revaluation:** in April 1955 ministers could not yet assess the effects of the revaluation under the Valuation for Rating Act 1953 and promised changes once they were known. **[P]** — [HC Deb 5 April 1955](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1955/apr/05/valuation-for-rating-revaluation)
- **The rental-evidence problem:** in 1981 an MP said rateable values were "based on hypothetical rental evidence", which produced "inconsistencies". This was the period when few houses were let on the open market. **[P, as claim in debate]** — [HC Deb 1 June 1981](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1981/jun/01/domestic-rates)
- **Scotland's different machinery:** in Scotland one official, "the assessor, the valuer and the electoral registration officer", compiled valuation rolls. Under the poll tax the same office also registered people for the community charge. **[P]** — [HL Deb 9 April 1987](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1987/apr/09/abolition-of-domestic-rates-etc-scotland)
- **Rental data underneath the 1910 survey:** the 1910 Valuation Office began from copies of Income Tax Schedule A registers (tax on income from ownership of property), bound as "Domesday Books". These recorded occupiers, owners, descriptions and **rateable values**, showing that rental-value assessments for rates and income tax were the existing valuation base in 1910. **[P, local authority archive]** — [Sheffield Archives](https://www.sheffield.gov.uk/sites/default/files/docs/libraries-and-archives/archives-and-local-studies/collections/Land%20Valuation%20Records%2C%201910.pdf)

**Per-tax summary (rates)**
- What was taxed: occupation of "hereditaments" (land and buildings together), by annual rental value. **[P for 1971 basis]**
- Who valued: by the 1970s, Inland Revenue valuation officers in England and Wales; in Scotland, local assessors. **[P]**
- How often: a five-year cycle in law is widely described for the 20th century **[U]**. In practice revaluations took effect in 1973 (after about ten years) and, per the 1955 debate, in the mid-1950s **[P]**. I found no later domestic revaluation in England and Wales before 1990 **[U: absence not proven]**.

### Inferences
- Because rates taxed land and buildings together, 20th-century land value campaigners sought "site value rating": rating land values alone. Snowden explicitly hoped his 1931 valuation might later let local rating shift from buildings and improvements to site values. **[P for Snowden's statement]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1)
- As private renting of houses shrank, valuers had to infer "hypothetical" rents. The rental basis therefore became less observable even before it became politically frozen.

### Gaps
**[U] Unverified background (needs a source before use):**
- Poor Relief Act 1601 (43 Eliz. I c.2) as the origin of parish rates on occupiers.
- Parochial Assessments Act 1836: defined rateable value as net annual value, the rent a tenant might reasonably pay from year to year less repairs, insurance and maintenance.
- Union Assessment Committee Act 1862: assessment committees for poor law unions.
- Valuation (Metropolis) Act 1869: quinquennial valuations in London.
- Rating and Valuation Act 1925: quinquennial valuation lists across England and Wales by rating authorities. Lists usually dated 1929 and 1934; the 1939 revaluation abandoned in wartime.
- Local Government Act 1948: valuation for rating transferred to the Inland Revenue's Valuation Office.
- 1956 lists: dwellings valued at 1939 rental levels under the Valuation for Rating Act 1953.
- Revaluations of 1963 and 1973; a further revaluation postponed and never carried out for domestic property in England and Wales.
- Scotland: Lands Valuation (Scotland) Act 1854 (annual valuation rolls by assessors); revaluations in 1961, 1966, 1971, 1978 and 1985. The 1985 Scottish revaluation is widely cited as the political trigger for the poll tax.
- None of these could be verified this session. Likely primary sources: Layfield Committee report (Cmnd 6453, 1976); Green Papers *Alternatives to Domestic Rates* (Cmnd 8449, 1981) and *Paying for Local Government* (Cmnd 9714, 1986); Lyons Inquiry final report (2007).

## 4. The 1909–10 "People's Budget" land value duties and the national valuation of 1910–15

### Takeaway
Lloyd George's land value duties needed a valuation of every parcel in the United Kingdom as at 30 April 1909, separating the value of the bare site from buildings and owners' improvements. The Valuation Office sent out more than 10 million owners' returns (Form 4). By September 1915 it had made about 7.8 million provisional valuations, with a staff that peaked at 4,882. Court defeats in 1913–14 (Lumsden; Norton Malreward and Chells) undermined how site value was calculated. The three main duties yielded about £1.34 million in total before repeal in 1920; the cost of the valuation was never officially stated. The valuation survives as one of the richest property records in British archives: 95,579 field books in IR 58 plus annotated Ordnance Survey maps.

### Cited Findings
**The duties (what was taxed, at what rate)**
- **Increment Value Duty (IVD):** "one pound for every full five pounds" of increment value, i.e. 20% of the rise in site value, charged on transfer, on grant of a lease and on death. **[P, as moved in the 1909 Budget resolutions]** — [HC Deb 11 May 1909](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1909/may/11/land-value-duties). TNA explains the aim as taxing the part of capital appreciation attributable to the site itself, excluding crops, buildings and improvements paid for by owners. A second site value was to be taken on a later sale, lease grant, transfer or the owner's death. **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)
- **Reversion Duty:** £1 for every £10 (10%) of the benefit accruing to a lessor when a lease ends. **[P, resolution]** — [HC Deb 11 May 1909](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1909/may/11/land-value-duties)
- **Undeveloped Land Duty (ULD):** an annual ½d in the pound on the capital site value of undeveloped land (about 0.2% a year). An MP calculated that land worth £500 an acre would pay "£1 0s. 10d." a year. Opposition frontbencher Pretyman understood that land valued under "£50 per acre" would not be liable (his understanding, not confirmed text). **[P, resolution; exemption U]** — [HC Deb 11 May 1909](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1909/may/11/land-value-duties)
- **Minerals:** the 1909 resolution applied the same ½d rate to the capital value of ungotten minerals **[P]** — [HC Deb 11 May 1909](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1909/may/11/land-value-duties). The enacted Mineral Rights Duty (on rental value of mineral rights; widely given as 1s in the pound) is **[U]**: the statute text could not be opened.
- **Valuation date ("datum line"):** 30 April 1909. **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/); [HC Deb 11 May 1909](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1909/may/11/land-value-duties)
- **Statute:** Finance (1909–10) Act 1910 (10 Edw. VII c.8). Its section 26(1) provided for the valuation. **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/). Passed on 29 April 1910. **[P]** — [HC Deb 30 April 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/apr/30/land-duties)
- **Expected yield:** the duties were reportedly designed to raise "just £½ million" in the first year. **[S, Journal of Liberal History article, search snippet only]** — [Douglas, Journal of Liberal History](https://liberalhistory.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/73_Douglas_Lloyd_George_land_taxes.pdf)

**How the valuation was done and by whom**
- The Inland Revenue set up a Valuation Office in 1910 (England and Wales) and 1911 (Scotland), with 118 valuation districts in England and Wales, each under a district valuer. The survey was organised by income tax parish. **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/). The TNA's own table of plan series lists more district entries than 118, which the guide does not reconcile **[U]**.
- First step: district valuers received copies of the Income Tax Schedule A registers, bound as "Domesday Books" (owner, occupier, description, rateable value). **[P]** — [Sheffield Archives](https://www.sheffield.gov.uk/sites/default/files/docs/libraries-and-archives/archives-and-local-studies/collections/Land%20Valuation%20Records%2C%201910.pdf)
- **Form 4-Land** was "filled in by landowners", and its information was transferred into field books. **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/). "Over 10,000,000 forms for owners were sent out and 9,600,000 had been returned" by 31 March 1912. **[P, Labour MP Myers citing official figures]** — [HC Deb 14 July 1920, c2429](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties)
- **Provisional valuations:** **Form 37-Land** recorded the provisional valuation and was kept by the district office; **Form 36-Land** was the copy sent to the owner. **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)
- **Field books** (IR 58) record owner, occupier, owner's interest, tenancy and rent, area, and often the date of erection, rooms, state of repair and sketch plans. Valuation figures normally include "the market value of fee simple of the whole property and the market value of the site divested of structures, timber and plants". **[P]** — [Sheffield Archives](https://www.sheffield.gov.uk/sites/default/files/docs/libraries-and-archives/archives-and-local-studies/collections/Land%20Valuation%20Records%2C%201910.pdf); [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)
- **Maps:** Ordnance Survey sheets annotated with assessment (hereditament) numbers. About 82% are at 1:2,500, with some towns at 1:1,250 and 1:500, and London at 1:1,056. Working plans are in local archives; record plans are at TNA (IR 121 London to IR 135 Northern). **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)
- **Staffing:** 1911: "940 valuers and 488 clerks, a total of 1,428"; 31 March 1912: 2,829 **[P]** — [HC Deb 14 July 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties). August 1914: 4,882; November 1915: 1,983; May 1920: 1,292 **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/may/04/reduction-of-staff-land-valuation)
- **Output:** by 30 September 1915, 7,784,000 provisional valuations covering 56,000,000 acres with a total value of £5,200,067,000 (Great Britain). **[P]** — [HC Deb 14 July 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties). The original valuation was completed in autumn 1915. **[P]** — [Sheffield Archives](https://www.sheffield.gov.uk/sites/default/files/docs/libraries-and-archives/archives-and-local-studies/collections/Land%20Valuation%20Records%2C%201910.pdf)
- **Cost:** in 1931 the Treasury said "I cannot give the cost of collection", because Inland Revenue costs could not be separated **[P]** — [HC Deb 30 April 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/apr/30/land-duties). A backbench MP said in 1920 "we have spent something like £2,000,000 on preparing this valuation" **[P as a claim; not an official figure]** — [HC Deb 14 July 1920, c2433](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties). By 1920 the land duties work was "estimated to cost £6,500 out of a total cost of £468,000" for the department, most of whose work was by then other valuation **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/may/04/reduction-of-staff-land-valuation). Works in Progress (2025) says administration cost four times the tax income but gives no source **[S, unsourced; lead only]** — [Watling, Works in Progress](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/the-failure-of-the-land-value-tax)

**Court rulings that undermined the valuation**
- **Lumsden (increment value duty on a builder's sale):** in 1913 the Treasury said the "original site value as finally adopted is £105" and that the site value on the sale was "called in question in proceedings now pending" **[P]** — [HC Deb 15 July 1913 (WA)](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1913/jul/15/site-value-case-of-mr-r-j-lumsden). In July 1914 the House of Lords was "equally divided", so the Crown's earlier wins before the Revenue judge and Court of Appeal stood. An MP complained that duty "is still demandable" even where the bare site's value had not risen. The government promised a relieving clause, but its Revenue Bill was dropped **[P]** — [HC Deb 23 July 1914](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1914/jul/23/land-taxes-lumsden-case). A law-report digest lists Haldane LC and Lord Shaw for the Crown and Lords Moulton and Parmoor dissenting **[S]** — [swarb.co.uk](https://swarb.co.uk/?p=474143)
- **Norton Malreward and Chells (valuation of agricultural land):** Mr Justice Scrutton decided "points of principle ... against the Crown" on 28 February 1914. "The service of valuations and assessments to Undeveloped Land Duty on agricultural properties" affected was "suspended", and an appeal was entered in the Norton Malreward case **[P]** — [HC Deb 21 April 1914](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1914/apr/21/valuation-of-agricultural-hereditaments)
- In 1920 Chamberlain said arrears could not be collected or assessed "owing to adverse decisions in the Courts and for other reasons". A backbencher spoke of valuations "declared by the Courts to have been made on a wrong basis" **[P]** — [HC Deb 19 April 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/apr/19/land-values-duties); [HC Deb 14 July 1920, c2433](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties)

**Revenue raised against cost**
- Total yields from imposition to repeal: IVD £648,000; Reversion Duty £279,000; ULD £412,000 (together £1,339,000). Mineral Rights Duty, which was not repealed, had a net receipt of £5,816,000 up to 31 March 1931. **[P]** — [HC Deb 30 April 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/apr/30/land-duties)
- **Sources disagree:** the digitised Hansard of 14 July 1920 has Chamberlain saying "The total amount which is actualy paid is £21,329,000" (spelling as printed), with arrears of £541,000, and agreeing that remissions plus arrears were "about" £22,000,000 **[P as printed]** — [HC Deb 14 July 1920, c2441–2](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties). This cannot be reconciled with the 1931 written answer (£1.34 million in total) or with Chamberlain's own April 1920 statement that the duties "produced hardly any revenue" **[P]** — [HC Deb 19 April 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/apr/19/land-values-duties). **[U]** Possibly a transcription error (a figure of £1,329,000 would fit the 1931 total), but I could not check the printed Hansard.
- Arrears in 1920: assessed and uncollected £541,000 (Chamberlain) versus £520,000 (ULD £320,000; IVD £120,000; Reversion £80,000) plus "not less than £500,000" of unassessed reversion duty (Myers). **[P, two figures in the same debate]** — [HC Deb 14 July 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties)

**Repeal (1920) and refunds**
- Announcing repeal on 19 April 1920, Chamberlain said "these duties in their present form are unworkable" and "have produced hardly any revenue". Collecting arrears was "clearly impracticable". Mineral Rights Duty was kept as "a simple tax, offering no practical difficulties of administration". He hoped taxpayers would "let bygones be bygones" over small refunds. **[P]** — [HC Deb 19 April 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/apr/19/land-values-duties)
- Finance Act 1920 (10 & 11 Geo. V c.18) repealed IVD (and the Reversion and Undeveloped Land duties); the Valuation Office continued, and its successor is the Valuation Office Agency **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/); [HC Deb 30 April 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/apr/30/land-duties). Repayments under section 57 reached £228,591 by 16 October 1920, with no income tax deducted and no interest paid **[P]** — [HC Deb 27 Oct 1920 (WA)](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1920/oct/27/land-values-duties-repayments)
- **Lead-only source disagrees on date:** Works in Progress says Lloyd George repealed the taxes "as prime minister in 1922". Primary sources date the repeal to the Finance Act 1920, when Lloyd George was Prime Minister. **[P vs S]** — [Watling, Works in Progress](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/the-failure-of-the-land-value-tax)

**What survives**
- IR 58 field books: 95,579 volumes, arranged by district valuation office and income tax parish. Southampton district and Isle of Wight books were destroyed by enemy action in the Second World War, and some Basildon books by fire **[P]** — [TNA catalogue C9396](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C9396). Each field book has space for 100 plots **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)
- Records known not to survive, mainly because of wartime bombing: Basildon, Chelmsford, Liverpool, Birkenhead and most of the Wirral, Coventry, Portsmouth, Southampton, Winchester **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)
- Survey forms "were not systematically retained". No Form 37s are at TNA; surviving sets are in local record offices or district valuation offices. Examples of Form 4-Land survive in other departments' files (ADM 116/1279, F 6/16, RAIL 1057/1714). An older (1987) TNA guide reportedly said no Form 4 sets survive **[P for current guide; S for 1987 statement]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/); [genguide summary](https://www.genguide.co.uk/source/valuation-office-field-books-maps-land-property/)
- Scotland's equivalent records (Inland Revenue Survey maps and field books) are at National Records of Scotland **[S, search result only]** — [NRS guide](https://nrscotland.gov.uk/research/guides/inland-revenue-survey-maps-and-field-books)

### Inferences
- The fieldwork largely succeeded (about 7.8 million provisional valuations by 1915), but the tax design depended on legal definitions of "site value" that the courts read against the Crown. The weakest point was the law: how to strip out buildings, timber and owners' improvements, especially on farmland. The valuers' capacity was less of a problem.
- The war (staff cut from 4,882 to 1,983 between August 1914 and November 1915) and the 1914 decision to drop the Revenue Bill left the legal defects unrepaired. By 1920 the 1909 base values were also more than a decade old and predated wartime inflation (an inference; no source checked on 1909–20 land price movements).
- Many writers summarise the "cost against yield" as a failure, but the only firm official numbers are the yields (£1.34 million for the three repealed duties). The often-quoted cost of about £2 million came from a backbench MP. Treat any precise cost-to-yield ratio as **[U]**.

### Gaps
- **Statutory definitions not verified [U]:** standard accounts describe four values: *gross value* (market value of the freehold as it stands); *full site value* (gross value less the value of buildings, timber, fruit trees and crops); *total value* (gross value less fixed charges such as tithe and rentcharges); and *assessable site value* (total value less buildings and owner-made improvements). Assessable site value was the base for IVD and ULD. The statute text (s.25 Finance (1909–10) Act 1910) could not be opened (irishstatutebook.ie returned 403).
- Form 4: exact issue dates, the return deadline and the penalty for non-return were not verified.
- Outcome of the Norton Malreward appeal; exact citation of *Lumsden* (House of Lords, July 1914).
- Share of total tax revenue: not found. Mineral Rights Duty's later history (when abolished) not found.
- Scholarly accounts not consulted: Avner Offer, *Property and Politics 1870–1914* (Cambridge University Press, 1981); Bruce K. Murray, *The People's Budget 1909/10: Lloyd George and Liberal Politics* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1980); Brian Short, *Land and Society in Edwardian Britain* (Cambridge University Press, 1997) and *The Geography of England and Wales in 1910: an evaluation of Lloyd George's 'Domesday' of landownership* (Historical Geography Research Series 22, 1989).

## 5. Philip Snowden's 1931 land value tax

### Takeaway
The Finance Act 1931 (Part III) legislated a tax of 1d in the pound a year on the capital value of land in Great Britain, valued as if bare of buildings. It was to be charged from 1933–34 after a two-year valuation of 10–12 million units. After the 1931 crisis the National Government suspended it in early 1932 before any tax was levied, and the Finance Act 1934 repealed it. One survival was the duty to produce sale and lease documents to the Revenue (section 28), which the government kept because sale-price information was "of first importance in saving public money".

### Cited Findings
- **Rate and base:** "one penny for each pound of the land value of every unit of land in Great Britain". Land value means the price a purchaser would pay if existing buildings and works (with exceptions) were absent, i.e. valued as a vacant plot. **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1). In the Lords the base was described as what "the fee simple ... with vacant possession might have been expected to realise upon a sale in the open market on the valuation date" **[S, search snippet of HL Deb 14 July 1931]** — [HL Deb 14 July 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1931/jul/14/finance-bill)
- **Planned valuation:** values "as from 1 August 1931", to stay in force for five years, with revaluations "as from 1 August 1936" and every five years after. About 10–12 million hereditaments, taking about two years, at an estimated £1,000,000–£1,500,000 over three financial years (perhaps £300,000–£400,000 in the first). Work could not effectively begin until about October 1931. **[P, as proposed by Snowden]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1). Whether the enacted valuation date stayed at 1 August 1931 is **[U]**.
- **First charge:** 1933–34. Snowden said it was "quite impossible to give even an approximate estimate" of yield until the valuation had progressed. **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1)
- **Exemptions and reliefs:**
  - agricultural land, so far as its value did not exceed agricultural value;
  - churches, churchyards and burial grounds;
  - National Trust land, local authority land, hospitals and almshouses, and statutory utilities' inalienable land;
  - minerals;
  - individuals owing 10s or less a year (about £120 of capital value), which Snowden said would cover "practically all working-class dwellings".
  **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1)
- **Who pays and how objections work:** the owner-occupier paid; a lessee paid if the lease ran more than 50 years; otherwise the freeholder paid. Objections went to referees appointed under the 1910 Act. A register of values would be open to inspection at local authorities. **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1)
- **Relation to 1909 and to rates:** Snowden called the scheme "more simple and more general" than 1909–10. He hoped the valuation could later support shifting local rates onto site values, and said the tax would help offset the roughly £35,000,000 a year de-rating subsidy. **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1)
- **Suspension:** "the proposals of Lord Snowden were suspended in the early part of 1932", with valuation and tax suspended "until Parliament should fix a date for their coming into operation". Neville Chamberlain called the tax "useless lumber" with "not the slightest prospect of this Parliament naming a date". **[P]** — [HC Deb 5 June 1934](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1934/jun/05/clause-25-repeal-of-land-value-tax). An opposition MP (Mr Maclean) said the Cabinet decided in May 1933 not to include a clause bringing the tax into operation in that year's Finance Bill **[P, as claim]** — same source.
- **Repeal:** clause 25 of the 1934 Finance Bill ("Repeal of Land Value Tax") was agreed in committee 232 to 56 on 5 June 1934. Section 28 of the 1931 Act was kept. It required the transferor or lessee to produce the instrument to the Commissioners on a sale of the freehold, a lease of seven years or more, or a sale of such a lease. Chamberlain said this information was "of first importance in saving public money". **[P]** — [HC Deb 5 June 1934](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1934/jun/05/clause-25-repeal-of-land-value-tax)
- **A vivid figure from the repeal debate:** an MP cited the Becontree estate, where land valued at £7,000 for rating sold for £295,000 **[P, as a claim in debate]** — same source.
- **Snowden's principle**, as quoted in 1934 from his Third Reading speech of 3 July 1931: to "assert the right of the community to the ownership of the land" **[P, as quoted]** — same source.

**Per-tax summary (1931 tax)**
- What: capital land value (site only) of all land in Great Britain except exemptions. Valued by Inland Revenue Commissioners and Valuation Office (body not named in the speech **[U]**). Planned revaluation every five years. Rate 1d in the pound (about 0.42% of capital value a year). Revenue: nil (never levied). Valuations: never made **[U for whether any preparatory valuation started]**.

### Inferences
- Snowden designed the 1931 tax to avoid the 1909 trap: one simple definition (vacant-site sale value) in place of four interacting values, and a periodic tax in place of a transaction-triggered one. It failed for political and fiscal-crisis reasons before its valuation machinery could be tested.
- Keeping section 28 in 1934 shows the state valued data on market transactions independently of any land tax. Background **[U]**: this is usually identified as the origin of the "Particulars Delivered" sales-evidence system used by valuers until Stamp Duty Land Tax returns replaced it in 2003.

### Gaps
- The enacted valuation date and the statutory instrument or Act that formally suspended the tax (often said to be the Finance Act 1932) were not verified.
- Any money actually spent on preparing the 1931 valuation was not found.

## 6. Post-war betterment taxes: the Uthwatt Report (1942), the development charge (1947), the betterment levy (1967) and Development Land Tax (1976)

### Takeaway
After 1945, Britain stopped trying to tax the annual value of all land and instead taxed "development value": the gain created when land is permitted or able to change use. Labour governments introduced three versions: a 100% development charge under the 1947 planning system, a 40% betterment levy in 1967, and Development Land Tax at 80% in 1976. Each was assessed by valuers measuring market value against existing-use or base value. Each was abolished or emptied by the next Conservative government after complaints of complexity, arbitrariness and deterred development. Revenues were modest: about £12 million in cash from the development charge by October 1952, and an average of about £40 million a year from DLT.

### Cited Findings
**1947 development charge (Town and Country Planning Act 1947)**
- **Machinery:** a Central Land Board assessed development charges, and a £300 million global sum compensated owners for loss of development rights. Scotland's share was distributed by the same Board. **[P]** — [HC Deb 7 July 1947](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1947/jul/07/clause-1-the-central-land-board)
- **Appointed day:** land with plans approved by 1 July 1948 (certificates under section 80) was exempt. An MP said such land commanded prices "absolutely sky high", and that the district valuer used them to assess charges on neighbouring land. **[P, claim in debate]** — [HC Deb 4 July 1952](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1952/jul/04/town-and-country-planning-development)
- **Revenue:** in 1951 the Central Land Board received £3,583,000, and £1,381,000 was set off against claims on the £300 million. Collection cost could not be separated because much of the Board's staff were dealing with claims, "of which nearly 950,000 have been registered" **[P]** — [HC Deb 29 Jan 1952 (WA)](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1952/jan/29/development-charge-revenue). Up to 17 October 1952, "£12,258,000 had been paid in cash" and £7,235,000 set off against claims **[P]** — [HC Deb 24 Oct 1952 (WA)](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1952/oct/24/development-charge-payments)
- **Criticism:** in July 1952 Conservative minister Ernest Marples called the charge "the worst part" of the Act. He said it added to development costs, acted as a tax on development, and was assessed arbitrarily with no right of appeal. MPs cited charges of £250–£375 on Brighton and Hove plots, a £10,000 charge (later £8,000) on a hotel, and 39 of 197 Brighton building licences returned. **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 July 1952](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1952/jul/04/town-and-country-planning-development)
- **End:** by April 1953 ministers were acting "on the assumption that the Town and Country Planning Act becomes law" (the 1953 Bill) **[P]** — [HC Deb 2 April 1953](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1953/apr/02/development-charge-compensation-claims). The Central Land Board was dissolved by an Order in Council debated on 17 March 1959 **[P, title]** — [Historic Hansard index](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/acts/town-and-country-planning-act-1947)

**1967 betterment levy (Land Commission Act 1967)**
- **Rate and base:** the Land Commission received "40 per cent" of development value. Development value was measured above a base linked to existing-use value plus 10%, or the price paid on a previous transaction (the "Schedule 5 base"). **[P for 40%; P/U for exact base formula, which was garbled in extraction]** — [HL Deb 27 Feb 1969](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1969/feb/27/the-land-commission-and-betterment-levy)
- **Costs:** the Commission said its collection costs for the previous year would exceed £2 million (cited by the Earl of Kinnoull, 1969). **[P, as cited in debate]** — [HL Deb 27 Feb 1969](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1969/feb/27/the-land-commission-and-betterment-levy)
- **Revenue and abolition (9 March 1971 Third Reading):**
  - Labour's Fred Willey said revenue "was rising steeply to £65 million" when the levy was discontinued; it is unclear whether he meant an annual or a cumulative figure.
  - Minister Graham Page said £27–28 million remained to be collected on 1 May 1971.
  - 444 transactions accounted for 40% of the levy (Willey).
  - Staff fell from about 1,000 to 100.
  - The Commission held 2,473 acres.
  - Land prices doubled from 1963 to 1970 (Page, citing *Economic Trends*).
  - Chargeable events on or before 22 July 1970 (Page) or 23 July (Glyn) remained liable.
  - The vote was 255 to 217.
  **[P]** — [HC Deb 9 March 1971](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1971/mar/09/abolition-of-betterment-levy-and)
- **Arguments:** the Conservatives cited an election pledge, hardship, accelerated land price rises, capital gains tax as an adequate substitute, and a saving of "over £2,500,000" in administration. Labour argued the levy was working, and that abolition handed a windfall to landowners and would not lower land prices. **[P]** — same source.

**1976 Development Land Tax (Development Land Tax Act 1976)**
- Royal Assent 22 July 1976 **[P, index]** — [Historic Hansard index](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/acts/development-land-tax-act-1976)
- **Rates:** basic rate 80%, with an intended rise to 100%. A 66⅔% rate applied to realised development value "up to a figure of £150,000 in any one year" in an interim period originally ending 31 March 1979 and extended to 31 March 1980. **[P]** — [HC Deb 11 July 1978](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1978/jul/11/development-land-tax-extention-of). Cut from 80% to 60% in 1979 **[P]** — [HC Deb 17 July 1980](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1980/jul/17/relief-from-development-land-tax)
- **Thresholds:** £10,000 (1976), £50,000 (1979), £75,000 (1984) **[P, opposition spokesman's account]** — [HC Deb 8 May 1985](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1985/may/08/clause-87)
- **Revenue and cost:** Economic Secretary Ian Stewart said the average gross yield over nine years was "about £40 million a year". In the latest year collection cost "£1 to every £13 of tax", more than three times as expensive as capital gains tax. Labour put the cost of abolition at £50 million in a full year. **[P]** — [HC Deb 8 May 1985](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1985/may/08/clause-87)
- **Abolition:** clause 87 of the Finance Bill 1985 ("Abolition of development land tax and tax on development gains") was agreed 184 to 117 on 8 May 1985. Stewart called DLT "an inefficient tax because it is extremely complicated and at a punitive rate", and said it deterred development. Labour argued that planning-created value belonged partly to the community and that there was no evidence the tax reduced housing starts. **[P]** — [HC Deb 8 May 1985](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1985/may/08/clause-87)
- DLT was the fiscal companion of the Community Land Act 1975: a Conservative MP called it "the handmaiden of an obnoxious piece of legislation". **[P, as statement]** — [HC Deb 17 July 1980](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1980/jul/17/relief-from-development-land-tax)

### Inferences
- In all three schemes the taxable base was valued against a counterfactual: what the land was worth without permission, or in its existing use. Valuers therefore had to estimate two hypothetical values per transaction. That is a heavier and more contestable task than a single market value, which may help explain the repeated complaints of arbitrariness and the high collection costs (1 in 13 for DLT). The evidence on whether these taxes held back land supply is disputed in the debates themselves: ministers asserted deterrence, while the opposition said there was no evidence it reduced housing starts.
- Each scheme was abolished within about two years of a change of government, so none ran long enough to test steady-state yields.

### Gaps
- **Uthwatt Report [U]:** Expert Committee on Compensation and Betterment, *Final Report* (Cmd 6386, HMSO, 1942). Background accounts say it recommended nationalising development rights in undeveloped land (with compensation), and a periodic levy (usually given as 75%) on increases in annual site value, with five-yearly revaluations. Neither the report nor a primary summary could be opened this session.
- **1947 [U]:** the charge rate (widely described as 100% of the increase in value due to permission) and the abolition date (widely given as 18 November 1952 announcement, Town and Country Planning Act 1953) were not verified from primary text.
- **1967 [U]:** start date (6 April 1967 is widely given), the planned increases to 45% and 50%, and total levy collected.
- **1976 [U]:** start date (1 August 1976 is widely given) and abolition date (disposals on or after 19 March 1985, Budget day, is widely given).
- The Housing, Communities and Local Government Committee report *Land Value Capture* (HC 766, 2018) has a history with figures but was blocked (403).

## 7. From domestic rates to the community charge to council tax: why England and Scotland still use 1 April 1991 values; Wales' 2005 revaluation and its 2024–2026 plans

### Takeaway
Council tax (from 1 April 1993) put each dwelling into one of eight value bands based on its estimated sale value on 1 April 1991. England never revalued: the 2007 revaluation was postponed, and the 2006 Act left the date to ministerial order. Scotland still uses 1991 values but plans two new top bands from April 2028 using up-to-date values for homes worth over £1 million. Wales revalued once (values at 1 April 2003, effective 1 April 2005): a third of homes went up a band and 8% went down. Wales then legislated for five-yearly revaluations from 2028, but on 20 October 2026 its new government paused that revaluation and said it would defer it to the next Senedd term.

### Cited Findings
**Community charge (poll tax)**
- Scotland: the Abolition of Domestic Rates Etc. (Scotland) Act 1987 received Royal Assent on 15 May 1987 **[P, index]** — [Historic Hansard index](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/acts/abolition-of-domestic-rates-etc-scotland-act-1987). Peers discussed people "liable on 1st April 1989", and charges of £200–£400 a year were mentioned **[P, indirect]** — [HL Deb 9 April 1987](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1987/apr/09/abolition-of-domestic-rates-etc-scotland)
- England and Wales: the Local Government Finance Act 1988 created "personal", "standard" and "collective" community charges **[P]** — [legislation.gov.uk s.1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/41/section/1/enacted). In 1987 the Welsh Office illustrated an average of £268 per property in domestic rates against £136 per adult under the community charge (illustrative 1987–88 figures) **[P]** — [HC Deb 24 July 1987 (WA)](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1987/jul/24/domestic-rates)

**Council tax: the valuation**
- Established by the Local Government Finance Act 1992; "Council Tax came into effect on 1 April 1993". The valuation date for England and Wales is 1 April 1991 (SI 1992/550). Listing Officers (appointed by HMRC Commissioners under s.20 LGFA 1992, a duty delegated to the Valuation Office Agency) compile and maintain the lists. **[P]** — [VOA Council Tax Manual s.1](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/council-tax-manual/section-1-introduction-and-essential-background)

  | Band | Value at 1 April 1991 |

  | A | up to £40,000 |
  | B | £40,000–£52,000 |
  | C | £52,000–£68,000 |
  | D | £68,000–£88,000 |
  | E | £88,000–£120,000 |
  | F | £120,000–£160,000 |

**England: the revaluation that never happened**
- "A proposed revaluation, due to take effect on 1 April 2007 (with an AVD of 1 April 2005), was postponed." **[P]** — [VOA Council Tax Manual s.1](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/council-tax-manual/section-1-introduction-and-essential-background). - Why it persists: a search-result summary attributed to the IFS the view that changing council tax is politically difficult **[S, search summary only; the IFS page returned 403]** — [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/15388). **Scotland**
- Current council tax is based on 1991 values. The Scottish Budget 2026–27 creates Band I (£1m–£2m) and Band J (over £2m) from April 2028. Liability for these will rest on an "up to date" valuation, with £5 million set aside for a partial, targeted revaluation affecting fewer than 1% of properties. COSLA estimated a yield of £12–16 million a year and a one-off revaluation cost of about £10 million. Receipts will stay with councils. **[P]** — [SPICe, Scottish Budget 2026-27 (16 Jan 2026)](https://www.parliament.scot/chamber-and-committees/research-prepared-for-parliament/research-briefings/2026/1/16/sb-2604)

**Wales: the 2005 revaluation and its effects**
- Valuation date 1 April 2003; took effect 1 April 2005. Bands set by Council Tax (Valuation Bands) (Wales) Order 2003; nine bands from A (up to £44,000) to the new I (over £424,000). The revaluation was "manual", with individual properties placed in bands. **[P]** — [VOA Council Tax Manual s.1](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/council-tax-manual/section-1-introduction-and-essential-background)

- A search-result summary of press and political sources (BBC, North Wales Daily Post, Public Finance and party pages) reported two further points: the Welsh Assembly Government limited increases to one band a year as a relief scheme, and revaluation pushed the average 2005–06 council tax rise to 9.1%. **[S, search summary only; underlying pages not opened]** — e.g. [Public Finance](https://www.publicfinance.co.uk/node/7898); [Daily Post](https://www.dailypost.co.uk/news/north-wales-news/revaluation-hit-lower-bands-hardest-2876440)

**Wales: plans for regular revaluation, and the 2026 pause**
- 15 May 2024 (Rebecca Evans, Cabinet Secretary for Finance): after consultation (1,676 responses; 24% backed reform from 2025 and 35% from 2028), "We now plan to introduce the structural reforms to the council tax system from 2028" and "I will bring forward an amendment to the Bill to begin five-yearly revaluations from 2028." **[P]** — [Welsh Government written statement](https://www.gov.wales/written-statement-update-council-tax-reform)

### Inferences
- Wales shows what happens when values are refreshed after only 12 years: one dwelling in three moved up a band, concentrated where prices had risen fastest (Cardiff). By 2026 the geography of price growth had reversed, with Blaenau Gwent up more than 300% versus under 150% in Cardiff (IFS). A 2028 revaluation would therefore have shifted bills toward former low-growth valleys areas, which helps explain the political reluctance.
- The 2026 Welsh decision repeats England's 2005 pattern: a statutory revaluation date is set, the transitional cost becomes visible, and the date is moved by further legislation.

## 8. Non-domestic (business) rates: the revaluation cycle in law and in practice

### Takeaway
Since 1990, business properties in England and Wales have been revalued on rental value by the Valuation Office Agency, nominally every five years until 2010 and every three years from 2023. In practice two revaluations were moved: 2015 to 2017 (after the downturn), and 2022 brought forward to 2021 then postponed by COVID-19 to 2023. The latest revaluation took effect on 1 April 2026 on rents at 1 April 2024.

### Cited Findings
- "From 1990 to 2010" new rating lists were compiled every five years. The 2015 revaluation "was postponed to 2017 in both England and Wales" (after the 2012 downturn). At Spring Statement 2018 the next revaluation was brought forward from 1 April 2022 to 1 April 2021. On 6 May 2020 the government announced it "would be postponed", and it was reset to 1 April 2023 on rents at 1 April 2021. **[P]** — [NDR Act 2023 Explanatory Notes, para 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/53/notes/division/3/index.htm)
- The Business Rates Review final report committed to three-yearly revaluations from 2023, so the next was due in 2026. The stated reasons were fairness and responsiveness to economic change. The 2026 challenge window was extended from three to six months. **[P]** — [EN paras 8, 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/53/notes/division/3/index.htm)
- Non-Domestic Rating Act 2023 (c. 53), Royal Assent 26 October 2023. Section 5 is headed "Frequency with which lists are compiled". It also created a duty to notify the VOA of changes and to supply rent, lease and trade information, with penalties "in line with HMRC practice". **[P]** — [legislation.gov.uk contents](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/53/contents); [EN div. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/53/notes/division/1/index.htm); [EN div. 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/53/notes/division/3/index.htm)
- 2026 revaluation: "It's based on open market rental values from 1 April 2024", effective 1 April 2026 in England and Wales. The Valuation Office "updates the rateable value of business properties". **[P]** — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/introduction-to-business-rates/revaluation); [Valuation Tribunal Service newsletter, Feb 2026](https://valuationtribunal.gov.uk/app/uploads/2026/02/VIP-February-2026-Issue-79.pdf)
- England 2026 also introduced separate multipliers for qualifying retail, hospitality and leisure properties (SI 2025/1093, guidance 16 October 2025). **[P]** — [VTS newsletter Feb 2026](https://valuationtribunal.gov.uk/app/uploads/2026/02/VIP-February-2026-Issue-79.pdf)
- Wales: the 2024 Act moves non-domestic revaluations to every three years. **[S, search summary only; page not opened]** — [Senedd Research bill summary](https://research.senedd.wales/research-articles/local-government-finance-wales-bill-bill-summary/); [Welsh Government press release](https://gov.wales/senedd-passes-bill-reform-local-tax-system-wales)
- Scotland is also revaluing for 2026. The basic property rate falls from 49.8p to 48.1p (rateable value up to £51,000). A new 15% retail, hospitality and leisure relief runs April 2026 to March 2029. Non-domestic rates income to councils is about £3.5 billion in 2026–27. **[P]** — [SPICe SB 26-04](https://www.parliament.scot/chamber-and-committees/research-prepared-for-parliament/research-briefings/2026/1/16/sb-2604)

### Inferences
- The gap between valuation date and list date (two years for 2026: values at 1 April 2024 for a list from 1 April 2026) means business rates always rest on somewhat old rents. The move to three-yearly cycles plus a duty to supply information is an attempt to keep values fresher through more data rather than more inspection.
- Unlike domestic property, business property has been revalued repeatedly since 1990. The postponements (2015, 2021) were justified by economic shocks, not by the transitional politics that froze domestic values.

### Gaps
- The legal instruments for the 2015→2017 and 2021→2023 postponements were not identified by name. Whether the 2026 antecedent valuation date gap shortens to one year from 2029 was not verified.

## 9. The High Value Council Tax Surcharge (HVCTS): Budget 2025, 2026 consultation and legislation

### Takeaway
At Budget 2025 the UK government announced an annual surcharge on owners of homes in England worth £2 million or more, from April 2028, in four value bands from £2,500 to £7,500 a year, uprated by CPI. For the first time since 1991, English homes (only the top fewer than 1%) will be valued at current (2026) market value. The Valuation Office will use automated valuation models checked by valuers, publish a draft list in autumn 2027 and revalue every five years. After a consultation (19 May to 14 July 2026, 424 responses), the government confirmed the design on 28 October 2026 and put the surcharge in the Finance Bill 2026–27.

### Cited Findings
- **Bands and charges (annual):**

  | Band | Value | Charge |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1 | £2.0m–£2.5m | £2,500 |
  | 2 | £2.5m–£3.5m | £3,500 |
  | 3 | £3.5m–£5.0m | £5,000 |
  | 4 | £5m+ | £7,500 |

  Starts 1 April 2028 ("from April 2028"). **[P]** — [GOV.UK HVCTS policy paper (28 Oct 2026)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/high-value-council-tax-surcharge-on-residential-properties/additional-council-tax-charge-on-high-value-properties); [consultation document](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge/high-value-council-tax-surcharge)
- "Charges will be uprated in line with Consumer Price Inflation (CPI) each year." Whether thresholds are uprated is left to a future government. **[P]** — [Consultation document](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge/high-value-council-tax-surcharge)
- **Who pays and collection:** owners, not occupiers. Councils collect alongside council tax on behalf of central government and are fully compensated for administration. **[P]** — [VTS newsletter Feb 2026](https://valuationtribunal.gov.uk/app/uploads/2026/02/VIP-February-2026-Issue-79.pdf); [GOV.UK publication page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/high-value-council-tax-surcharge-on-residential-properties)
- **Valuation method:**
  - The surcharge applies to dwellings worth £2 million or more "in 2026".
  - The Valuation Office will run "a targeted valuation exercise", using "the comparable method": sales of similar properties, adjusted for differences, not individual sale prices alone.
  - Automated valuation models (AVMs) give a first estimate, which valuers review and assure. Where sales are thin, more weight goes on individual property information.
  - Data: the VO property database, Stamp Duty Land Tax data, local authority plans, and government geospatial data. The VO may contact owners to confirm details.
  - Revaluation every 5 years, next in 2033.
  - New builds are banded on completion or occupation. Improvements alone do not change the band until sale or revaluation.
  **[P]** — [Consultation document, Annex B](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge/high-value-council-tax-surcharge)
- **Challenges and appeals:** an initial 8-month window to challenge banding, then 6 months for newly liable owners. The VO must respond within 4 months, and appeals go to the Valuation Tribunal. The VO "will publish a draft list of properties in scope of the charge in Autumn 2027". "HVCTS revaluations will take place every 5 years." **[P]** — [Consultation response (28 Oct 2026), ch. 7](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae0e337779378370fd80a85/HVCTS_Summary_of_Responses.pdf)
- **Scale and revenue:** "Fewer than 1% of residential properties in England"; revenue "around £430 million" a year from 2028–29 **[P]** — [Consultation document](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge/high-value-council-tax-surcharge); [VTS newsletter](https://valuationtribunal.gov.uk/app/uploads/2026/02/VIP-February-2026-Issue-79.pdf). The October 2026 policy paper estimates about 150,000 individuals and 15,000 businesses or civil-society bodies liable. Its Exchequer table (£m): 2026–27 −90; 2027–28 −115; 2028–29 +365; 2029–30 +395; 2030–31 +395; 2031–32 +405 **[P]** — [GOV.UK policy paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/high-value-council-tax-surcharge-on-residential-properties/additional-council-tax-charge-on-high-value-properties). **Discrepancy:** the 2028–29 figure (£365m) is below the "around £430 million" first cited; the page does not explain this or the negative early years (plausibly set-up costs; inference **[U]**).
- **2026 consultation and decisions:**
  - Consultation ran 19 May to 14 July 2026; response published 28 October 2026 **[P]** — [GOV.UK consultation page](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge)
  - 424 responses: 243 public, 25 local authorities, 156 others.
  - Deferral confirmed for households with income of £35,000 or less, savings of £16,000 or less, or meeting disability criteria; deferral ends on change of ownership.
  - The government did not adopt the non-UK-resident premium it had consulted on, citing "challenges around implementation and enforcement". Instead it is "legislating to levy a 100% premium on homes which are substantially unfurnished and unoccupied for a period of 12 months or more".
  - Next steps: "legislating in Finance Bill 2026". Signed by James Murray MP (Financial Secretary to the Treasury) and Jim McMahon MP (local government minister).
  **[P]** — [Consultation response](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae0e337779378370fd80a85/HVCTS_Summary_of_Responses.pdf)

- **Budget 2026 context:** the policy paper is listed among "Budget 2026 tax related documents", published 28 October 2026 **[P]** — [GOV.UK publication page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/high-value-council-tax-surcharge-on-residential-properties). Accounting firms reported that Budget 2026 brought "No reform of council tax" and "No changes to Mansion tax bands", and that no land value tax or annual property levy was announced **[S]** — [Bishop Fleming summary](https://www.bishopfleming.co.uk/insights/autumn-budget-2026-summary-key-tax-changes-and-announcements). A preview named John Healey as Chancellor in a "Burnham government" **[S, unverified]** — [Grant Thornton](https://www.grantthornton.co.uk/insights/autumn-budget/2026-predictions/)

### Inferences
- The HVCTS creates a second valuation regime on top of 1991 council tax bands: current-value, model-assisted, five-yearly. It is the first recurring tax on current English residential values since domestic rates. The design (AVMs plus valuer review, transaction data from SDLT, a published draft list) shows how mass-appraisal methods are meant to keep a value-based tax current at low cost, at least for a small slice of the stock.
- The banding (fixed charges within broad value ranges) limits how much a valuation error matters, except near band edges. Respondents worried that comparables are thin for unique high-value homes (consultation response ch. 7).

- The Finance Bill 2026–27's parliamentary progress and the identity of the Chancellor in October 2026 were not verified from primary sources.

## 10. Land value tax proposals with official standing today (to November 2026)

### Takeaway
I found no land value tax proposal currently adopted as policy by the UK, Scottish or Welsh governments. Wales legislated for regular revaluation (2024), then paused it (2026) and said it would look at "more targeted" improvements. Scotland's 2026–27 budget adds high-value council tax bands, not a land value tax. Earlier Welsh and Scottish feasibility work on land value tax could not be verified this session.

### Cited Findings
- The Welsh Government's 15 May 2024 council tax reform statement does not mention land value tax. **[P]** — [Welsh Government](https://www.gov.wales/written-statement-update-council-tax-reform)
- On 20 October 2026 the Welsh Government said it would look at "other, more targeted ways to improve the system that don't come with the same price tag" and welcomed ideas. No land value tax was announced. **[P]** — [Welsh Government](https://www.gov.wales/council-tax-revaluation-paused-in-difficult-budget-context)
- The Scottish Budget 2026–27 briefing covers council tax bands and non-domestic rates. The part I could read does not mention land value tax. **[P, partial read]** — [SPICe SB 26-04](https://www.parliament.scot/chamber-and-committees/research-prepared-for-parliament/research-briefings/2026/1/16/sb-2604)
- Budget 2026 (UK, 28 October 2026): commentators report no land value tax or annual property levy, and the Prime Minister reportedly rejected replacing stamp duty and council tax with a single annual property tax "at this stage". **[S]** — [HomeOwners Alliance](https://hoa.org.uk/news/new-property-tax/); [Bishop Fleming](https://www.bishopfleming.co.uk/insights/autumn-budget-2026-summary-key-tax-changes-and-announcements)

## 11. Plain-language concepts a newcomer needs

### Takeaway
Nearly every British episode turns on a few technical ideas: what is being valued (site or site plus buildings), on what measure (rent or capital), as at what date, by whom, and how often it is refreshed.

### Cited Findings
- **Annual (rental) value versus capital value:** rates and council tax's predecessors taxed annual rental value (open market rent), as in the 1973 revaluation under s.19 General Rate Act 1967 **[P]** — [HC Deb 24 Nov 1971](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1971/nov/24/rate-assessments-revaluation). Council tax uses capital (sale) value bands as at a fixed date **[P]** — [VOA Council Tax Manual](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/council-tax-manual/section-1-introduction-and-essential-background)
- **Site value (land value):** the value of the land as if bare of buildings and owners' improvements. In 1909 it excluded crops, buildings and owner-funded improvements **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/). In 1931 it was the price of the land with buildings assumed absent **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1)
- **Hereditament / assessment number:** the unit of property that gets one valuation. 1910 maps carry hereditament numbers that index the field books **[P]** — [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)
- **Antecedent valuation date (AVD):** the date at which values are estimated, often years before a list takes effect: 1 April 1991 for council tax in England from 1993; 1 April 2003 for Wales from 2005 **[P]** — [VOA Council Tax Manual](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/council-tax-manual/section-1-introduction-and-essential-background); 1 April 2024 for business rates from 2026 **[P]** — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/introduction-to-business-rates/revaluation)
- **Revaluation:** re-estimating all values at a new AVD. It redistributes the tax burden, which is why it is politically hard. In Wales 2005, 33.3% of homes went up a band **[P]** — [VOA data](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80c92be5274a2e8ab52281/Band_changes_due_to_2005_reval_in_Wales_final.xls)
- **Quota (apportioned) tax versus rate tax:** a quota tax fixes the total for each area and shares it out; a rate tax applies a rate to each property's value. The land tax switched to quotas in 1698 **[P]** — [AIM25/LMA](https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/land-tax-commissioners-6)
- **Redemption:** paying a lump sum to extinguish a perpetual tax charge. For the land tax this cost 25 times the annual charge by 1950 **[P]** — [TNA C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030)
- **Development value / betterment:** the extra value land gains from permission or the prospect of a more valuable use, measured above existing-use or base value. Taxed in 1947 (development charge), 1967 (40% levy) and 1976 (DLT at 80%, later 60%) **[P]** — [HL Deb 27 Feb 1969](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1969/feb/27/the-land-commission-and-betterment-levy); [HC Deb 11 July 1978](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1978/jul/11/development-land-tax-extention-of)
- **Listing officer / assessor / district valuer:** the official who values. VOA Listing Officers compile council tax lists in England and Wales; in Scotland the assessor also acts as electoral registration officer; in 1910 there were district valuers **[P]** — [VOA Council Tax Manual](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/council-tax-manual/section-1-introduction-and-essential-background); [HL Deb 9 April 1987](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1987/apr/09/abolition-of-domestic-rates-etc-scotland); [TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)
- **Banding:** grouping values into ranges with a fixed charge or multiplier per band (council tax; HVCTS). Banding reduces the effect of small valuation errors but causes cliff edges at band boundaries **[P for HVCTS bands]** — [Consultation document](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge/high-value-council-tax-surcharge)
- **Automated valuation model (AVM) / mass appraisal:** statistical models estimating many values from sales data, then reviewed by valuers. Used for the HVCTS **[P]** — same source.
- **Transitional relief:** phasing in bill changes after revaluation. Its cost was the reason Wales gave for pausing in 2026 (up to £80 million in year one) **[P]** — [Welsh Government](https://www.gov.wales/council-tax-revaluation-paused-in-difficult-budget-context)

### Inferences
- A rough rule from the British record: the narrower and more hypothetical the valuation concept (assessable site value net of improvements; development value against a no-permission counterfactual), the more litigation and administrative cost followed. Simple bands and market values have proved administratively durable but politically hard to refresh.

### Gaps
- None beyond those noted in earlier sections.

## 12. Scenes and anecdotes (well sourced)

### Takeaway
Vivid, primary-sourced moments a teaching narrative can use.

### Cited Findings
- **The ten-million-form survey (1910–12):** more than 10,000,000 Form 4 returns went to owners; 9,600,000 came back by 31 March 1912. Staff grew from 1,428 in 1911 to 2,829 in 1912 and peaked at 4,882 in August 1914, when war began. **[P]** — [HC Deb 14 July 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties); [HC Deb 4 May 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/may/04/reduction-of-staff-land-valuation)
- **The builder's case (Lumsden, 1913–14):** a builder faced increment duty although the bare site had not risen in value. The Lords split evenly in July 1914, the Crown's win stood, and the promised relieving Bill fell when the Revenue Bill was dropped. **[P]** — [HC Deb 23 July 1914](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1914/jul/23/land-taxes-lumsden-case); [HC Deb 15 July 1913](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1913/jul/15/site-value-case-of-mr-r-j-lumsden)
- **A Somerset farm stops the farmland valuation (1914):** after Mr Justice Scrutton's ruling in the Norton Malreward and Chells cases, Lloyd George told the Commons that service of valuations and Undeveloped Land Duty assessments on agricultural property had been suspended. **[P]** — [HC Deb 21 April 1914](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1914/apr/21/valuation-of-agricultural-hereditaments)
- **"Let bygones be bygones" (1920):** repealing the duties, Chamberlain hoped taxpayers would not reclaim small sums. By 16 October 1920, £228,591 had been refunded, without interest. **[P]** — [HC Deb 19 April 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/apr/19/land-values-duties); [HC Deb 27 Oct 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1920/oct/27/land-values-duties-repayments). He noted refunds came "in very trifling sums, like 30s." **[P]** — [HC Deb 14 July 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties)
- **Becontree, 1934:** in the debate killing Snowden's tax, an MP cited land valued at £7,000 for rating that sold for £295,000. **[P, as a claim]** — [HC Deb 5 June 1934](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1934/jun/05/clause-25-repeal-of-land-value-tax)
- **Nearly a million claims (1952):** the Central Land Board's staff were busy with "nearly 950,000" claims on the £300 million compensation fund, while it collected £3.6 million of development charge in 1951. **[P]** — [HC Deb 29 Jan 1952](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1952/jan/29/development-charge-revenue)
- **Brighton's returned licences (1952):** 39 of 197 Brighton building licences were handed back, attributed mainly to development charges. The minister called the charge "the worst part" of the Act. **[P]** — [HC Deb 4 July 1952](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1952/jul/04/town-and-country-planning-development)
- **£1 to collect every £13 (1985):** the minister abolishing DLT said it cost £1 to collect each £13, more than three times capital gains tax. **[P]** — [HC Deb 8 May 1985](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1985/may/08/clause-87)
- **Cardiff versus Blaenau Gwent (2005 and 2026):** in 2005 almost two-thirds of Cardiff homes went up a band and fewer than 7% in Blaenau Gwent **[P]** — [VOA data](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80c92be5274a2e8ab52281/Band_changes_due_to_2005_reval_in_Wales_final.xls). By 2026 the IFS reported values up more than 300% in Blaenau Gwent against under 150% in Cardiff, which is why a new revaluation would hit different places **[S]** — [IFS via Wired-Gov](https://wired-gov.net/wg/news.nsf/articles/Welsh+Government+council+tax+announcement+IFS+response+23102026091500?open=)
- **Valuing mansions by algorithm (2026–27):** to value about 1% of English homes for the first time since 1991, the Valuation Office will run automated valuation models over SDLT sales data and its property database, with valuers checking results, and publish a draft list in autumn 2027. **[P]** — [Consultation document](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge/high-value-council-tax-surcharge); [Consultation response](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae0e337779378370fd80a85/HVCTS_Summary_of_Responses.pdf)

### Inferences
- These scenes support a teaching arc: valuation feasible (1910) → law and politics fail (1914–20; 1931–34) → development-value taxes deter or irritate (1952; 1985) → revaluation as the enduring political bottleneck (Wales 2005/2026) → technology-assisted valuation as the latest attempt (2026).

### Gaps
- No primary-sourced anecdote found for the 18th-century land tax (e.g. glaring regional under-assessment). Ward (1953) would be the place to look.
