# Land taxation and valuation in Australia and New Zealand, from the colonial period to 4 November 2026

Notes for a worldwide chronology of land taxation. Valuation is the through-line: what was valued (land alone or land plus buildings), on what basis (area or carrying capacity, rental value, sale price), who valued it, how often, and what happened when valuations aged, were frozen, were disputed or were abandoned. The notes are descriptive and take no position on land value taxation.

**Research date:** 4 November 2026. **Coverage:** to 4 November 2026.

## 1. Dated timeline (for merging into the global chronology)

### Takeaway
The region began with English-style rating on annual (rental) value. New Zealand and the Australian colonies then turned, from the late 1870s, to taxing land value, usually "unimproved" value with a progressive scale aimed at large estates. Australia ran a federal land tax from 1910 to 1952–53. New Zealand abolished its national land tax with effect from 1 April 1992. Since then land value has survived mainly in Australian state land taxes and local rates, while many NZ councils moved from land value to capital value. Since 2012 the reform agenda has been to replace stamp duty with recurrent land-based taxes: the ACT from 2012, NSW briefly in 2023, Victoria for commercial land from 2024.

### Inferences
- The region repeatedly moved its tax base between three valuation concepts: annual (rental) value, capital value (land plus buildings) and unimproved or land value. NZ local rating went from annual value (1876) to capital value (1882) to land value (dominant from about 1940 to the 1980s) and back towards capital value (1985 onward).
- New Zealand and Australia borrowed from each other directly. The Commonwealth's 1910 Act cites NZ (1908), NSW and WA statutes in its margin notes, and a 1924 NZ paper traces the 1891 NZ Act to South Australia's 1884 Act.

### Gaps
- No primary dates retrieved for Tasmania's first land tax (often given as 1880) or Queensland's (often given as 1915). Both dates are from background knowledge and unverified.
- The exact Commonwealth abolition statute and its date (1952 or 1953) are unresolved; see Section 6.

## 2. New Zealand: the national land tax (1878–1992) — aims, rates, revenue, abolition

### Takeaway
NZ taxed land nationally in three phases.
- **1878–79:** a short-lived land tax.
- **1879–1891:** a property tax on capital (land plus improvements and other property).
- **1891–1992:** a land tax on value with improvements exempt, from 1891 partly and later fully. Under Ballance it was paired with a graduated land tax on large estates, and absentee owners paid a surcharge.

The progressive (graduated) element was abolished in 1931 and a restoration was proposed in 1936; both rest on newspaper extracts only. By the late 1980s the tax had a $175,000 exemption. It was abolished by the Land Tax Abolition Act 1990 with effect from the year of payment beginning 1 April 1992. Officials costed new land taxes in 2009 and 2018; none was adopted.

### Cited Findings
- **1878 land tax.** The Land Tax Act 1878 levied a duty "by way of land-tax" at a halfpenny rate on value above an exemption. The search extract garbles the unit ("one halfpenny for every one hundred pounds"). Parliament chose a halfpenny over a proposed penny, and Atkinson argued that at a halfpenny the Government "would not be able to raise more than £208,000" — [Papers Past 1878 search extracts, e.g. Waikato Times 29 Aug 1878](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/WT18780829.2.13); [Evening Post 4 Sep 1878](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/EP18780904.2.8) [P\*; wording of the rate unresolved]. McCluskey et al. describe the 1878 Act as "Land tax on the unimproved value of land" and note that "Valuation rolls were first compiled following the 1878 Act" — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 2](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **In force and replaced.** The Act came into operation on 1 January 1879, with half-yearly collection on 1 April and 1 October — [Manawatu Herald 7 Jan 1879](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/MH18790107.2.7) [P\*]. Parliament then "adopted a general property tax in place of the land tax", described as "an impost on only one class of property". The Property Assessment Act 1879 repealed the 1878 Act — [official scan of the 1879 Act, search extract](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1879/19/en/1879-12-19/official_scan.pdf) [P\*]
- **1879–1893: tax on capital value.** "Between 1879 and 1893 land tax was levied on capital values, but an amendment to the Property Tax Act in 1893 resulted in land tax being levied on unimproved values only" — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 2](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]. Internal conflict: the same paper later says that in 1891 the tax was "modified to give partial exemption to improvements and in the following year full exemption was granted", which would date full exemption to 1892 — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 3](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [U: 1892 or 1893]
- **The 1891 Act's lineage.** The 1891 Act was based on "the South Australian Land and Income Tax Act of 1884". The graduated land tax was introduced by Ballance "in his Financial Statement speech" — [AJHR 1924 paper, search extract](https://dynamic-rsnz.natlib.govt.nz/imageserver/parliamentary/P29pZD1BSkhSMTkyNC1JLjEuMTEzNCZnZXRwZGY9dHJ1ZQ==) [P\*]
- **Progression and income tax.** A scholarly abstract says the 1891 Act introduced NZ's first income tax and the principle of progression — [Accounting History, DOI 10.1177/1032373211424581](https://doi.org/10.1177/1032373211424581) [S, abstract only]. Conflict on "first": McCluskey et al. say the 1844 Property Rate Ordinance already taxed "both property and income" — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 1](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]. The two claims depend on what counts as an income tax.
- **Why the 1891 tax.** McCluskey et al. link the turn to unimproved value in the early 1890s to depression-era support for J. S. Mill (the "unearned increment" should be shared) and Henry George — [McCluskey et al. 2002, pp. 2–3](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]. A 2018 submission to the Tax Working Group names two historical uses for NZ land tax: "curbing land aggregation and speculation" and raising revenue — [Hobbs submission, TWG 2018, search extract](https://taxworkinggroup.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2018-09/twg-subm-3976749-dylan-hobbs.pdf) [U, submission]
- **Graduated tax and absentees.** The graduated tax schedule sat in Schedule B of the 1891 Act and was re-imposed annually by Land-tax Acts (e.g. 1893, 1894) "as amended" — [Land-tax Act 1894, search extract](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1894/8/en/latest/) [P\*]. In 1903 the absentee rate became 50% above the scale — [1903 amendment, search extract](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1903/37/en/latest/) [P\*]. *Andrew v Crombie* (1893) 12 NZLR 726 dealt with proof of absence and the "Increase of Scale of Taxation" for absentees — [NZ Law Reports, search extract](https://www.lawreports.nz/andrew-v-crombie-1893-12-nzlr-726/) [P\*]. Mortgages: a 1936 article says mortgages were not chargeable with the graduated tax, but no deduction was allowed for mortgages owing on the land — [Papers Past 1936, search extract](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/TAWC19360826.2.33) [P\*/U]
- **1931 and 1936.** The graduated land tax was abolished in 1931 with an estimated loss of £320,000, offset by higher income tax. Before abolition it had yielded "between £1,000,000 and …" over the preceding 15 years (the extract is cut off). In 1936 the Government proposed restoring it, estimated at £1,300,000 — [NZ Herald 5 Aug 1936 items, search extracts](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/NZH19360805.2.114); [NZH19360805.2.124](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/NZH19360805.2.124) [P\*/U: full text not read]
- **1976–1990.** The Land Tax Act 1976 was the last principal Act. On 28 July 1989 the Press reported that the $175,000 exemption would be axed from March 1990 in a two-stage reform — [Press, search extract](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19890728.2.12.29) [P\*]
- **Abolition.** The Land Tax Abolition Act 1990 (assent 8 Aug 1990) abolished land tax "on and from the year of payment commencing on 1 April 1992" and repealed the Land Tax Act 1976 — [legislation.govt.nz, search extract](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1990/92/en/latest/) [P\*]. Sources disagree on the year: a NSW Parliament briefing says NZ "abolished it in 1990", another source says 1991, others 1992. The 1990 date is enactment; the legal effect runs from the 1992 year of payment — [search extracts listed in the Access log](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/parliamentary-business/research-and-library/research-publications-data/land-tax-in-nsw) [U]
- **Why abolished.** Two studies address this but were not read. Barry Reece, "The Abolition of Land Tax in New Zealand: Searching for Causes and Policy Lessons", *Australian Tax Forum* (1993), p. 223 — [University of Auckland NZ tax history bibliography](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/law/our-research/new-zealand-tax-history-bibliography/-marginal--new-zealand-tax-history-studies/r.html) [reference only]. A Victoria University of Wellington thesis, "The Use of Land Value Taxation in New Zealand (1891–1991)", which uses a historical-institutionalist frame to explain the abolition — [VUW open access](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/The_Use_of_Land_Value_Taxation_In_New_Zealand_1891_-_1991_/17147717) [reference only; page blocked]. A 2018 TWG submission argues there is "little evidence" the tax was effective at reducing land prices, that its base may have been too narrow, and that it was moving towards a revenue model before abolition — [Hobbs submission, search extract](https://taxworkinggroup.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2018-09/twg-subm-3976749-dylan-hobbs.pdf) [U, submission]
- **Later proposals.** For the 2009 Tax Working Group, officials estimated a non-deductible 1% land tax on all land (except public, conservation and Māori authority land) at "gross revenue of $3.8 billion per annum". A deductible version would raise "almost a fifth less". The estimate assumed land values would fall 16.7% when the tax was imposed — [TWG 2018 discussion paper, paras 26–27](https://taxworkinggroup.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2018-09/twg-bg-3964969-rfrm-and-land-taxes.pdf) [P]. The 2018 paper adds that "A lower rate is likely to be desirable if a land tax is contemplated" — [same, para 28](https://taxworkinggroup.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2018-09/twg-bg-3964969-rfrm-and-land-taxes.pdf) [P]

### Tax profile — NZ land tax 1878–79
- **What was taxed:** land value above an exemption. McCluskey calls the base "unimproved value" — [McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S].
- **Valued how and by whom:** new valuation rolls, first compiled after the 1878 Act — [same](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]. The valuing body was not identified.
- **Rate:** a halfpenny (unit garbled in the source) — [P\*, above].
- **Revenue:** Atkinson's ceiling estimate was £208,000 — [P\*, above]. Actual yield not found.
- **Fate:** replaced in 1879 by the property tax.

### Tax profile — NZ property tax 1879–1891
- **What was taxed:** property including land and improvements, at capital value — [McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S].
- **Rate, exemption and revenue:** not retrieved. The commonly cited 1d in the £ with a £500 exemption is background knowledge and unverified.
- **Valuation:** McCluskey et al. note the national property tax on capital "justified the introduction of a common valuation and tax basis", which was one reason given for allowing capital-value rating locally from 1882 — [p. 2](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S].

### Tax profile — NZ land tax and graduated land tax 1891–1992
- **What was taxed:** land value, with improvements exempt (partly from 1891, fully from 1892 or 1893). There was an exemption threshold ($175,000 by 1989), a graduated scale on larger estates (Schedule B), and an absentee loading (+50% from 1903) — [sources above] [S/P\*].
- **Valued how and by whom:** initially valued within the tax administration. A 1932 retrospective says valuation arrangements "became unworkable" once councils could rate on unimproved value in 1896, so the Government set up "a separate Department of State altogether, independent of the Land and Income Tax Department", to value land for both taxation and local rating — [Press 21 May 1932, search extract](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19320521.2.109) [P\*]. From then on one government valuation roll served both national land tax and local rates (see Section 4).
- **How often values were updated:** the frequency in law for the national tax was not retrieved.
- **Rates:** the 1891 ordinary rate and the graduated scale were not retrieved verbatim.
- **Revenue:** the graduated tax yielded about £1m+ over 15 years before 1931 (truncated extract), and its 1931 abolition cost an estimated £320,000 — [P\*/U]. No series of revenue as a share of total tax was retrieved.
- **Exemptions, caps, phase-out:** the exemption was lifted to $175,000 and then scheduled to be removed in 1990 as part of a two-stage path to abolition — [Press 1989](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19890728.2.12.29) [P\*].
- **Abolition:** effective from the year of payment beginning 1 April 1992 — [P\*].

### Inferences
- Atkinson's £208,000 ceiling at a halfpenny in the £ implies a taxable land base of roughly £100 million (£208,000 × 480 halfpence per £). That makes sense only if the rate was a halfpenny per pound of value, which suggests the "per hundred pounds" wording in the extract is an OCR or summary error. This is my inference.
- One design feature survived across the Tasman. NZ statute required owners to return their own values and let the state buy undervalued land at the owner's own figure. The Commonwealth's 1910 Act cites "N.Z., 1908, No. 95, s. 33" beside its acquisition clause (see Section 6) — [Land Tax Assessment Act 1910](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]. The NZ provision itself was not read.

### Gaps
- Exact 1891 rates (ordinary land tax, graduated scale bands, exemption, the £3,000 improvements cap often cited) were not retrieved; legislation.govt.nz and Papers Past full texts were blocked.
- No revenue series (land tax as a share of NZ tax revenue, 1891–1991) was retrieved. A 2018 NZIER report reportedly charts it — [search extract](https://taxworkinggroup.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2018-09/twg-subm-3983172-property-council-new-zealand-3-of-3.pdf) [U].
- Not retrieved: when owner-occupied residential land and farmland were exempted (often said to be the 1970s–80s, unverified), and the 1990 Labour government's stated reasons for abolition.

## 3. New Zealand: local rating on land value versus capital value versus annual value

### Takeaway
NZ local government has always relied on property rates; over half of council revenue came from rates around 2000. The base swung in three steps. Annual value dominated in the 1840s–1870s, capital value was allowed from 1882, and land (unimproved) value, allowed from 1896 by ratepayer poll, became dominant by WWII. From 1985 the trend reversed towards capital value.

Under the Local Government (Rating) Act 2002 each council chooses land, capital or annual value. By 2019 about 71% used capital value. Notable switches include Dunedin (1989), Hutt City (1997) and Auckland, whose 2012 amalgamation rating moved former land-value areas onto capital value by statute and produced large shifts in rates bills.

### Cited Findings
- Today councils choose among "(i) the total value of land, buildings and other improvements (capital improved value); (ii) land value only (previously known as unimproved value); and (iii) the annual rental value (also known as annual value)" — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 1](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **1882 reasons for allowing capital value:** "(i) undeveloped land and land held for speculative purposes had no rental value but a definite market or capital value; (ii) farm improvements usually added more to the annual rental value than to the capital value; and (iii) the introduction of a national property tax based on capital justified the introduction of a common valuation and tax basis" — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 2, citing Dowse & Hargreaves 1999](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **1896 Rating on Unimproved Value Act:** "the local authority could only change to unimproved value if the proposal was first approved by a poll of ratepayers. Similarly, once unimproved value had been adopted as the basis for rating it could only be abandoned with the support of ratepayers" — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 3](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **The long swing:** "By the Second World War, land value based rating had become the dominant system and this trend continued through to the 1980s. However, since 1985 there has been a noticeable swing back towards the use of capital improved value. This is more evident within the larger urban areas." The paper's Figure 1 plots the shares at 1942, 1956, 1966, 1985 and 2002; exact percentages are not legible in the text extraction — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 3](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **Switching rules:** "Up until 1976 local authorities could switch from capital value to annual value rating systems without reference to ratepayers, but any change in adopting or abandoning land value required a majority poll of ratepayers. At present local authorities can change the rating system without recourse to a taxpayer poll" — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 4](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **Switches 1989–2000:** "Since 1989, nine local authorities have switched from land to capital value rating (out of 57 that initially used land values); no authorities have switched to land value rating." The nine: Dunedin City (1989), Tasman District (1991), Banks Peninsula District (1992), South Waikato District (1993/94), Invercargill City (1994), South Taranaki District (1994), Otorohanga District (1996), Hutt City (1997), Franklin District (1999/2000) — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 19 and n. 10](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **Position in 2002:** the "main-four" cities (Auckland, Wellington, Christchurch, Dunedin) all used capital or annual value. Hamilton used land value, as did North Shore City, Waitakere City, Manukau City and Papakura District within greater Auckland. Councils on capital or annual value had mean incomes on average 8.1% higher than land-value councils — [McCluskey et al. 2002, pp. 4, 15, 19](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **Rates' share of council revenue:** rates were 56.3% (1997), 55.6% (1998), 54.9% (1999), 56.1% (2000) and 57.1% (2001) of local authority revenue. Total revenue was NZ$3,846.9m in the year to June 2001 (Statistics NZ data) — [McCluskey et al. 2002, Table 2](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **Uniform charges:** a cap of 30% applies to uniform charges (flat per-property charges that do not vary with value). LGNZ has long sought its removal — [LGNZ, April 2020](https://www.lgnz.co.nz/documents/168/Prod-Com-funding-review-analysis.pdf) [P]; [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 11](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **Auckland 2012, a statutory change of base.** Auckland Council "is required to introduce a single rating system across Auckland from 1 July 2012" and "must use the capital valuation basis for general rates in 2012/13". The base was set by s 38 of the Local Government (Auckland Transitional Provisions) Act 2010 because a single region-wide valuation roll had to exist before the Council did. The Council could later change to annual or land value. Transition was to run 1 July 2012 – 30 June 2015 — [DIA Regulatory Impact Statement, 16 Apr 2012](https://regulation.govt.nz/assets/RIS-Documents/ris-dia-acrar-may12.pdf) [P]
- **Size of the Auckland shift.** Without a transition policy, 112,239 ratepayers faced rates increases above 15%, 79,368 faced increases of 10–15%, and 111,863 faced decreases of more than 10%. "some of the largest rates increases are faced by large utilities previously rated on land value" — [DIA RIS 2012, Table 1 and para 14](https://regulation.govt.nz/assets/RIS-Documents/ris-dia-acrar-may12.pdf) [P]. The Council's draft remission option capped residential increases at 15% and business increases at 20% — [same, para 17](https://regulation.govt.nz/assets/RIS-Documents/ris-dia-acrar-may12.pdf) [P]
- **2019 Productivity Commission.** The Commission found 71% of NZ councils set rates on capital value. It argued they should use land value because "Landowners cannot do anything to change the unimproved value of their land", and that the move from land to capital value was "likely" to have reduced efficiency, more for business than residential property — [Otago Daily Times, 18 Dec 2019](https://www.odt.co.nz/regions/wanaka/commission-councils-setting-rates-wrong) [S; report blocked]. LGNZ's response: the Commission "expresses support for land value taxes" yet "acknowledge[s] that capital value rating is regarded as a better proxy for benefit than land value rating. This tension is never resolved" — [LGNZ, April 2020](https://www.lgnz.co.nz/documents/168/Prod-Com-funding-review-analysis.pdf) [P, as LGNZ's view]
- **Councils still on land value (unverified).** Search extracts say Kaipara District is "one of the few district councils still using land value" for the general rate, Nelson and Far North rate on land value, and Whanganui is weighing a switch. A Whanganui councillor is quoted: "most councils use capital value" — [Whanganui Chronicle](https://www.nzherald.co.nz/whanganui-chronicle/news/whanganui-district-council-weighing-up-rating-on-capital-or-land-value-as-part-of-review/QC2JK7GQSZESHNU2BHUXDJHFRA); [Nelson City Council](https://nelson.govt.nz/building-and-property/rates/revaluation-what-does-it-mean-for-you); [Far North DC](https://www.fndc.govt.nz/services/property/Property-valuations) [U, search extracts only]

### Inferences
- Between 1896 and 1976 the poll requirement made land-value rating "sticky". It was hard to adopt and equally hard to abandon. Removing the poll (1976) and the 1988–2002 rating reforms coincided with the post-1985 swing to capital value. Causation is not established by these sources.
- Auckland's 2012 change of base was imposed by statute and not chosen locally. The redistribution table shows how much a change of valuation base alone can move individual bills even when total revenue is fixed.

### Gaps
- No current (2025–2026) authoritative list of which NZ councils use which base. The DIA and Productivity Commission sources were blocked. The 71% figure is from journalism reporting the 2019 report.
- Not retrieved: rating bases for Wellington City and Christchurch and the dates they switched; Auckland's later choices after 2012.
- No information found on the NZ Government's 2025–2026 rates-capping proposals; search budget exhausted.

## 4. New Zealand: the valuation system (Valuation Department → Valuation New Zealand → Quotable Value and the Office of the Valuer-General)

### Takeaway
From 1896 NZ had a single government valuation department that produced one roll for land tax, rates and other uses, defining unimproved value, value of improvements and capital value. In 1998 the department was split. A regulator, the Office of the Valuer-General inside LINZ, sets rules and audits. The valuation work became contestable: councils contract providers such as the state-owned Quotable Value (QV) or private firms.

Councils must revalue at least every three years. The Valuer-General audits each revaluation and can refuse to approve release of the new values. Owners can object, with the onus on the objector. The Rating Valuations Rules were under review in 2025, with consultation expected in 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **1896 origins.** The Valuation Department was set up on 17 October 1896 under the Government Valuation of Land Act 1896 — [Wellington City Archives, search extract](https://archivesonline.wcc.govt.nz/nodes/view/7993) [P\*]. The 1896 Act "defined the terms of unimproved and improved value. The improved value was in essence the self-created value, while the unimproved value was that value created by the community and the value of the land in its 'original' condition" — [McCluskey et al. 2002, p. 3](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **First roll and consolidations.** The first general valuation under the 1896 Act was reported in 1898 — [AJHR 1898, search extract](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/parliamentary/AJHR1898-I.2.1.3.27) [P\*]. The 1896 provisions, as amended in 1912, were consolidated in the Valuation of Land Act 1925 — [1948 parliamentary paper, search extract](https://slbrartest.natlib.govt.nz/imageserver/parliamentary/P29pZD1BSkhSMTk0OC1JLjEuMjc1NiZnZXRwZGY9dHJ1ZQ==) [P\*]
- **1904.** The Valuer-General's position was separated from the Commissioner of Taxes under the Public Officers' Appointment and Powers Act 1904 — [Wikipedia, "Valuer-General of New Zealand", search extract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valuer-General_of_New_Zealand) [U, lead only]
- **1998 split.** Valuation New Zealand was disestablished on 30 June 1998 and its functions split between the new Office of the Valuer-General (part of LINZ, which sets standards and audits rating valuations) and Quotable Value New Zealand Ltd — [Wellington City Archives, search extract](https://archivesonline.wcc.govt.nz/nodes/view/7993) [P\*]. QV began as a Crown-owned company on 1 July 1998 and became a state-owned enterprise in January 2005 — [Wikipedia, search extract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotable_Value) [U]. RNZ (2024) describes QV as "the state-owned enterprise Quotable Value" — [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S]
- **Who values today.** "Land Information New Zealand (LINZ) does not carry out property valuations." Councils undertake rating valuations and may contract a valuation service provider "like QV". "Property valuations are done by registered valuers." "Valuations occur at least every three years" — [LINZ, page updated 12 Oct 2022](https://www.linz.govt.nz/regulatory/valuation/property-valuation-new-zealand) [P]
- **Audit.** Before a council releases new values, the Valuer-General checks "the overall accuracy of rating valuations". Reviewers interview valuers and council staff and examine the council's assurance statement, the methodology report (the "basis") and market evidence for five property types (residential, commercial, industrial, rural, lifestyle). The Valuer-General then either approves release or does not, in which case "further work is required to achieve the appropriate standard". A revaluation is done once every three years; roll-maintenance, objection and process audits are targeted — [LINZ, Audits of rating valuations](https://www.linz.govt.nz/guidance/property-valuation/audits-rating-valuations) [P]
- **The three values on a notice.** Capital value is the probable selling price on the valuation date, excluding chattels. Land value is the probable price of the bare land. Improvement value is the difference between the two — [RNZ, 11 Sep 2024](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S]. QV: rating valuations "reflect the likely selling price of a property at the effective revaluation date" and are "usually carried out on all New Zealand properties every three years" — [QV](https://www.qv.co.nz/services/rating-valuations/object-rating/) [P, provider page]
- **What revaluations do and do not change.** Rating values set how the rates "pie" is divided, not its size. "Ratings values are used to determine how the rates pie is split up, not how big the rates pie is" (Brad Olsen, Infometrics) — [RNZ 2024](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S]
- **Objections.** Objections are governed by ss 32–36 of the Rating Valuations Act 1998 and the Rating Valuations Regulations 1998. The onus of proof rests on the objector, and under s 37 the Valuer-General may also object to any valuation — [search extracts of the Act and Regulations](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/secondary-legislation/pco-drafted/1998/177/en/latest/) [P\*]. In practice an objection must be lodged "on or before the date shown on your notice of valuation", is acknowledged within 10 working days and is "referred to a valuer for re-consideration" — [QV](https://www.qv.co.nz/services/rating-valuations/object-rating/) [P]. RNZ gives "about six weeks" from release — [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S]
- **Māori freehold land.** After the Court of Appeal's "Mangatu case", the Valuer-General issued the first Māori freehold land rating-valuation guidelines in 2000. These were reviewed in 2019, with consultation from 10 June to 5 July 2019. The updated guidance adds an allowance for administrative costs under Māori Land Court rules and a larger adjustment reflecting the classes of preferred buyers who must be offered the land before any sale — [LINZ](https://www.linz.govt.nz/guidance/property-valuation/valuation-rules-guidelines-and-publications/rating-valuation-maori-freehold-land-guidance-review-mangatu-adjustments) [P]
- **Rules review (2025–2026).** LINZ and the Valuer-General plan to update the Rating Valuations Rules for "a nationally consistent, impartial, independent, and equitable rating valuation system", reviewing them "in phases over 2025". A final draft is to be formally consulted on, "expected in 2026" (page updated 7 Jul 2025) — [LINZ](https://www.linz.govt.nz/guidance/property-valuation/valuation-rules-guidelines-and-publications/rating-valuations-rules-review) [P]. Status at 4 Nov 2026 not confirmed.
- **Scale.** QV revalued more than 362,000 properties in the 2024 financial year — [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S]

### Inferences
- From 1896 to 1998 NZ ran one government valuation roll for national tax and local rates. After the 1992 end of land tax, valuation became mainly a local-rating service, which may help explain the 1998 split into a regulator and a contestable provider market. This is my inference, not a sourced statement.
- The three-year cycle combined with audit means values can lag the market badly in volatile years (see Section 10).

### Gaps
- The statutory revaluation interval in the Rating Valuations Act 1998 itself, and the effective-date rules, were not read; legislation.govt.nz was blocked. The "at least every three years" rule comes from LINZ and QV.
- Not retrieved: the historical revaluation interval under the Valuation of Land Act 1951 (often said to be five years), and the "Land Valuation Tribunal" appeal stage.
- The year and citation of the Mangatu case were not retrieved.

## 5. Australia: colonial land taxes (which colonies, when, on what base, for what purpose)

### Takeaway
Land taxes came to the Australian colonies late in the nineteenth century, after landowner-dominated parliaments had long blocked them. Most were progressive taxes on unimproved value aimed at large estates. The clearest early case found is Victoria's 1877 tax on large estates, which valued land by how many sheep it could carry rather than by sale price. Reported dates for other colonies (SA 1884, NSW 1895, WA 1907, and Tasmania and Queensland) rest on partial evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **General pattern.** Land taxes were "first introduced by state administrations in the late nineteenth century", after a long period in which parliaments dominated by wealthy landowners blocked them; "In most states land was taxed at progressive rates, based on unimproved value" — [Treasury, Reinhardt & Steel 2006](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/economic-roundup-winter-2006/a-brief-history-of-australias-tax-system) [S]
- **Victoria 1877 (41 Vic. No. 575).** The tax fell on owners of "landed estates", meant to break up large holdings and overseen by a Commissioner of Land Tax. Taxable land was "divided into four classes, according to its estimated grazing capabilities". First class was land "capable of carrying two or more sheep to the acre", "assessed at £4 per acre". The other classes ran down to land carrying less than one sheep per acre; the per-acre values for the lower classes are garbled in the extract. Reports give the thresholds as estates over 640 acres and worth over £2,500, with parcels within a set distance aggregated. A 1906 High Court report mentions parcels "not more than five miles apart" — [Papers Past search extracts, e.g. Otago Witness 1 Feb 1879](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/OW18790201.2.7.4); [Hawke's Bay Herald 30 Jan 1879](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/HBH18790130.2.8); [Globe 30 Aug 1877](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/GLOBE18770830.2.8); [HCA 1906 judgment, search extract](https://www.hcourt.gov.au/sites/default/files/eresources/1906/HCA/8.pdf) [P\*/U]. Sources differ: the 1877 draft bill described the second class as land carrying "three sheep to two acres", and the reports describe the £2,500 exemption differently.
- **South Australia 1884.** Reported as the first Australian colony to tax land on unimproved capital value — [Wikipedia, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_value_tax_in_Australia) [U]. Indirect primary-source support: the 1924 NZ paper calls SA's 1884 "Land and Income Tax Act" the model for NZ's 1891 Act — [AJHR 1924, search extract](https://dynamic-rsnz.natlib.govt.nz/imageserver/parliamentary/P29pZD1BSkhSMTkyNC1JLjEuMTEzNCZnZXRwZGY9dHJ1ZQ==) [P\*]
- **NSW and WA.** The Commonwealth's Land Tax Assessment Act 1910 cites "N.S.W., 59 Vic. No. 15" (s 10, the charge on unimproved value, and the definition of "absentee") and "W.A. 1907, No. 16" as models — [LTAA 1910, margin notes](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P; OCR partly garbled]
- **Purpose.** The Victorian 1877 tax aimed to break up large holdings — [search extracts above](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/OW18790201.2.7.4) [P\*]. The federal 1910 tax had the same stated aim — [Treasury 2006](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/economic-roundup-winter-2006/a-brief-history-of-australias-tax-system) [S].

### Tax profile — Victoria's 1877 Land Tax
- **What was taxed:** large "landed estates", not all land.
- **Valued how:** by a classification of carrying capacity. Land was graded by how many sheep per acre it could carry, and each class was deemed to have a set capital value per acre (first class £4/acre). This is an area- and yield-based valuation, not a sale-price valuation — [extracts above] [P\*].
- **Valued by whom:** a Commissioner of Land Tax classified the land. The 1906 High Court case concerned classification and part-sales of estates — [HCA 1906, search extract](https://www.hcourt.gov.au/sites/default/files/eresources/1906/HCA/8.pdf) [P\*].
- **Rate:** not confirmed. Two 1879 reports on William John Clarke's six estates give a valuation of £479,227 (or £497,227) and annual tax of £5,846 (or £5,546) — [NZ newspaper search extracts, 1879](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/PBH18790131.2.6) [P\*/U, figures conflict].

### Inferences
- Clarke's reported tax-to-value ratio, about 1.1–1.2% depending on which figures are used, suggests a flat rate of roughly 1–1.25% on the deemed classified value. This is my inference and needs checking against the statute.
- Victoria's 1877 method shows a pre-market-valuation approach to "land value": a statutory per-acre value attached to a physical capability class. Later Australian taxes replaced it with sale-price-based unimproved value (see the 1910 definitions in Section 6).

### Gaps
- No primary text read for the SA 1884, NSW 1895, Tasmanian, WA 1907 or Queensland statutes. Rates, thresholds and dates (Tasmania 1880, Queensland 1915) are unverified.
- Not retrieved: NSW's 1906 arrangement under which the state land tax was suspended where councils levied unimproved-value rates, and NSW's 1956 reintroduction (Land Tax Management Act 1956). These are leads from background knowledge, unverified.

## 6. Australia: the Commonwealth land tax, 1910–1952/53

### Takeaway
The 1910 Commonwealth land tax was explicitly progressive on unimproved value and aimed at large estates. Wikipedia, a lead only, attributes it to the Fisher Labor government, which is consistent with the November 1910 assent. It exempted the first £5,000 of value for resident owners. Marginal rates rose smoothly from 1d to 6d in the pound, and absentees paid more with no exemption. Owners valued their own land in annual returns, backed by Commissioner valuations, appeals to the courts, and a power to buy land at the owner's own understated value plus 10%. Valuations were the most contested part of the scheme. Treasury attributes the 1952 abolition to the tax's declining effectiveness, primary-producer exemptions narrowing it to urban land, unstable revenue, and poor integration with income tax.

### Cited Findings
- **Title and start.** "An Act to impose a Progressive Land Tax upon Unimproved Values", Land Tax Act 1910, No. 21 of 1910, "[Assented to 16th November, 1910.]". Levied for the financial year beginning 1 July 1910 and each year after — [Land Tax Act 1910](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00021/asmade/1910-11-16/text/original/word) [P]
- **Rates for resident (non-absentee) owners, First Schedule.** On taxable value up to £75,000 the rate is "One penny where the taxable value is One pound sterling" and increases uniformly, so the increment of tax is 2d at £15,000, 3d at £30,000, 4d at £45,000, 5d at £60,000 and 6d at £75,000. "For every pound sterling of taxable value in excess of £75,000 the rate of tax shall be Sixpence" — [Land Tax Act 1910](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00021/asmade/1910-11-16/text/original/word) [P]
- **Rates for absentees, Second Schedule.** 1d per £ on the first £5,000. Above £5,000, 2d at the first pound of excess, rising uniformly to 7d at £80,000. "For every pound sterling of taxable value in excess of £80,000 the rate of tax shall be Sevenpence" — [Land Tax Act 1910](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00021/asmade/1910-11-16/text/original/word) [P]
- **Exemption.** Taxable value is (a) for an absentee, "the total sum of the unimproved value of each parcel"; (b) for any other owner, "the balance ... after deducting the sum of five thousand pounds" — [Land Tax Assessment Act 1910, s 11(2)](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]. Conflict: Wikipedia says the exemption was "the first £3,000" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Tax_Act_1910) [U]. The statute governs: £5,000.
- **Who counts as an absentee:** "a person who does not reside in Australia" — [LTAA 1910, s 3](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]
- **Statutory definitions (valuation basis).** "'Unimproved value,' in relation to land, means the capital sum which the fee simple of the land might be expected to realize if offered for sale on such reasonable terms and conditions as a bona fide seller would require, assuming that the improvements (if any) thereon or appertaining thereto and made or acquired by the owner or his predecessor in title had not been made." "'Value of improvements' ... means the added value which the improvements give to the land at the date of valuation irrespective of the cost of the improvements" — [LTAA 1910, s 3](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]
- **Self-assessment by returns.** Every taxpayer must each year furnish returns of all land owned "at noon on the thirtieth day of June" and "of the improved value and unimproved value of every parcel". After a full return, the owner may file supplementary returns of acquisitions and disposals in each of the next two years. In effect owners re-valued everything every third year — [LTAA 1910, s 15](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]
- **Commissioner's valuations:** "The Commissioner may, if, as, and when he thinks fit, make or cause to be made valuations of any land", and "may obtain and use ... any valuations made by or for any State or any authority constituted under a State". Assessments are made from returns, valuations and other information "whether any return has been furnished or not" — [LTAA 1910, ss 17–18](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]
- **Appeals:** to the High Court in its original jurisdiction or a State Supreme Court. The court may reduce or increase the assessment — [LTAA 1910, ss 44–46](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]
- **Compulsory acquisition at the owner's own value (s 48).** "For the protection of the revenue against the undervaluation of land": if an owner understated unimproved value by "twenty-five per centum or more" and a High Court Justice was "not satisfied that the undervaluation was not made with a view to evading taxation", the Commonwealth could acquire the land. Compensation was "the unimproved value stated in the return" plus the fair value of improvements, "together with the amount of ten per centum upon that improved value, by way of an allowance for compulsory dispossession". The State could take the land over within three months. The margin cites "N.Z., 1908, No. 95, s. 33" — [LTAA 1910, s 48](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]
- **Purpose.** Introduced federally in 1910 "as a form of wealth tax and as a means to break up large tracts of under-utilised land" — [Treasury 2006](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/economic-roundup-winter-2006/a-brief-history-of-australias-tax-system) [S]. Possible conflict: the fetch tool's summary of the same article has it saying the tax was "introduced as a flat rate tax", which contradicts the Act's title and schedules. This may be an extraction error; the statute governs [U].
- **Administration and early disputes (lead only).** A Commissioner of Land Taxation was appointed with an office in Treasury. Returns were sent out 10 Jan 1911, were due 1 Mar 1911, and assessments were issued by 21 Apr 1911. About 15,000 returns were assessed in the first year. Revenue was about £1.3–1.4 million a year in the first three years. Over 1,800 appeals and objections were lodged by mid-1913. Holdings were split among relatives, partners or companies to get under the threshold — [Wikipedia, citing Leigh Edmonds, "The 1910s: Laying the Foundations", *A brief history of the Australian Taxation Office* (ATO, 2010), pp. 5–22](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Tax_Act_1910) [U: ATO PDF blocked]. "Land valuations brought loud complaints and became the most contentious aspect of the new tax" — [search extract of a Wikipedia mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Tax_Act_1910) [U]
- **Abolition and reasons.** "In 1952, land taxes were abolished at the federal level". Reasons given: the tax became less effective as wealth diversified beyond land; it ignored net property wealth (mortgage debt); primary producers were largely exempted, "restrict[ing] the application of land tax to urban property"; revenue "became less stable, susceptible to the fluctuations of town property markets"; and "land taxes were also unpopular as the federal and state taxes were not well integrated with income taxes" — [Treasury, Reinhardt & Steel, 4 Sep 2006](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/economic-roundup-winter-2006/a-brief-history-of-australias-tax-system) [S]. Wikipedia says "Federal land taxes ran until 30 June 1952" — [Wikipedia, "Land value tax in Australia", lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_value_tax_in_Australia) [U]. Wikipedia instead gives repeal on 1 April 1953 by a "Land Tax Abolition Act 1953" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Tax_Act_1910) [U]
- **Later rate formulas.** Amending Acts changed the formulas, e.g. 1914 ("R = 2 + E/18,750" pence for value above £5,000, apparently the absentee schedule since E is the excess over £5,000) and 1940 (a restated formula for £5,000–£80,000) — [Federal Register search extracts: 1914 Act](https://legislation.gov.au/C1914A00028/asmade/1914-12-21/text/original/pdf); [1940 Act](https://legislation.gov.au/C1940A00016/asmade/1940-05-27/text/original/pdf) [P\*]

### Tax profile — Commonwealth land tax
- **What was taxed:** unimproved value of all land owned, aggregated per owner, above £5,000 for residents and from £1 for absentees — [LTAA 1910](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P].
- **Valued how and by whom:** a hypothetical sale price excluding the owner's improvements. Owners self-assessed in returns; the Commissioner could value or adopt State valuations — [P].
- **Updated how often:** owner returns annually, with full returns every three years in effect — [s 15] [P]. Actual practice not retrieved.
- **Rates:** 1d rising to 6d per £ (residents), 1d and 2d rising to 7d (absentees) — [P]. Note 1d/£ = 0.42% and 6d/£ = 2.5%.
- **Revenue:** about £1.3–1.4m a year in 1910–13 [U]. No share of Commonwealth revenue was retrieved.
- **Exemptions:** the £5,000 deduction, plus s 13 exemptions (text not read in full). Primary-producer exemptions narrowed the tax over time — [Treasury 2006](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/economic-roundup-winter-2006/a-brief-history-of-australias-tax-system) [S].
- **What happened to valuations:** they were contested from the start (over 1,800 appeals or objections by mid-1913 [U]). The law's backstop was the s 48 threat of purchase at the owner's own value.

### Inferences
- Self-assessment backed by a threat of purchase at the declared price, plus court appeal, was the 1910 answer to the valuation problem. It shifted the cost of valuation to owners and gave them a reason not to understate.
- Holdings were reportedly split to escape the progressive scale [U]. This would have eroded the tax's anti-aggregation aim. The Henry Review's later recommendation to tax per holding rather than on aggregated holdings turns that logic around (Section 9).

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: revenue by year (1910–1952) and its share of Commonwealth taxation; the number of objections and appeals; the 1952–53 abolition statute and its exact effective date. The ATO history and Year Books were blocked or unsearchable.
- High Court challenges to the tax (e.g. *Osborne v Commonwealth* (1911)) were not retrieved; lead only.

## 7. Australia: state land taxes today (base, thresholds, rates, aggregation, exemptions, revenue)

### Takeaway
Every state and the ACT raises land tax revenue; the Northern Territory records none. The tax is on aggregated land holdings above a threshold, at progressive rates, on the Valuer-General's land value. NSW calls it "land value" but describes it to taxpayers as unimproved value; Victoria uses site value; Queensland uses site or unimproved value. Exemptions for the principal place of residence and for primary-production land were confirmed for NSW in this session; other states were not checked. In 2024-25 state land taxes raised $20.0bn, about 14% of state government taxation, and local municipal rates raised a further $25.1bn. Land tax revenue nearly tripled between 2015-16 and 2024-25.

Recent changes:
- Victoria cut its threshold to $50,000 for 2024–2033.
- NSW froze its thresholds from 2025.
- Foreign-owner surcharges have risen, to 5% in NSW from 2025.

## 8. Australia: valuation by Valuers-General, and the base each state's councils use for rates

### Takeaway
State Valuers-General value land en masse from analysed sales, preferring sales of vacant land. NSW sets values as at 1 July each year; Queensland reviews selected council areas each year. The legal concepts differ:
- **Unimproved value:** land "in its natural, undisturbed state". Used for rural land in Queensland and in the Commonwealth's 1910 tax.
- **Site value:** land excluding buildings, structures, landscaping and services. Land works such as clearing, filling and levelling are handled separately; in Queensland their cost may be deducted if incurred within the past 12 years. Used in Victoria for land tax, and in Queensland for non-rural land.
- **Land value:** NSW's term.

Some taxes use capital improved value (land plus buildings): Victoria's VRLT, and many councils' rates. NSW averages three years of values for land tax. Queensland reviews selected council areas each year rather than all. For the historical anchor, the federal 1910 definition of unimproved value, see Section 6.

### Cited Findings
- **Queensland.** "Land zoned rural gets an unimproved value. All other land, including rural-residential land and unzoned land, gets a site value" — [Qld Government, methods](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/methods) [P]. Unimproved value values the land "as if it's in its natural, undisturbed state, without any structural or land improvements". Site value excludes buildings, fencing, dams, landscaping, excavations, pools, irrigation works and services. Site improvements such as clearing, filling or levelling paid for within the past 12 years may be deductible — [Qld Government, rural vs non-rural](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/rural-vs-nonrural) [P]
- **Queensland method.** Mass appraisal "assesses values for many properties at a common date". Valuers prefer sales of "vacant or lightly improved properties" and exclude family sales. "Each year, the Valuer-General reviews land values in selected local government areas around the state". "All valuations go through a quality assurance and review process ... in accordance with the Land Valuation Act 2010" — [Qld Government, methods](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/methods) [P]. Valuations feed council rates, state land tax and state land rental — [Qld Government, rates and land tax](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/rates-land-tax) [P]
- **NSW.** Values are set by the Valuer General as at 1 July each year; land tax uses a three-year average; objections go to the Valuer General — [Revenue NSW](https://www.revenue.nsw.gov.au/taxes-duties-levies-royalties/land-tax/understanding-land-tax/how-land-tax-is-calculated) [P]. The statutory definition of "land value" (Valuation of Land Act 1916 s 6A) could not be read (AustLII and NSW legislation blocked) [gap]
- **Victoria.** Land tax uses site value — [SRO Victoria](https://sro.vic.gov.au/about-us/rates-and-statistics/current-rates/land-tax-current-rates) [P]. VRLT uses capital improved value — [SRO Victoria](https://sro.vic.gov.au/about-us/rates-and-statistics/current-rates/vacant-residential-land-tax-current-rates) [P]. Whether a property falls under CIPT depends on the Australian Valuation Property Classification Code (AVPCC) "as coded by the Valuer-General". Codes in the 200s, 300s, 400s and 600s (commercial, industrial, extractive, infrastructure and utilities) qualify — [DTF Victoria](https://www.dtf.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-10/Commercial-and-Industrial-Property-Tax-Reform-Information-Sheet.pdf) [P]. The windfall gains tax lists the Valuation of Land Act 1960 among its governing legislation — [SRO Victoria](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/windfall-gains-tax) [P]
- **Councils' rating base by state.** Retrieved only for Queensland, where the Valuer-General's site or unimproved values are used by councils — [Qld Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/rates-land-tax) [P]. For other states, see Gaps.

### Inferences
- The legal "land value" is a counterfactual, valued as if buildings (and sometimes land works) did not exist. That is why valuers lean on vacant-land sales and why such values are hard to verify in fully built-up areas. Queensland's preference for "vacant or lightly improved" sales states this directly.

## 9. Reforms: ACT's duty-to-rates switch, NSW First Home Buyer Choice, Victoria's CIPT, the Henry Review, and 2025–2026 changes

### Takeaway
The Henry Review (dated December 2009) recommended replacing conveyance stamp duty with a broad land tax, ideally on all land, with rates rising according to value per square metre, levied per holding. Three jurisdictions tried versions:
- **ACT, from 2012 (start year not confirmed in a primary source):** a 20-year shift from duty to land-value-based general rates. General rates are forecast at $944.2m in 2026-27. A July 2026 review found the shift had stalled. The ACT abolished duty for first home buyers from 1 July 2026.
- **NSW:** an opt-in annual property tax for first home buyers, open only from 16 January to 30 June 2023.
- **Victoria:** from 1 July 2024, commercial and industrial property pays duty one last time, then a flat 1% annual CIPT on unimproved land value after 10 years (first liabilities in 2034–35).

### Cited Findings — Henry Review
- **Recommendation 51:** "Ideally, there would be no role for any stamp duties, including conveyancing stamp duties, in a modern Australian tax system. Recognising the revenue needs of the States, the removal of stamp duty should be achieved through a switch to more efficient taxes, such as those levied on broad consumption or land bases. Increasing land tax at the same time as reducing stamp duty has the additional benefit of some offsetting impacts on asset prices" — [Henry Review Final Report, Part One (dated Dec 2009), List of recommendations](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-10/afts_final_report_part_1_consolidated.pdf) [P]
- **Recommendation 52:** "Given the efficiency benefits of a broad land tax, it should be levied on as broad a base as possible. In order to tax more valuable land at higher rates, consideration should be given to levying land tax using an increasing marginal rate schedule, with the lowest rate being zero, with thresholds determined by the per-square-metre value" — [same](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-10/afts_final_report_part_1_consolidated.pdf) [P]
- **Recommendation 53:** "In the long run, the land tax base should be broadened to eventually include all land. If this occurs, low-value land, such as most agricultural land, would not face a land tax liability where its value per square metre is below the lowest rate threshold" — [same](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-10/afts_final_report_part_1_consolidated.pdf) [P]
- **Recommendation 54:** incremental reforms, including "(a) ensuring that land tax applies per land holding, not on an entity's total holding, in order to promote investment in land development; (b) eliminating stamp duties on commercial and industrial properties in return for a broad land tax on those properties; and (c) investigating various transitional arrangements necessary to achieve a broader land tax" — [same](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-10/afts_final_report_part_1_consolidated.pdf) [P]
- **Efficiency evidence:** KPMG Econtech modelling for the Review estimated that "welfare losses of municipal rates and land tax are lower than, or similar to, those of the GST, and a lot lower than for personal tax on labour income and company income tax". In Chart 1.5's ordering, from highest to lowest welfare loss per dollar, conveyancing stamp duties sit above labour income tax. Land taxes and municipal rates sit near the bottom, with only the petroleum resource rent tax lower. The numeric values were not legible in the text extraction. The Review warns that results "are only indicative" and cannot capture "the full range of efficiency costs associated with conveyance stamp duties" — [Henry Review Part One, Box 1.1](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-10/afts_final_report_part_1_consolidated.pdf) [P]
- **Government response:** a search extract attributes to a local-government news report the statement that the Rudd Government rejected the expansion of land tax to all landowners. The originating article was not pinned down; the candidate is [governmentnews.com.au](https://www.governmentnews.com.au/?p=5806) [U]. The primary government response was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings — ACT (duty to general rates, 2012– )
- **Revenue mix, 2024-25:** ACT general rates (ABS "municipal rates") $812m and conveyance duty $330m, out of ACT taxation of $2,754m. In 2015-16 the figures were $423m, $286m and $1,568m — [ABS Table 9](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/government/taxation-revenue-australia/2024-25/55060DO001_202425.xlsx) [P]
- **Pegasus review, reported 3 Jul 2026.** The review, commissioned by the Legislative Assembly's budget estimates committee, found eliminating conveyance duty by the original deadline "extremely unlikely" without "some dramatic changes in forthcoming budgets". Its findings:
  - conveyance duty would still be about 10% of own-source revenue in 2029-30;
  - "general rates is forecast to remain flat at around 29 per cent from 2025-26 onwards";
  - the substitution "appears to have come to a complete stop";

  The article says the government has "only two years left in the original 20-year timetable" — [Canberra Times, Jasper Lindell](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9303822/act-stamp-duty-phase-not-on-track-to-meet-timetable-review-says/) [S]
- **2026-27 Budget, 10 Jun 2026.** From 1 July 2026, stamp duty is abolished for first home buyers buying a home to live in, described as making the ACT "the first jurisdiction in Australia to permanently eliminate stamp duty for first home buyers". Relief extends to eligible pensioners, eligible NDIS participants and people who have not owned property in the previous five years. The off-the-plan owner-occupier concession becomes permanent. Duty is removed from new unit-titled and turn-key units. ### Cited Findings — Victoria's Commercial and Industrial Property Tax

### Inferences
- The three Australian experiments differ in how they handle the transition from duty, a one-off tax on the buyer, to a recurrent tax on land value:
  - The ACT raises rates on everyone gradually while cutting duty rates.
  - NSW let individual buyers choose, one property at a time.
  - Victoria switches property by property at the next sale, with a 10-year lag and an optional loan.
- The ACT case suggests that the political ceiling on annual rates increases, rather than valuation itself, became the binding constraint on the switch (Pegasus: rates rises "a few percentage points above wage growth" are insufficient). This reading rests on one journalistic report of the review [S].

## 10. What happened to valuations over time (revaluations, freezes, disputes, changes of base)

### Takeaway
In both countries, fights over "land value" were fights over three things: the valuation base, who sets the value, and how fast values follow the market. Six episodes illustrate this:
- **Self-assessment.** Owners' own returns, policed by threat of purchase (Australia, 1910).
- **A single state roll.** NZ's single government roll from 1896, split into an auditor and contestable providers in 1998.
- **Smoothing.** NSW's three-year averaging, and NSW threshold freezes from 2025.
- **Lagging cycles.** NZ councils revalue every three years, so values set at a market peak lingered in 2022–24.
- **Changes of base.** Auckland's statutory move to capital value in 2012 redistributed bills sharply.
- **Stalled substitution.** The ACT's duty-to-rates switch stalled when rates rises became the binding limit.

### Cited Findings
- **Self-assessment and acquisition (Cth 1910):** see Section 6 — [LTAA 1910, ss 15, 17, 44, 48](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]. Disputes were numerous (over 1,800 appeals or objections by mid-1913) — [Wikipedia citing ATO history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Tax_Act_1910) [U]
- **NZ valuation administration:** valuation for land tax and rates moved into a separate department after the 1896 rating reforms made earlier arrangements "unworkable" — [Press 1932, search extract](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19320521.2.109) [P\*]. It was split in 1998 — [WCC Archives, search extract](https://archivesonline.wcc.govt.nz/nodes/view/7993) [P\*]. The Valuer-General can withhold approval of a revaluation — [LINZ](https://www.linz.govt.nz/guidance/property-valuation/audits-rating-valuations) [P]
- **Values set at the peak (NZ):** in September 2024 the average NZ home was valued at $905,357, "down 15 percent from the market's peak at the end of 2021". Some regions had rating values set at the peak, and Bayleys' Chris Farhi said it was "very likely" some regions would see "significant reductions", with Auckland and Wellington likely the biggest corrections. Capital values "have become less important overall". Christchurch and Dunedin were last valued in 2022 — [RNZ, 11 Sep 2024](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S]
- **Change of base (NZ):** nine councils moved from land to capital value between 1989 and 2000, and none moved the other way — [McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]. Auckland's 2012 move redistributed bills (Section 3) — [DIA RIS 2012](https://regulation.govt.nz/assets/RIS-Documents/ris-dia-acrar-may12.pdf) [P]. By 2019, 71% of councils were on capital value — [ODT](https://www.odt.co.nz/regions/wanaka/commission-councils-setting-rates-wrong) [S]
- **Change of terminology (NZ):** "land value" replaced "unimproved value" in 1970 — [McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]
- **Averaging and freezes (NSW):** three-year averaging of Valuer General land values for land tax; general and premium thresholds frozen from the 2025 land tax year — [Revenue NSW](https://www.revenue.nsw.gov.au/taxes-duties-levies-royalties/land-tax/understanding-land-tax/how-land-tax-is-calculated); [Revenue NSW thresholds](https://www.revenue.nsw.gov.au/taxes-duties-levies-royalties/land-tax/understanding-land-tax/thresholds-and-rates) [P]
- **Partial annual coverage (Qld):** "Each year, the Valuer-General reviews land values in selected local government areas" — [Qld Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/methods) [P]
- **Abandonment of a land-value tax (NZ):** the national land tax was narrowed (the $175,000 exemption) and abolished from 1 April 1992 — [P\* sources in Section 2]

### Inferences
- Averaging (NSW) and three-year cycles (NZ) both trade accuracy for stability. They delay both rises and falls in taxable values relative to the market. The RNZ report shows the downside in a falling market: bills set on peak values while sale prices drop.

### Gaps
- No primary account retrieved of valuation freezes in Australian states, of court cases that overturned mass valuations, or of SA's re-inspection program (leads only; see Section 8).

## 11. Plain-language explanations of key concepts

- **Unimproved value.** What the land would sell for if the owner's buildings and other improvements had never been made. The Commonwealth's 1910 definition: "the capital sum which the fee simple of the land might be expected to realize if offered for sale on such reasonable terms and conditions as a bona fide seller would require, assuming that the improvements ... had not been made" — [LTAA 1910, s 3](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]. Queensland applies it to rural land "as if it's in its natural, undisturbed state" — [Qld Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/rural-vs-nonrural) [P].
- **Site value.** Land value excluding buildings and structures, and excluding things like services and landscaping (Queensland's list), while recognising that the land sits in a serviced, developed setting. Used in Victoria for land tax and in Queensland for non-rural land — [SRO Vic](https://sro.vic.gov.au/about-us/rates-and-statistics/current-rates/land-tax-current-rates); [Qld Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/rural-vs-nonrural) [P].
- **Land value (NZ, NSW).** NZ's name since 1970 for what had been "unimproved value" — [McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]. NZ notices show it as "the probable price of the bare land" — [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S]. NSW's statutory "land value" was not read.
- **Capital value or capital improved value (CV/CIV).** The value of land plus buildings and other improvements, i.e. the likely sale price of the whole property — [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S].
- **Annual value or rental value.** A tax base equal to the yearly rent a property could fetch: the "English system" of rating, used in NZ in the 1840s — [McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf) [S]. Australian variants (NAV in Victoria, AAV in Tasmania, GRV in WA) are not defined from primary sources here.
- **Value of improvements.** The value buildings and works add to the land "irrespective of the cost of the improvements" — [LTAA 1910](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]. In NZ it is capital value minus land value — [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S].
- **Mass appraisal.** Valuing many properties at once at a common date from analysed sales, instead of one-by-one inspection — [Qld Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/title/valuation/about/methods) [P].
- **Revaluation cycle.** How often the official values are redone: at least every three years for NZ rates — [LINZ](https://www.linz.govt.nz/regulatory/valuation/property-valuation-new-zealand) [P]; annually for NSW land values — [Revenue NSW](https://www.revenue.nsw.gov.au/taxes-duties-levies-royalties/land-tax/understanding-land-tax/how-land-tax-is-calculated) [P].
- **Averaging.** Taxing the average of several years' values to smooth jumps. NSW land tax uses three years — [Revenue NSW](https://www.revenue.nsw.gov.au/taxes-duties-levies-royalties/land-tax/understanding-land-tax/how-land-tax-is-calculated) [P].
- **Aggregation.** Adding up all of an owner's taxable land before applying the threshold and the progressive scale. This makes large holders pay higher rates — [Revenue NSW](https://www.revenue.nsw.gov.au/taxes-duties-levies-royalties/land-tax/understanding-land-tax/how-land-tax-is-calculated) [P]. The Henry Review preferred taxing each holding separately — [Henry Rec. 54(a)](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-10/afts_final_report_part_1_consolidated.pdf) [P].
- **Threshold and progressive (marginal) rates.** No tax below a threshold, and rising percentages on value above it (e.g. NSW 1.6%, then 2% above the premium threshold) — [Revenue NSW](https://www.revenue.nsw.gov.au/taxes-duties-levies-royalties/land-tax/understanding-land-tax/thresholds-and-rates) [P]. In pre-decimal money, "1d in the £" means one penny per pound of value, about 0.42%.
- **Graduated land tax.** NZ's 1891-style extra tax on larger estates, rising with estate size — [AJHR 1924, search extract](https://dynamic-rsnz.natlib.govt.nz/imageserver/parliamentary/P29pZD1BSkhSMTkyNC1JLjEuMTEzNCZnZXRwZGY9dHJ1ZQ==) [P\*].
- **Absentee or foreign-owner surcharge.** Higher land tax for owners living overseas. Examples: NZ +50% on the graduated scale from 1903 [P\*]; the Commonwealth 1910 absentee schedule [P]; NSW 5% from 2025 [P]; Victoria +4 percentage points [P].
- **Conveyance (stamp) duty.** A one-off tax on buying property, charged on the price. The Henry Review argued it should be replaced by land tax — [Henry Review](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-10/afts_final_report_part_1_consolidated.pdf) [P].
- **Uniform annual general charge (UAGC, NZ).** A flat per-property charge alongside value-based rates, capped at 30% of certain rates revenue — [LGNZ 2020](https://www.lgnz.co.nz/documents/168/Prod-Com-funding-review-analysis.pdf) [P].
- **Revaluation audit and objection.** In NZ the Valuer-General audits each council revaluation before release — [LINZ](https://www.linz.govt.nz/guidance/property-valuation/audits-rating-valuations) [P]. Owners may object by the date on the notice, and a valuer reconsiders — [QV](https://www.qv.co.nz/services/rating-valuations/object-rating/) [P].
- **AVPCC.** The Australian Valuation Property Classification Code, a land-use code the Valuer-General attaches to each property. Victoria uses it to decide which properties enter CIPT — [DTF Victoria](https://www.dtf.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-10/Commercial-and-Industrial-Property-Tax-Reform-Information-Sheet.pdf) [P].

## 12. Scenes and anecdotes (sourced)

1. **"Buy it at your own price" (Australia, 1910).** The Commonwealth's first land tax let landowners write down their own land values, with a catch. If the Commissioner thought an owner had understated unimproved value by 25% or more, he could go to a High Court Justice. Unless the Justice was satisfied that the undervaluation was not aimed at evading tax, the Commonwealth could take the land, paying only the owner's own declared figure plus improvements and 10% "by way of an allowance for compulsory dispossession". The margin of the statute credits the idea to New Zealand — [Land Tax Assessment Act 1910, s 48](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf) [P]
2. **Counting sheep to value land (Victoria, 1877).** Victoria's first land tax did not ask what an estate would sell for. Assessors classed land by how many sheep an acre could carry: two or more sheep to the acre made first-class land, deemed to be worth £4 an acre. Newspapers across the Tasman printed the bills of the great pastoralists. William John Clarke's six estates were reported at about £479,000 to £497,000, with an annual tax of about £5,500 to £5,800; the reports disagree on both figures — [Papers Past search extracts, 1879](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/OW18790201.2.7.4); [PBH18790131.2.6](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/PBH18790131.2.6) [P\*/U]
3. **The halfpenny debate (New Zealand, 1878).** As Parliament debated the colony's first land tax, the rate was cut from a penny to a halfpenny. Major Atkinson argued that at a halfpenny the Government would not be able to raise more than £208,000 (the wording is from a search extract). By December 1879 the land tax had been replaced by a general property tax, remembered as "Harry Atkinson's property tax" (Te Ara cartoon title, lead only) — [Papers Past 1878 search extracts](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/WT18780829.2.13); [Property Assessment Act 1879, search extract](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1879/19/en/1879-12-19/official_scan.pdf) [P\*]
4. **Auckland's 2012 reset.** When Parliament required the new Auckland Council to rate the whole region on capital value from 1 July 2012, the council's modelling showed what a change of base can do on its own. Without a transition policy, 112,239 ratepayers faced increases above 15%, while 111,863 would have seen falls of more than 10%. Some of the largest increases fell on "large utilities previously rated on land value" — [DIA RIS, 16 Apr 2012](https://regulation.govt.nz/assets/RIS-Documents/ris-dia-acrar-may12.pdf) [P]
5. **"A complete stop" (Canberra, 2026).** Fourteen years into a 20-year plan to swap stamp duty for land-value-based rates, an independent review for the ACT Legislative Assembly found that the substitution "appears to have come to a complete stop". General rates were forecast to sit at about 29% of own-source revenue indefinitely. Weeks earlier the same government had abolished duty outright for first home buyers — [Canberra Times, 3 Jul 2026](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9303822/act-stamp-duty-phase-not-on-track-to-meet-timetable-review-says/); [Grant Thornton](https://www.grantthornton.com.au/insights/client-alerts/act-state-budget-2026-27/) [S]
6. **Valued at the top (New Zealand, 2024).** Many NZ homeowners were paying rates on values struck near the 2021 market peak. By September 2024 the average home was 15% below that peak, and valuers expected "significant reductions" when the 2024 round of rating revaluations, applying from July 2025, was released — [RNZ, 11 Sep 2024](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/527715/rateable-values-market-values-and-rates-what-you-need-to-know) [S]
