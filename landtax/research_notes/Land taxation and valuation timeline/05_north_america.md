# North America (United States and Canada), 1879 to 4 November 2026: land value taxation, land valuation, and valuation freezes

**Scope.** These notes cover US and Canadian experiments with taxing land more heavily than buildings, how North American assessors have valued land, and the valuation freezes and caps that shaped the field. Coverage ends on 4 November 2026.

**Confidence labels used throughout**
- **[P]** Confirmed in a primary source: a statute or legislative record, a court judgment, an official or agency publication, a contemporaneous official report (Haig 1915), or the original author's own book or memoir.
- **[S]** Secondary source only: peer-reviewed or scholarly work, or reputable journalism.
- **[U]** Unresolved. Either the claim appears only in an advocacy source, or the sources conflict, or I could not access the primary record.

**Research-process caveat.** The shared web-search budget ran out partway through this task. After that point I used only direct fetches of known URLs. Some 2025–2026 items (Virginia bill history, other states' bills, Christopher Berry's national paper, Ontario's plans after 2026) therefore rest on fewer sources than I wanted. Each such item is flagged below and listed in the Access log.

## 1. Context: Henry George and the single-tax movement (brief)

### Takeaway
Henry George's *Progress and Poverty* (1879) gave North American reformers both a programme (take land rent through taxation and exempt buildings) and a slogan ("the single tax"). Almost every experiment below was led by self-described single-taxers. None of them, however, produced a tax system that relied on land alone.

### Cited Findings
- Full reference: Henry George, *Progress and Poverty: An Inquiry into the Cause of Industrial Depressions and of Increase of Want with Increase of Wealth* (San Francisco: author's edition, 1879; New York: D. Appleton, 1880). [P: the book itself]
- Fairhope, Alabama, was founded in 1894 by members of a single-tax club from Des Moines, Iowa, who based their colony on George's theories in *Progress and Poverty* — [Fairhope Single Tax Corporation, home page](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/); [Fairhope history page](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/fairhope-history). [S: the corporation's own account]
- Houston's tax commissioner J. J. Pastoriza is described by Haig (1915) as "an ardent single-taxer." According to Haig, Pastoriza had pledged that once he made a comfortable fortune he would retire and "devote his energy to forwarding the cause of Henry George." He sold his printing business at 49 and ran for office in 1911 — Robert Murray Haig, *The Exemption of Improvements from Taxation in Canada and the United States* (New York: Committee on Taxation of the City of New York, 1915), p. 242, [archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- Haig judged in 1915 that even in the most advanced Canadian city, "the result has nowhere been that all revenues are collected from land … The single tax is, therefore, not really in existence." He cited a rough calculation by Archibald Stalker, using 1911 figures, that only about fifty per cent of all taxes paid by Edmonton residents came from land once provincial and Dominion taxes were counted — Haig 1915, p. 263, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- Cleveland mayor Tom L. Johnson, a Georgist, wrote that his first acts as mayor included establishing "a Tax School designed to show the inequalities in taxation" — Tom L. Johnson, *My Story*, ed. Elizabeth J. Hauser (New York: B. W. Huebsch, 1911), p. 125, [archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml). [P: memoir]

### Inferences
- In North America, "single tax" in practice meant something narrower: municipal exemption, or reduced assessment, of buildings (and often personal property) inside the local property tax. Provincial, state and federal taxes were left untouched.

### Gaps
- I did not research the national single-tax organisations (for example the Joseph Fels Fund or the Single Tax Review) or US state-level single-tax ballot campaigns, such as those in Oregon and California in the 1910s. These are outside the questions asked.

## 2. The Somers system of land valuation, and Cleveland under Tom L. Johnson

### Takeaway
William A. Somers of St Paul, Minnesota, devised a way to value urban land separately from buildings. Citizen committees fixed a public "unit" value: one front foot of land at a standard depth, placed in the middle of each block. Printed depth and corner tables then converted unit values into a value for every lot. Buildings were valued separately, from replacement cost and condition. Cleveland used the system from 1901–02 under Mayor Tom L. Johnson, and Houston adapted a "unit-value system" in 1911–12.

### Cited Findings
- Somers published *The Valuation of Real Estate for the Purpose of Taxation* (St. Paul, Minn.: Rich & Clymer, printers, copyright 1901). The title page carries the maxim "To ascertain the value of anything ask one who knows. His answer will be what he thinks it worth, based upon his thinking that he knows what others think it worth" — [archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich). [P]
- **Core idea, "Community Opinion."** Somers argued that in cities there is no shared opinion of what individual lots are worth, but there is a shared opinion "of the relative value of streets." He used that opinion as the basis for valuation — Somers 1901, pp. ~18–20, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich). [P]
- **The unit.** "The value of one foot in width for some fixed depth is the best measure for this purpose." Assuming "in every case that the unit of one foot frontage is located in the center of a block … the corner influence, will be entirely eliminated." Committees of citizens mark unit values on maps, beginning with the most valuable central district and working outward. The maps are to be "made records accessible to all citizens and taxpayers" — Somers 1901, pp. 19–21, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich). [P]
- **Depth and corner tables.** Committees set ratios for lots of other depths, and these become "scales." The book's retail-district example uses a unit value of $1,000 per front foot at 100 feet deep: a 60-foot-deep lot reads 80% ($800 per front foot) and a 140-foot-deep lot reads 113% ($1,130). Separate "corner scales" divide the extra value of corner squares between lots — Somers 1901, pp. ~21–25, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich). [P]
- **Buildings, valued separately.** "The cost of reproduction, rather than the original cost, is a determining factor." Also relevant are "probable period of usefulness and present utility," and rents are the best guide to utility. Because experts are too expensive at scale, a standard "Building Slip" questionnaire is used — Somers 1901, "Improvements" section, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich). [P]
- **Cleveland before reform.** Ohio's constitution required appraisal at "true value in money." Land and buildings "were appraised once in ten years by appraisers elected in the wards," who had a ninety-day period to do the work. According to Johnson, small taxpayers paid full rates, while "public service corporations, steam railroads and large land-owning interests were paying between ten and twenty per cent. only of the amount required by law" — Johnson, *My Story*, pp. 126–127, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml). [P: memoir, a partisan account]
- **Cleveland's Tax School (1901–02).** Clerks copied the county auditor's assessments onto "a map sixteen feet square and comprising one whole ward," which was hung in City Hall for public discussion. From discussion and from "a searching investigation of the records of real estate transfers and leases," the school "ascertained the real value of one foot front of land by one hundred feet in depth, which method is known as the Somers unit system." Board members signed the unit map, a photographer copied it, and clerks computed the value of each parcel. Cleveland had 101,000 subdivisions of land, and their assessed values "varied from two per cent. of to sixty-eight per cent. above the actual or market value" — Johnson, *My Story*, pp. 127–128, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml). [P: memoir]
- **Legal end of the Tax School.** "On October 8, 1902, W. J. Crawford, the Republican boss, and a large property owner, brought suit to enjoin the expenditure of city money" on the school. The injunction was made permanent and the school suspended after "twenty months." Johnson credits it with "making public service corporations pay sixty thousand dollars a year more in taxes" — Johnson, *My Story*, pp. 129–130, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml). [P: memoir]
- **Statutory aftermath in Ohio.** Johnson wrote that land and buildings were "now appraised separately for taxation" and that the decennial appraisal had been "replaced by a quadrennial appraisement" by five-member boards elected locally without party designation — Johnson, *My Story*, p. 130, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml). [P: memoir. I did not check the Ohio statute itself.]
- **Somers hired for Cleveland's 1910 reappraisal.** A board of appraisers elected in fall 1909 employed "W. A. Somers, who had furnished his system to us in 1901," as chief clerk. The real estate duplicate (the tax roll), about $180 million as made in 1900 plus later buildings, rose to $500 million after the reappraisal (exempt property excluded) — Johnson, *My Story*, pp. 291–292, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml). [P: memoir]
- **Adoption in Houston.** Pastoriza, "applying a unit-value system," re-assessed business-district land for the 1911 roll. For 1912 the system was "extended to the assessment of land throughout the city," with committees from the Chamber of Commerce and the Houston Real Estate Exchange — Haig 1915, pp. 242–243, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]

### Inferences
- The Somers system turned land valuation into a public, map-based and partly participatory exercise. That is why it suited land-tax reformers: it produced a separate, published land value for every lot, which a split-rate or land-only tax needs.
- The method values land as a function of street frontage and depth, so it works best on a regular urban grid. It says little about irregular rural parcels; Somers's book handles farms separately, through map-based community review.

### Gaps
- I could not verify other adoptions of the Somers system claimed in the literature (for example by other Ohio cities or by later commercial appraisal firms). The only adoptions I confirmed are Cleveland (Johnson) and Houston's "unit-value system" (Haig).
- Somers's own career dates (for example any St Paul assessing role) are not confirmed here.

## 3. Pennsylvania's split-rate ("graded") tax, 1913–2026, and what the studies find

### Takeaway
A 1913 Pennsylvania act (Act No. 147, approved 15 May 1913) required Pittsburgh and Scranton to cut the city tax rate on buildings, step by step, to half the land rate by 1925. Third-class cities were later allowed to adopt split rates; Harrisburg did so in 1975. Further laws extended the option to school districts coterminous with third-class cities (1993) and to boroughs (1998). Pittsburgh sharply widened its land/building rate gap in 1979–80, then abandoned the two-rate tax after the 2000–2001 Allegheny County reassessment. Altoona went to a pure land tax in 2011 and dropped it from 2017. Peer-reviewed studies (Oates and Schwab 1997; Plassmann and Tideman 2000; Banzhaf and Lavery 2010) mostly find more building or denser development under split rates. The authors themselves note that the effect cannot be cleanly separated from other local events, and an earlier cross-city study found no significant effect. Which municipalities use split rates as of 2026 is **unresolved** in primary sources.

### Cited Findings
**The 1913 law and its phase-in**
- "In May, 1913, an act was passed by the Pennsylvania legislature which changed the tax systems in force in Pittsburgh and Scranton, by prescribing a gradual reduction in the tax on buildings. The act applies to 'cities of the second class,' of which these cities are the sole members." Haig cites it as "Act of the General Assembly No. 147, approved May 15, 1913" — Haig 1915, pp. 255–256, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P: statute quoted verbatim in a contemporaneous official report]
- Text quoted by Haig. Assessors "shall classify all real estate in the city … so as to distinguish between the buildings on land and the land exclusive of the buildings." The councils must then levy on buildings a rate equal to nine-tenths of the highest rate for 1914 and 1915; eight-tenths for 1916–1918; seven-tenths for 1919–1921; six-tenths for 1922–1924; and "five-tenths … for the year one thousand nine hundred and twenty-five, and for each year thereafter … so that … there shall, in any year, be two rates of taxation" — Haig 1915, p. 255, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- Haig's gloss: the first 10% reduction came in 1914, the second in 1916, and further reductions every third year "until 1925, when the assessment of buildings will equal fifty per cent." The scheme applied "merely to municipal taxes, not to county taxes," and school rates were excluded — Haig 1915, p. 256, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- In 1914 "Pittsburgh and Scranton, Pennsylvania, the same year, reduced the tax on buildings from one hundred to ninety per cent." — Haig 1915, p. 261, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Origins.** A December 1911 report of the Pittsburgh Civic Commission's Committee on Housing recommended reducing building assessments to 50%. Before that came the 1908 Pittsburgh Survey and Shelby M. Harrison's 1910 report, which criticised Pittsburgh's classification of real estate. Under that classification, "agricultural" land was taxed at half the full rate and "rural" estates of well-to-do residents at two-thirds, and there were sixty-three separate ward school-tax districts. The 1911 Halferty Bill abolished the classification — Haig 1915, pp. 256–257, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]

**Later enabling laws and adopters**
- "In 1975, Harrisburg became the third city to adopt two-rate property taxation after third class cities were authorized to set separate tax rates for land and buildings. School districts coterminous with third class cities were authorized to implement two-rate property taxes in 1993, and boroughs were allowed in 1998 (Bourassa 2009)." The number of split-rate municipalities "peaked in 2000," and "Five municipalities, including Pittsburgh, reverted to the conventional property tax after 2000." Counties and school districts, except Aliquippa and Clairton, kept uniform rates — Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, *Significant Features of the Property Tax: Pennsylvania* (June 2019, data updated July 2020), [PDF](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf). [S]
- Yang (2014), Table 1, lists Pennsylvania two-rate cities with first and last years, as of about 2010–2014. Still using at the time of the study: Aliquippa (from 1988), Allentown (1997), Altoona (2003), Clairton (1989), DuBois (1991), Duquesne (1985), Ebensburg (2000), Harrisburg (1975), Lock Haven (1991), McKeesport (1980), New Castle (1982), Scranton (1913), Titusville (1990), Washington (1985). Rescinded: Coatesville (1991–2005), Connellsville (1992–2003), Hazleton (1991–1992), Oil City (1989–2002), Pittsburgh (1913–2000), Steelton (2000–2007). Uniontown was excluded because it abolished the tax after one year. Mean land-to-structure rate ratios range from 1.58 (Steelton) to 18.85 (DuBois); Pittsburgh's is 4.48 — Zhou Yang, "The Effects of the Two-Rate Property Tax: What Can We Learn from the Pennsylvania Experience?" Lincoln Institute Working Paper WP14ZY1 (2014), p. 12, [PDF](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf). [S]
- Aliquippa adopted a land value tax in 1988 "taxing land at more than seven times the rate of buildings." Land-tax revenue rose "from less than one-fifth to more than 80 percent of city tax revenue," and the school district followed in 1993. Harrisburg adopted in 1975, and between the early 1980s and early 2000s its vacant buildings fell "from more than 4,000 to about 500." Note that this is a before/after count that does not isolate the tax's effect — J. Brian Charles, *Governing*, 19 Aug 2019, [link](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html). [S]

**Pittsburgh 1979–80**
- The Oates and Schwab abstract: the paper "Analyzes the impact of property tax reform in Pittsburgh (1979-1980), which dramatically increased the tax rate applied to land (but not structures)." Citation: Wallace E. Oates and Robert M. Schwab, "The Impact of Urban Land Taxation: The Pittsburgh Experience," *National Tax Journal* 50(1) (March 1997): 1–21, [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ntj/journl/v50y1997i1p1-21.html). [S]
- The often-repeated claim that Pittsburgh raised the land rate "to more than five times the rate on structures" appeared only in a search-engine snippet of a fuller abstract; the Scinapse page was behind a CAPTCHA. **[U]** The exact 1979–80 millages are also **[U]**: I could not access the paywalled full text.

**Pittsburgh's 2001 repeal**
- "Pittsburgh reverted to the conventional property tax after 2000 due to a drastic increase in assessed land value after the property reassessment." Yang adds that Pittsburgh's Business Improvement District "still employs a pure land value tax as a surcharge on the regular property tax" — Yang 2014, p. 3 and n. 4, [PDF](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf). [S]
- *Governing* (2019): "The 2000-2001 reassessments raised land value taxes by 81 percent and building taxes by 43 percent." Affluent residents filed thousands of appeals, and the city "scrapped the tax in 2001." Separately, Pittsburgh began in 1997 to use a pure land value tax in its central business district — [Governing](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html). [S]
- Joan Youngman (Lincoln Institute), on the lesson: "If you fail to revalue, you'll have a revolt because you get sticker shock." — [Governing, 2019](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html). [S]
- Advocacy claims, unverified: Mayor Tom Murphy shifted to a flat rate on 19 January 2001; council adopted a 10.8-mill uniform rate as a "temporary measure"; 54% of homeowners paid more under the single rate — [progress.org wiki, lead only](https://www.progress.org/wiki/pittsburgh/). A figure of 130,000 assessment appeals appears in 2026 Maryland legislative testimony seen only in search results. **[U]**
- **Context on aging valuations in Pennsylvania.** Assessment is done by the 67 counties, which may use a base year and decide when to reassess. "As of 2010, seven counties had not conducted a reassessment since at least the mid-1980s." In *Clifton v. Allegheny County* (2009) the Pennsylvania Supreme Court found that Allegheny County's use of a base year, as implemented, violated uniformity — [Lincoln Institute PA brief](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf). [S]

**Altoona: pure land tax and reversal**
- Since 2002, Altoona's city property taxes were "partly based on land value"; since 2011 they were based entirely on land. Because land was only about one-seventh of total assessed value, the land millage was 394. In June 2016 council informally agreed to eliminate the land value tax starting the next year, with Councilman Dave Butterbaugh the main holdout. Reasons given included that the tax had not proved effective and caused confusion. Mayor Matt Pacifico said the high land millage discouraged business. Councilman Matt Cacciotti said it deterred neighbours from buying vacant lots. Finance Director Omar Strohm noted that city property taxes were only about one-fourth of residents' total bills. The majority followed the city manager and the Act 47 recovery coordinator in returning to the standard method — William Kibler, *Altoona Mirror*, 6 June 2016, [link](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2016/06/city-council-decides-to-cut-land-value-tax/). [S]
- Lincoln Institute: "In 2011, The City of Altoona … became the first city in Pennsylvania and the first city in the country to levy a pure land value tax at the municipality level, but in 2016 the city council decided to abandon the land value tax, reverting to assessing land and buildings in 2017" — [Lincoln PA brief](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf). [S]
- Discrepancies: Yang (2014) dates Altoona's start to 2003, while the *Mirror* says 2002. The Lincoln brief's reference list dates the Kibler article "April 3," but the article URL and its dateline show June 2016. **[U on exact start year]**

**Empirical studies and how contested they are**
- Mathis and Zech (1982): a cross-section of 27 Pennsylvania cities found no significant impact on construction. Bourassa (1990) found no "liquidity effect" in Pittsburgh, McKeesport or New Castle, but found an "incentive effect" in Pittsburgh — summarised in Yang 2014, p. 2, [PDF](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf). [S]
- Oates and Schwab (1997) found that Pittsburgh "experienced a significant increase in levels of building activity relative to other Midwestern cities during the 1980s, despite the sharp decline of the city's steel industry." They "recognized that the findings were based on a single city in a special macroeconomic environment" and "were unable to separate the effect of property tax reform from other economic events" — Yang 2014, pp. 2–3, [PDF](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf). [S]
- Plassmann and Tideman (2000) "compared 15 Pennsylvania cities that tax land value at a higher rate than improvements with similar sized single-rate Pennsylvania cities between 1972 and 1994," and found that "higher tax rates on land value lead to increased construction." This summary is from Yang 2014, p. 3, [PDF](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf). Citation: Florenz Plassmann and T. Nicolaus Tideman, "A Markov Chain Monte Carlo Analysis of the Effect of Two-Rate Property Taxes on Construction," *Journal of Urban Economics* 47(2) (2000): 216–247, [RePEc record, no abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v47y2000i2p216-247.html). [S]
- Banzhaf and Lavery (2010) "confirm the theoretical prediction that the split-rate tax raises the capital/land ratio." They also find "the primary effect is in more housing units, rather than bigger units," calling the tax "potentially a powerful anti-sprawl tool." Citation: H. Spencer Banzhaf and Nathan Lavery, "Can the land tax help curb urban sprawl? Evidence from growth patterns in Pennsylvania," *Journal of Urban Economics* 67(2) (2010): 169–179, [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v67y2010i2p169-179.html). Yang reports that they estimated "an effect of 5-6% points for the improvement effect" — [Yang 2014, n. 18](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf). [S]
- Yang (2014) used new 1970–2010 tax-rate data and found effects in the same direction but larger, for example a comparable estimate of "about 6.8%." The effect again came from denser housing rather than bigger houses — [Yang 2014](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf). [S: working paper, not peer reviewed]
- Yang and Hawley (2021) found that switching to split-rate taxation had a significantly positive effect on aggregate market values. The effect of changing split-rate parameters was smaller and depended on specification. Commercial property appeared to benefit more. Land values fell, though "not drastically" — Zhou Yang and Zackary Hawley, *Split-Rate Taxation: Impacts on Tax Base*, Lincoln Institute working paper (January 2021), [page](https://www.lincolninst.edu/publications/working-papers/split-rate-taxation). [S]
- Mark Alan Hughes (2005) called Pennsylvania's experience "a compelling example of the limited role that evidence often plays in policy decisions." Quoted in [Governing, 2019](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html). [S]

**Status as of 2026**
- A land-value-tax advocacy tracker (as of 29 October 2026) says "Harrisburg uses it today." It gives no complete list of Pennsylvania split-rate municipalities — [landeconomics.org tracker, lead only](https://landeconomics.org/lvt-legislation). **[U]**

### Inferences
- The 1913 Pennsylvania design was a statutory, slow, automatic phase-in (one-tenth every three years) confined to city taxes. That design avoided a sudden shift of the tax burden onto land. Compare the Canadian lesson (Section 5) that shifts worked best where land values were rising.
- Pittsburgh's 2001 reversal and Altoona's 2016 reversal both turned on valuation and visibility rather than on rates. Pittsburgh's long-unrevised land assessments jumped at reassessment. In Altoona, land was a small share of assessed value (so the land millage had to be very high), and the city levy was a small share of each tax bill.
- On the empirical record: every study except Mathis and Zech points in the same direction (more construction, or more units per acre). All of them, however, rely on a small number of mostly declining Pennsylvania cities that chose to adopt split rates themselves. That self-selection, and the confounding with other events that Oates and Schwab themselves acknowledged, is why the size and causal nature of the effect remains disputed.

### Gaps
- **The list of Pennsylvania municipalities using split rates as of 2026 is unresolved.** I found no current DCED or state list, and the search budget ran out before I could check municipal ordinances. Scranton's and Allentown's current status, and Harrisburg's 2026 land and building millages, are unverified. The Harrisburg eCode360 document I fetched was a council agenda, not the levy ordinance.
- The exact 1979–80 Pittsburgh millages, and the date and vote of the 2001 council action, are unverified.
- Revenue shares, such as the land-tax share of Pittsburgh city revenue in particular years, were not found.

## 4. Houston's "Houston Plan" (1911/12–1915) under J. J. Pastoriza

### Takeaway
Without any legal authority, Houston's elected Finance and Tax Commissioner J. J. Pastoriza assessed land at about 70% of value and buildings at 33⅓% in 1912, then at 25% from 1914, while omitting money, credits and household goods from the rolls. He publicised the scheme openly. Pastoriza claimed "magical" results; Haig's 1915 field investigation could not confirm them. A Texas appellate court affirmed an injunction against the plan on 25 May 1915, holding that it violated the state constitution's requirement that "Taxation shall be equal and uniform."

### Cited Findings
- **Before Pastoriza.** Assessments were "chaotic." Pastoriza testified that "hundreds of buildings … were not on the tax rolls at all." For 1911 he re-assessed business-district land with a unit-value system, aiming "at equality rather than fullness," with land at about 50% of value — Haig 1915, p. 242, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **The 1912 plan.** Pastoriza proposed land at about full value and buildings at 25%. After opposition, land was entered at about 70% and buildings at 33⅓%. Franchises of public-service corporations were added to the rolls, valued at $1,783,500 in 1912 and $2,216,130 in 1913. "Cash, notes, mortgages, credits and other evidences of debt, as well as household furniture were totally exempted" — Haig 1915, p. 243 and n. 1, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **1914.** Building assessments fell to 25%. A city-wide reassessment raised values, and the city limits were extended to double the area. Pastoriza's attempt to assess land at 100% failed, and land "remained seventy." Machinery was classed as improvements at 25% — Haig 1915, p. 243 and n. 2, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Openly unconstitutional.** Asked in his 1913 campaign about the constitution's proportional-assessment rule, Pastoriza replied: "It is true that the constitution says this, but there is not a city or a county in the state of Texas that has ever complied with this constitutional requirement … so what is the use of splitting hairs?" His advertisement said: "If you like the plan, vote for me; if you don't, vote against me." He was re-elected "by a vote larger by 1,200 than that of any other candidate for commissioner" — Haig 1915, p. 243, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- Haig called Houston "the most interesting case of unauthorized and illegal undervaluation of buildings … The constitution and statutes of the state are openly flouted and the fact advertised." — Haig 1915, p. 261, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Claimed results, and who claimed them.** "'The effect resulting from the partial exemption from taxation of personal property and improvements upon land in Houston, Texas, has been magical,' declares Mr. Pastoriza." His evidence was building statistics, assessments, bank clearings and population. In the *New York Evening Post* of 11 August 1914 he wrote that almost every permit applicant said "'Pastoriza's plan' has compelled him to build." Haig's investigator spent several hours in the permit office and "heard no applicant plead the pressure of taxation." Of the owners Pastoriza named, one partly credited the plan. The owner of a six-story building "denied flatly that the tax system had been any influence at all." Haig concluded it was "not easy to establish a direct connection" between the plan and building activity, given Houston's ship channel and growth — Haig 1915, pp. 244–250, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Collection record.** 94% of 1913 taxes were collected by year-end, rising to 96% two months later — Haig 1915, p. ~244, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **The court ruling.** *City of Houston v. Baker*, 178 S.W. 820 (Tex. Civ. App. 1915), decided 25 May 1915 (McMeans, J.). For the 1914 assessment the court found land without improvements taxed at "70 per cent. of full value," improvements at 25%, and merchandise at 50%. Household goods, money, stocks, bonds, mortgages, notes and credits were deliberately omitted. Property in the taxed classes exceeded $109,000,000, and the city intended to follow the same plan in 1915. The city conceded the plan was not legal. The court held it "violative of the provision of the Constitution which demands uniformity of taxation" (Tex. Const. art. 8, §1: "Taxation shall be equal and uniform") and affirmed the trial court's injunction — [opinion text, hallapproved.com](https://hallapproved.com/tx/cases/appeals/1915/4148821). [P]
- Unverified details: the suit was brought by large owners organised as the "Harris County Taxpayers Association" (*J. W. Baker et al. v. City of Houston*). Judge John A. Read is said to have ruled the plan unconstitutional on 2 March 1915. Some accounts give 20 May 1915 for the appeal ruling, against 25 May in the opinion text, and say Houston then taxed land at 70% and improvements at 50% for 1915–16 — [progress.org wiki, lead only](https://www.progress.org/wiki/houston-plan-of-taxation/). **[U]**

### Inferences
- The Houston Plan was a valuation strategy rather than a tax law: it used fractional assessment ratios set by an elected assessor to mimic a split rate. That is why a uniformity clause could end it outright, whereas Pennsylvania's version rested on statute.
- The "results" debate shows a pattern that recurs throughout this file: advocates offered before/after building statistics, while a contemporary investigator found the causal link hard to establish.

### Gaps
- The trial court's judgment date and parties are not confirmed from the trial record.
- Houston's tax treatment after 1915 is not confirmed.

## 5. Western Canada (and Pueblo), the 1913 bust, and Robert Murray Haig's 1915 report

### Takeaway
Between 1895 and 1913, western Canadian cities reduced or abolished the local tax on buildings. Vancouver cut building assessments to 50% (1895) and 25% (1906), and exempted them entirely in 1910. Edmonton's 1904 charter exempted improvements. Calgary moved from 80% (1909) to 25% (1912). Alberta forced all its towns onto a land-only basis in 1912. Haig's 1915 report for New York City, written after the 1913 land bust, concluded that results "depend largely upon local conditions." The shift had worked where land values were rising fast. Speculative land proved an undependable tax base in the slump. By 1969, according to Calgary's treasurer, the cities had "reverted to taxing improvements," and Alberta then taxed 60% of building value.

### Cited Findings
**Haig's commission**
- The report was prepared for the Committee on Taxation of the City of New York, "appointed April 10, 1914 by Honorable John Purroy Mitchel, Mayor." Alfred E. Marling was chairman, Edwin R. A. Seligman chaired the executive committee, Frederick C. Howe was secretary, and members included Lawson Purdy. Haig was "Instructor in Economics, Columbia University." The report covers Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Ontario, Manitoba (Winnipeg), Saskatchewan (Regina, Saskatoon), Alberta (Edmonton, Calgary, towns), British Columbia (Vancouver, Victoria) and, in the US, Houston, Pueblo, Pittsburgh and Scranton, and Everett — Haig 1915, front matter and contents, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]

**Vancouver**
- First charter 1886. By 1891 the charter "provided that land and improvements should be assessed separately and granted full power to the council to exempt improvements, wholly or in part." In 1895 council cut building assessments by 50%, reducing the tax base from $18,301,084 to $15,988,554; receipts fell from $257,883.32 (1894) to $235,248.80 (1895). In 1905 a cut to 40% failed eight to three. In 1906 buildings went to 25%, and the base nonetheless grew from $22,641,765 (1905) to $28,623,670. In 1910 council "decided to exempt improvements entirely," a plank of Mayor L. D. Taylor's platform, though there "has never been in Vancouver a direct expression of popular sentiment on the question." Council renewed the exemption each year after 1910 — Haig 1915, pp. 186–187, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **How values were set.** The charter required the assessor "annually" to estimate rateable property "at its actual cash value as it would be appraised in payment of a just debt from a solvent debtor, the value of the improvements, if any, being estimated separately" — Haig 1915, p. ~187, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **British Columbia generally.** For "more than twenty years a general law has forbidden the taxation of improvements in municipalities at more than fifty per cent. of their value." Of 61 city and district municipalities, 39 exempted improvements entirely and 17 taxed them at the 50% maximum — Haig 1915, BC section (pp. 162–174), [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Vancouver in the bust.** Population was 106,110, "smaller by 16,000 than it was two years ago" — Haig 1915, p. 174, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]

**Edmonton and Calgary**
- "The Edmonton charter, adopted in 1904, which exempted improvements from taxation came to be 'looked upon as an ark of the covenant.'" A newspaper editor told Haig: "The single tax in Edmonton is an accident … The citizens, meeting together to decide what to tax, decided to tax the other fellow," since most unimproved land belonged to non-residents — Haig 1915, pp. 106–107, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Calgary.** Before 1909 land and buildings were assessed at about 100%. In 1909 buildings went to 80%, and in 1910 land to 90%. In 1911 buildings fell to 50% under a charter amendment that required at least a 10-point annual reduction "until all such assessments on buildings and improvements shall have been extinguished." In 1912 buildings went to 25% and land to 100%. In 1913 council "experienced a change of heart," held buildings at 25%, and obtained an amendment relieving it of the compulsory reductions — Haig 1915, pp. 113–114, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Alberta towns.** "The legislature of Alberta in 1912, at one long leap, and upon its own initiative, placed all of the towns of the province upon a single-tax basis," through the Town Act, disregarding the towns' wishes. Saskatchewan's statute, by contrast, required municipal consent and a gradual transition. In Leduc, land was assessed at only $179,290, and the 1912 rate was 42 mills against 20 mills in 1911, when other property was taxed too. An extra six-mill rate was found to have "no legal justification" and was abandoned in 1913 — Haig 1915, pp. 129, ~143–144, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Other provincial measures.** Alberta levied "a new unearned increment tax … which appropriates for provincial purposes five per cent. of the increases in land values." Saskatchewan, Alberta and BC levied "wild land taxes." Lethbridge and smaller BC municipalities imposed super-taxes on vacant land — Haig 1915, pp. 261–263, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]

**Haig's conclusions (1915)**
- "The cities of western Canada are, in their nature, exceedingly dynamic," so before/after comparisons "invite all sorts of statistical disasters." Comparisons between cities also failed, because "Every large city in the Canadian West has taken long steps toward the single tax" — Haig 1915, p. 264, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Revenue.** Regina, Saskatoon, Edmonton and Vancouver showed signs that a land-only base would not yield enough "without resort to tax rates unattractively high." At the end of 1913 unpaid taxes were $576,933.97 in Saskatoon and $689,969.19 in Vancouver. Less than half of Edmonton's 1914 taxes had been paid by mid-March. "Calgary has not had a tax sale of land since 1911, Vancouver since 1909 and Edmonton never!" There was "much talk in Vancouver about the necessity of abandoning the policy." Haig: "land values cannot under all circumstances be depended upon to supply in a satisfactory manner the necessary public revenue" — Haig 1915, p. 265, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Speculation.** "In none of the larger municipalities have the land taxes seriously interfered with" speculation, because city rates were low. In some Alberta towns, rates high enough to force owners to surrender land showed that "speculation in land can be utterly destroyed by this method" — Haig 1915, p. ~275, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Overall.** The plan "has had very undesirable effects where its introduction has involved a material decrease in the tax base." It succeeded where "land values were increasing enormously," because "most of the shock has been absorbed by new land values, large in amount and, to a considerable extent, unexpected and undiscounted." Also: "In Winnipeg one-third of the value of the buildings was eliminated in one year and few people seem to have realized that the action had been taken." Results were "very largely a matter of local conditions" — Haig 1915, pp. 277–279, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **A valuation note.** "In assessing land values, it is universally the custom in Canada to make no allowances for that part of the land value which is due to the expenditure of capital in the land or upon improvements directly benefiting the land." — Haig 1915, p. 263 n. 2, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Informal differential assessment was widespread.** Ontario municipalities "undervalue improvements greatly." Manitoba cities illegally assessed improvements at 50% (Brandon, Saint Boniface) or 60% (Portage la Prairie). Portland, Oregon, assessed improvements at 50% and land at 75% — Haig 1915, p. 261, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]

**Pueblo, Colorado (1913)**
- At an election on 5 November 1913, voters approved 2,711 to 2,171 a charter amendment exempting improvements from municipal taxes by 50% from 1 January 1914 and by 99% from 1 January 1915. It also capped the city tax on personal property at 1 mill. For 1914, city taxes were 16.3 mills on full land value plus half of improvements, while state, county and school taxes (13.69 mills) stayed on the old base. Commissioner J. Knox Burton wrote on 2 April 1915 that no changes "in values, rentals, building activity or general business activity" could be traced to the amendment — Haig 1915, pp. 253–254, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]

**The reversals**
- F. G. Arscott, Treasurer of the City of Calgary, in a brief to the Municipal Finance Officers Association of the United States and Canada (63rd Annual Conference, Toronto, 25–27 May 1969), reprinted June 1969: "The boom collapsed in 1913 … Inflated land prices used as a basis of assessment collapsed, and with shrinking revenues, the over burdened municipalities, including Calgary, reverted to taxing improvements to stave off bankruptcy." He reports that a 1939 study by H. Bronson Cowan concluded the tax would have restrained speculation had it been introduced before the boom. He also states that "to this day the municipalities of Alberta exempt 40% of the assessment value of buildings," that this was to rise to 55% (that is, buildings taxed at 45%), and that Calgary's building assessment was two-thirds of its taxable base — [scan hosted on a Georgist archive](https://cooperative-individualism.org/arscott-f-g_site-taxation-1969-may.pdf). [S: a contemporaneous municipal official's brief, reached through an advocacy-site scan; I could not verify the original periodical]

### Inferences
- The Canadian experiments are the clearest North American case of valuation risk in a land-only base. Assessments built on boom-time "speculative" land prices left cities with uncollectable levies when prices collapsed in 1913. Taxing buildings again restored a base that held its value.
- Haig's point that the shift is easiest when land values are rising, so that new, unexpected value absorbs the tax, has close parallels in the Pennsylvania phase-in and in Pittsburgh's 2001 reassessment shock.

### Gaps
- Exact years in which Vancouver, Edmonton and Calgary resumed taxing improvements, and at what percentages, are **unresolved**. Arscott gives no dates. An advocacy wiki's claim that Vancouver's land-only policy lasted until 1984 is unverified. A 1977 *Urban History Review* article and an Alberta heritage page that might settle this were inaccessible (see Access log).
- I did not read Haig's sections on Everett, Washington, or on Regina, Saskatoon and Winnipeg in detail.

## 6. Fairhope, Alabama (1894) (brief)

### Takeaway
Fairhope is a private Georgist "enclave." A corporation owns the land and leases it on 99-year renewable leases. Lessees own their buildings and pay rent, which today is set by the property taxes due plus fees. It was never set by a true land-rent appraisal.

### Cited Findings
- In November 1894, 28 single taxers from Des Moines, Iowa, gathered at Fairhope. The Fairhope Industrial Association was renamed the Fairhope Single Tax Corporation in 1904 — [Fairhope history](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/fairhope-history); [home page](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/). [S]
- The corporation owns about 4,500 acres, "roughly 25% of Fairhope," according to the home page; the history page says about 4,600 acres. Leases are 99 years and renewable, and lessees own improvements — [home](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/); [history](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/fairhope-history). [S; acreage figures differ]
- In theory rent was to capture the full annual rental value of land. In practice pier fees covered costs until the causeway opened in 1927. Today rent equals the city, school, county and state real estate taxes due, plus an administrative add-on and a "demonstration fee." It does not reflect annual rental value — [Fairhope history](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/fairhope-history). [S]

### Inferences
- Fairhope shows a single tax carried out by private contract within a jurisdiction that keeps a conventional property tax. Its "valuation" has in practice tracked the public assessor's figures rather than an independent rent appraisal.

### Gaps
- How and how often the corporation historically appraised land rent is not documented on the pages I accessed.

## 7. California Proposition 13 (1978) and Proposition 19 (2020): the archetypal valuation freeze

### Takeaway
Proposition 13, approved on 6 June 1978, rolled assessments back to 1975 market values and capped the tax rate at 1%. Annual growth in assessed value is limited to 2% (or inflation if lower), with reassessment to market value only on a change of ownership or new construction. This turned a market-value tax into an acquisition-value tax. Assessed and market values diverge the longer property is held. Proposition 19 (2020) widened tax-base transfers for older, disabled and disaster-hit homeowners and narrowed the parent-to-child exclusion.

### Cited Findings
- "On June 6, 1978, California voters overwhelmingly approved Proposition 13 … [it] rolled back most local real property … assessments to 1975 market value levels, limited the property tax rate to 1 percent plus the rate necessary to fund local voter-approved bonded indebtedness, and limited future property tax increases" — California State Board of Equalization (BOE), *Publication 29: California Property Tax, An Overview* (March 2025), [PDF](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf). [P]
- **Before 1978**, "real property was appraised cyclically, with no more than a five-year interval between reassessments," so assessed values were "usually kept at or near current market value levels." Under Proposition 13, property is "reassessed to current market value only upon a change in ownership or completion of new construction (called the base year value)," with annual increases limited "to no more than 2 percent." It "converted the market value-based property tax system to an acquisition value-based system" — [BOE Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf). [P]
- The Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) says taxable value rises each year "by 2 percent or the rate of inflation, whichever is lower." The average rate before Proposition 13 was 2.67%. Property tax payments "dropped by roughly 60 percent immediately." Because market values usually grow faster than 2%, "the taxable value of most properties is less than their market value. The longer a property is owned, the wider this gap tends to grow" — LAO, *Common Claims About Proposition 13* (September 2016), [PDF](https://lao.ca.gov/reports/2016/3497/common-claims-prop13-091916.pdf). [P]
- **Fiscal shock.** County property tax revenues fell "from $10.3 billion in 1977-78 to $5.04 billion in 1978-79." State "bailouts" cost $4.17 billion in the first year and $4.85 billion in the second — [BOE Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf). [P]
- **Courts.** The California Supreme Court upheld Proposition 13 in *Amador Valley Joint Union High School District v. State Board of Equalization* on 22 September 1978. In 1992 the US Supreme Court, in *Nordlinger v. Hahn*, held that it did not violate equal protection — [BOE Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf). [P]
- **Effects measured by the LAO.** 16% of properties sold in 1977–78 against 5% in 2014–15, with Proposition 13 "some part" of the decline. Homeowners paid about 34% of secured property taxes in 1979–80, a low of 32% in the mid-1980s, and about 37% in 2015–16. The property tax raised $55 billion in 2014–15 — [LAO 2016](https://lao.ca.gov/reports/2016/3497/common-claims-prop13-091916.pdf). [P]
- In 2022–23 the property tax raised "more than $89 billion for local government," split 14% counties, 12% cities, 53% schools and 20% other — [BOE Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf). [P]
- **Proposition 19 (November 2020 ballot).** From 1 April 2021, homeowners who are 55 or over, severely disabled, or disaster-affected may transfer their taxable value anywhere in the state, may buy a more expensive home (with a partial increase), and, for the 55+ and disabled groups, may do so three times. From 16 February 2021, the parent-child (and some grandparent-grandchild) exclusion applies only to a home used as the heir's primary residence, and to farms. If market value exceeds taxable value by more than $1 million (indexed), taxes rise. The LAO estimated gains to local governments of "tens of millions" at first, possibly growing to "a few hundred million" a year — [LAO ballot analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=19&year=2020). [P]

### Inferences
- Proposition 13 is the clearest North American case of a deliberate valuation freeze. Assessed value is no longer an estimate of current value but a historical purchase price indexed by at most 2%. Land and buildings are frozen together, so the land component of value, which drives most appreciation, is the part most shielded.

### Gaps
- I could not open the constitutional text (Cal. Const. art. XIII A) on leginfo.legislature.ca.gov: HTTP 403. The precise wording of the "1975–76 tax bill" base-year definition is therefore cited only through BOE's summary ("1975 market value levels").
- I did not retrieve vote percentages for Proposition 13 or Proposition 19, or recent statewide statistics on the gap between assessed and market values. Fischel's work on Proposition 13 was not consulted.

## 8. Florida: Save Our Homes (1992) and Amendment 3 (CS/HJR 1-F), voted on 3 November 2026

### Takeaway
Florida's 1992 "Save Our Homes" amendment caps annual increases in a homestead's assessed value at the lower of 3% or CPI, resetting to just value on sale. A separate 10% cap applies to non-homestead property for non-school levies. In a June 2026 special session the legislature proposed CS/HJR 1-F. It raises the non-school homestead exemption to $150,000 (2027) and $250,000 (2028), cuts the non-homestead cap to 5% from 2027, and makes post-2026 arrivals wait until their fifth year for the larger exemption. Voters approved it as Amendment 3 on 3 November 2026, with about 62% (60% needed), according to media counts with 99% of precincts reporting. I could not access the official state results page.

## 9. How US assessors value land separately from buildings, and the research on regressive assessment

### Takeaway
US assessment rolls generally carry separate land and improvement values within one market-value (or capped) assessment. The land/building split matters for the tax only where rates differ, as in Pennsylvania split-rate cities or under proposals such as Detroit's. Historically, land was valued by front-foot "unit" methods (Somers) and buildings by replacement cost less depreciation. Since 2017, investigations and studies have shown that mass-appraisal systems often over-assess cheap homes relative to expensive ones (regressivity). The best-known cases are the *Chicago Tribune*/ProPublica "Tax Divide" series on Cook County, Christopher Berry's estimates, and Avenancio-León and Howard's national evidence of a racial "assessment gap."

### Cited Findings
**Separate land and building values**
- California defines real property to include land and, separately, "Improvements—defined as all buildings, structures, fixtures, and fences erected on or affixed to the land" — [BOE Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf). [P]
- Florida statute s. 192.001(12) defines "real property" as "land, buildings, fixtures, and all other improvements to land" — cited in the [House staff analysis, n. 4](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/Analyses/h0001z.SAC.PDF). [P]
- Historic methods: Somers's front-foot unit with depth and corner tables for land, and a "Building Slip" with reproduction cost, period of usefulness and present utility for buildings — [Somers 1901](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich) (see Section 2). [P]
- Calgary's treasurer (1969): land assessments rest "on traditional factors of zoning, use, services available and distance from the central or other focus points," and buildings on "varying amounts of labor and capital invested in them" — [Arscott 1969](https://cooperative-individualism.org/arscott-f-g_site-taxation-1969-may.pdf). [S]
- Pennsylvania's 1913 act made the land/building split a statutory duty of assessors in second-class cities: "so as to distinguish between the buildings on land and the land exclusive of the buildings" — [Haig 1915, p. 255](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). [P]
- **Capacity matters.** On Detroit's 2023 split-rate plan, Detroit's assessor said that without more funding the plan was "dead in the water" — [BridgeDetroit, 20 Oct 2023](https://www.bridgedetroit.com/detroits-land-value-tax-plan-explained/). [S]

**Regressive assessment**
- **"The Tax Divide."** A ProPublica Illinois and *Chicago Tribune* series. The ProPublica series page lists items from 17 July 2017 to 22 March 2018, including the commercial and industrial findings on 7 December 2017. The project was a Pulitzer finalist for Local Reporting (16 April 2018) — [ProPublica series page](https://www.propublica.org/series/the-tax-divide). [S]
- **Independent study for Board President Toni Preckwinkle** (Civic Consulting Alliance; an assessment expert using IAAO industry standards), covering 2014–2016. It found "a very regressive system" causing "a wealth transfer from owners of lower-value homes to those of higher-value homes." In Chicago, a $600,000 home likely carried an effective tax rate 24% lower than a $300,000 home. Over 90% of model values were changed before notices went out, the office engaged in "sales chasing," and appeals worsened inequity. Owners of homes over $1 million were twice as likely to appeal as owners under $500,000 — ProPublica/*Tribune*, 15 Feb 2018, [link](https://www.propublica.org/article/cook-county-residential-property-assessment-study-results). [S]
- **Christopher Berry** (Center for Municipal Finance, University of Chicago Harris School) estimated that "at least $2.2 billion" in property taxes shifted from undervalued to overvalued Chicago homes in 2011–2015, with as much as $1 billion shaved off bills for the top 10% of homes by value. Method: arm's-length sales (about 2.5% of homes) compared with bills at a "fair" rate. Berry: "Everyone — even the assessor — now agrees that the system is regressive" — ProPublica/*Tribune*, 15 Mar 2018, [link](https://www.propublica.org/article/cook-county-property-tax-shift-regressive-assessments). [S]
- **Assessor Joseph Berrios rejected the findings** at the time: "the study they used and the methods they advocate are unreliable" (from search-result extracts of ProPublica coverage). Political follow-up: "Cook County Assessor Joe Berrios' Defeat Opens the Door to Reform" (22 March 2018), after the 20 March 2018 Democratic primary that Fritz Kaegi contested on a reform platform — [series page](https://www.propublica.org/series/the-tax-divide); [15 Feb 2018 article](https://www.propublica.org/article/cook-county-residential-property-assessment-study-results). [S]
- **National evidence.** Holding jurisdiction and tax rates fixed, Black and Hispanic residents face a "10%–13% higher tax burden for the same bundle of public services." About half of the gap is between neighbourhoods and half within them. Assessments respond less to neighbourhood attributes than market prices do, and appeal behaviour also differs. The gap does not stem from differences in transaction prices or housing features — Carlos F. Avenancio-León and Troup Howard, "The Assessment Gap: Racial Inequalities in Property Taxation," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 137(3) (2022): 1383–1434, doi:10.1093/qje/qjac009, [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:137:y:2022:i:3:p:1383-1434.). [S: peer reviewed]

### Inferences
- Regressivity is a valuation problem (assessment-to-sale ratios falling as price rises) rather than a rate problem. It interacts with freezes and caps: Avenancio-León and Howard also wrote on assessment caps and the racial gap, which I did not access. In land-tax debates it matters because split-rate systems depend on accurate land values, which are harder to observe than whole-property sale prices.

### Gaps
- I could not access the IAAO *Standard on Mass Appraisal* (PDF blocked). Modern land-valuation techniques (sales comparison of vacant lots, allocation, extraction, land residual, ground-rent capitalisation) are therefore not documented from a primary standards source here.
- Christopher Berry's national paper ("Reassessing the Property Tax," c. 2021) was not located before the search budget ran out.
- Post-2018 Cook County reforms (Kaegi's tenure from December 2018, new models, Illinois legislation) are not verified in fetched sources.

## 11. Ontario: assessments held at 1 January 2016 values

### Takeaway

## Merge aid B: tax profiles

**B1. Pennsylvania graded or split-rate tax (Pittsburgh and Scranton from 1913; others later)**
- *Taxed:* Land and buildings, both under the municipal property tax, at two rates. County and school taxes stayed uniform, except Aliquippa's and Clairton's school districts — [Haig p. 256](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft); [Lincoln PA brief](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf).
- *Valued by:* City assessors in 1913, required to separate "the buildings on land and the land exclusive of the buildings." Today county assessment offices value property, using market value or a base year at county discretion — [Haig p. 255](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft); [Lincoln PA brief](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf).
- *Updating:* No statewide revaluation cycle. Counties decide; seven counties had not reassessed since at least the mid-1980s as of 2010 — [Lincoln PA brief](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf).
- *Rates and phase-in:* Building rate at 0.9 of the land rate (1914–15), 0.8 (1916–18), 0.7 (1919–21), 0.6 (1922–24), 0.5 (1925 onward) — [Haig p. 255](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft). Later land/building ratios ranged from about 1.6 to 18.9 by city — [Yang 2014](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf). Altoona's land-only levy was 394 mills in 2016 — [*Altoona Mirror*](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2016/06/city-council-decides-to-cut-land-value-tax/).
- *Revenue:* Aliquippa's land-tax share went from under one-fifth to over 80% of city tax revenue after 1988 — [Governing](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html). No Pittsburgh series was found.
- *What happened to valuations:* Pittsburgh's 2000–01 reassessment raised land-value taxes by 81% (buildings 43%) and the city dropped the split rate in 2001 — [Governing](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html).

**B2. Cleveland and Ohio real property tax under the Somers unit system (1901–1910)**
- *Taxed:* Land and buildings at "true value in money," at one rate.
- *Valued by:* Before reform, ward-elected decennial appraisers, working within a ninety-day window. Then the Tax School (1901–02), and from 1909–10 an elected board of appraisers employing Somers. Land was valued as a front foot × 100 feet unit, published on maps.
- *Updating:* Decennial, later quadrennial.
- *Effect on valuations:* Pre-reform assessments ranged from 2% of to 68% above market value. The duplicate rose from about $180m to $500m after the 1910 reappraisal. Source for all of B2: [Johnson, *My Story*, pp. 126–130, 291–292](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml) [P: memoir].

**B3. Houston Plan (1912–1915)**
- *Taxed:* Land at about 70% of value, improvements at 33⅓% (1912) then 25% (1914), merchandise at 50%, franchises added. Money, credits and household goods were omitted.
- *Valued by:* The elected Finance and Tax Commissioner, using a unit-value system and committees of the Chamber of Commerce and the Real Estate Exchange.
- *Updating:* Annual rolls; re-assessment in 1914.
- *Revenue facts:* Taxed classes exceeded $109m; 94–96% of 1913 taxes collected.
- *Ended:* By the 1915 injunction under the uniformity clause.
- Sources: [Haig pp. 242–250](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft); [*City of Houston v. Baker*](https://hallapproved.com/tx/cases/appeals/1915/4148821) [P].

**B4. Vancouver and British Columbia exemption of improvements (1895–)**
- *Taxed:* Land at "actual cash value"; improvements estimated separately and taxed at 50% (1895), 25% (1906), then 0% (1910).
- *Valued by:* City assessor, annually.
- *BC cap:* Improvements could not be taxed at more than 50% of value. In 1914, 39 of 61 municipalities exempted them fully.
- *Valuations:* Tax base $15.99m (1895), $22.64m (1905), $28.62m (1906). Unpaid taxes reached $689,969.19 by the end of 1913.
- Source: [Haig pp. 162–187, 265](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft) [P].

**B5. Edmonton (1904), Calgary (1909–1913) and the Alberta towns (1912)**
- Edmonton's charter exempted improvements from 1904.
- Calgary: buildings at 80% (1909), 50% (1911) and 25% (1912–13); land 90% (1910) then 100% (1912).
- Alberta towns were put on a land-only basis by provincial law in 1912. Leduc's rate went from 20 to 42 mills.
- Alberta also imposed a 5% provincial unearned-increment tax.
- Source: [Haig pp. 106–114, 129–144, 263](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft) [P].
- Later: the cities reverted to taxing improvements after the 1913 collapse. By 1969 Alberta taxed 60% of building assessment, moving to 45% — [Arscott 1969](https://cooperative-individualism.org/arscott-f-g_site-taxation-1969-may.pdf) [S].

**B6. Pueblo (1914–15)**
- City levy only. Improvements 50% exempt in 1914 and 99% in 1915; personal property capped at 1 mill.
- For 1914, 16.3 mills on land plus half of improvements; state, county and school taxes stayed on the old base.
- Source: [Haig pp. 253–254](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft) [P].
- Later fate: not researched.

**B7. Fairhope Single Tax Corporation (1894–)**
- Private land lease on 99-year renewable terms. Rent is now set equal to the public property taxes due plus fees — [Fairhope history](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/fairhope-history) [S].

**B8. California Proposition 13 (1978–)**
- *Taxed:* Land and improvements together, at 1% plus voter-approved debt rates.
- *Valued by:* County assessors at base-year value: 1975 market value, or market value at change of ownership or new construction. Factored up by at most 2% a year (or CPI if lower).
- *Updating:* Only on sale or new construction. Before 1978, reappraisal at most every five years.
- *Revenue:* $55bn (2014–15); more than $89bn for local government (2022–23).
- *Valuations over time:* Assessed values fall further below market the longer property is held.
- Sources: [BOE Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf); [LAO 2016](https://lao.ca.gov/reports/2016/3497/common-claims-prop13-091916.pdf) [P].

**B9. Florida homestead and non-homestead regime**
- Just value, then caps (homestead: lower of 3% or CPI since the 1992 amendment; non-homestead: 10% for non-school levies, falling to 5% from 2027), then exemptions, giving taxable value.
- Homestead exemption for non-school levies rises to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028.
- Estimated local non-school revenue loss: $4.95bn (FY2027-28), $8.78bn (FY2028-29), $11.86bn recurring.
- Sources: [enrolled CS/HJR 1F](https://flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/BillText/er/PDF); [House analysis](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/Analyses/h0001z.SAC.PDF) [P].

**B10. Ontario current value assessment (CVA)**
- MPAC values all property province-wide on a four-year cycle in normal times.
- The 2020 update was postponed; 2026 taxes still use 1 January 2016 values — [MPAC](https://www.mpac.ca/en/UnderstandingYourAssessment/AssessmentCycle) [P].

**B11. Detroit split-rate proposal (2023, not enacted)**
- Buildings 20 to 6 mills; land 118 mills; revenue-neutral; voter approval required — [BridgeDetroit](https://www.bridgedetroit.com/detroits-land-value-tax-plan-explained/) [S].
- Enabling bill stalled — [Michigan Legislature](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Bills/Bill?ObjectName=2023-HB-4966) [P].

## Merge aid C: plain-language glossary (for a newcomer)

- **Ad valorem property tax:** a tax set as a percentage of a property's value. California's LAO uses the example of a $100,000 assessed value × 1% = $1,000 tax — [LAO 2016](https://lao.ca.gov/reports/2016/3497/common-claims-prop13-091916.pdf).
- **Just value, assessed value, taxable value (Florida usage):** *just value* is the market-value estimate. *Assessed value* is just value after caps and special-use classifications. *Taxable value* is assessed value minus exemptions — [FL House analysis](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/Analyses/h0001z.SAC.PDF).
- **Fractional assessment / assessment ratio:** valuing property at a set percentage of market value, such as Houston's land at 70% and buildings at 25%. Applying different ratios to land and buildings mimics a split rate — [Haig pp. 242–243](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft).
- **Mill / millage:** a tax rate expressed per $1,000 of value. One mill is $1 per $1,000, or 0.1%. Example: Altoona's 394-mill land levy — [*Altoona Mirror*](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2016/06/city-council-decides-to-cut-land-value-tax/).
- **Split-rate (two-rate or "graded") tax:** one property tax with a higher rate on land than on buildings. A **land value tax** in the pure sense sets the building rate at zero, as in Altoona from 2011 to 2016.
- **Improvements:** buildings and other structures on land. California: "all buildings, structures, fixtures, and fences" — [BOE Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf).
- **Exemption of improvements:** removing buildings from the local tax base in whole or part, as in Vancouver (1910) and Edmonton (1904).
- **Unit-foot (front-foot) valuation:** the value of a one-foot-wide strip of street frontage at a standard depth, such as 100 ft. Adjusted by **depth tables** for deeper or shallower lots and **corner tables** for corner influence — [Somers 1901](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich).
- **Reproduction-cost building valuation:** valuing a building at what it would cost to rebuild, adjusted for remaining useful life and current usefulness — [Somers 1901](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich).
- **Reassessment / revaluation:** re-estimating all values at a new date. Long gaps let assessed values drift from market values, and a sudden catch-up causes "sticker shock" ([Youngman in Governing](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html)), as in Pittsburgh in 2001.
- **Base-year (acquisition-value) assessment:** taxing on the purchase price indexed by a capped factor, rather than on current market value, as under Proposition 13 — [BOE Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf).
- **Assessment cap:** a legal limit on annual growth in assessed value, for example Florida's 3%/CPI or 10%, falling to 5%. Distinct from a **rate cap** such as California's 1%.
- **Homestead exemption:** a fixed dollar amount of a primary residence's assessed value that is not taxed.
- **Portability:** carrying a capped assessment's benefit to a new home. In Florida, a homestead held in any of the prior three years qualifies — [enrolled CS/HJR 1F](https://flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/BillText/er/PDF).
- **Uniformity clause:** a state constitutional rule that taxation be "equal and uniform" (Texas art. 8, §1). It ended Houston's plan and constrains Pennsylvania's base-year practice — [*City of Houston v. Baker*](https://hallapproved.com/tx/cases/appeals/1915/4148821); [Lincoln PA brief](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf).
- **Enabling legislation:** a state law that allows, but does not require, local governments to adopt a tax, as in the 2026 Kentucky and Virginia laws.
- **Regressive assessment:** when cheaper homes are assessed at a higher share of their market value than expensive homes, so their owners pay higher effective tax rates — [ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/cook-county-residential-property-assessment-study-results).
- **Mass appraisal / sales ratio study:** valuing many properties at once with statistical models, and checking the results by comparing assessments with actual sale prices. ProPublica refers to industry standards set by the IAAO — [ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/cook-county-residential-property-assessment-study-results).
- **Unearned increment tax:** a tax on increases in land value. Alberta took 5% for provincial purposes before 1915 — [Haig p. 263](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft).
- **Value capture:** recovering part of the land-value rise caused by public investment, for example through land value taxation in NY transit districts — [NY GML § 119-r](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GMU/119-R).

## Merge aid D: scenes and anecdotes (all sourced)

1. **The sixteen-foot map (Cleveland, 1901–02).** Tom L. Johnson's Tax School hung "a map sixteen feet square and comprising one whole ward" in City Hall and invited taxpayers to argue over street values. The agreed front-foot unit values were "written into the center of each block," signed by the board, photographed and handed to clerks, who computed values for Cleveland's 101,000 parcels. Assessments had ranged from 2% of to 68% above market value. A property owner and Republican boss sued on 8 October 1902 to stop the spending — [Johnson, *My Story*, pp. 127–130](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml).
2. **The permit office test (Houston, 1914–15).** Pastoriza claimed that nearly every builder told the permit clerk that "'Pastoriza's plan' has compelled him to build." Haig's investigator sat in the permit office for hours and "heard no applicant plead the pressure of taxation." The owner of the six-story building Pastoriza pointed to "denied flatly that the tax system had been any influence at all" — [Haig 1915, pp. 249–250](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft).
3. **"Vote against me" (Houston, 1913).** Pastoriza's campaign advertisement: "If you like the plan, vote for me; if you don't, vote against me." He won by 1,200 votes more than any other commissioner. Two years later a court enjoined his plan as unconstitutional — [Haig 1915, p. 243](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft); [*City of Houston v. Baker*](https://hallapproved.com/tx/cases/appeals/1915/4148821).
4. **"Tax the other fellow" (Edmonton, 1904).** A newspaper editor told Haig: "The single tax in Edmonton is an accident … The citizens, meeting together to decide what to tax, decided to tax the other fellow." Most vacant land belonged to non-residents — [Haig 1915, p. 107](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft).
5. **"Edmonton never!" (1915).** When the boom broke, Haig noted that "Calgary has not had a tax sale of land since 1911, Vancouver since 1909 and Edmonton never!" Meanwhile, less than half of Edmonton's 1914 taxes had been paid by the following March — [Haig 1915, p. 265](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft).
6. **Vacant lots as "a shelter for criminals" (Vancouver, 1905–06).** Arguing for heavier taxes on vacant land, Vancouver councillors said such land "served as a shelter for criminals." The 1906 vote cut building assessments to 25% — [Haig 1915, p. 186](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft).
7. **Pueblo's verdict (1915).** Pueblo's finance commissioner: "no one has gained anything by the single tax except merchants renting the buildings they occupy, who are paying this year about one-eighth the tax on their stock of goods they paid last year" — [Haig 1915, p. 254](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft).
8. **"To touch, sharply" (Calgary, 1969).** Calgary's treasurer told North American finance officers that site tax "is no panacea," noting that the Latin *taxare* means "to touch, sharply" — [Arscott 1969](https://cooperative-individualism.org/arscott-f-g_site-taxation-1969-may.pdf).
9. **394 mills (Altoona, 2016).** The only US city taxing land alone needed a 394-mill land rate, because land was only one-seventh of assessed value. Councilman Cacciotti said the high tax deterred neighbours from buying demolition-cleared lots. Council voted informally to quit — [*Altoona Mirror*](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2016/06/city-council-decides-to-cut-land-value-tax/).
