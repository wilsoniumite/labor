# Pre-modern land assessment (to c. 1850) and land / site value rating in the Global South (to 4 November 2026)

Compiled 2026-11-04. Scope: (a) how early states measured and taxed land (Egypt, Rome, imperial China, Mughal and British India, Ottoman Empire, Domesday England, France and the Physiocrats); (b) land/site value rating today in Jamaica, Kenya, South Africa and a few other developing countries. Denmark 1688, Britain after 1692, Prussia and Meiji Japan are excluded (covered by other researchers).

**Confidence labels used throughout:** **[P]** = confirmed in a primary source (statute, government statement, official record, ancient/contemporaneous text); **[S]** = secondary source only (scholarly work, official explanatory page, journalism); **[U]** = unresolved, conflicting, or seen only in a search-result snippet / low-quality source. Where I translate Chinese or Latin myself, I say so.

**Important limitation:** the shared web-search budget for this session ran out part-way through (after about a dozen searches by this researcher). After that I could only fetch pages whose URLs I already had. Several planned lines of inquiry (Ray Huang and Irfan Habib's secondary analyses, ryotwari and Bombay settlements, the indiction, Mexicali, Belize, Papua New Guinea, ICTD/World Bank papers) could therefore not be pursued. Those gaps are flagged in each section and in the Access log. Where I could not find a source I did not fill the gap with a weaker one.

## A1. Ancient Egypt: how land and harvests were assessed by the Nile flood

### Takeaway
Egyptian states taxed the **expected harvest of measured fields**. They recorded field areas, applied a standard yield per unit of area, and used the **height of each year's Nile flood** to forecast what the land would produce and so what it could pay. The flood changed every year, so assessment was renewed every year (every two years in the Old Kingdom) and never frozen. The evidence is fragmentary, temple-centred and debated.

### Cited Findings
**Nature of the evidence**
- [S] Egyptologists warn that the sources are thin: "Since even the most valuable documentary sources for taxation are essentially 'one of a kind,' it is not possible to explain fully the process of taxation or to reconstruct cogently the history of taxation in any one period." — Sally L. D. Katary, "Taxation (until the End of the Third Intermediate Period)," *UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology* (2011), p. 1–2 — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp) ([PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9p13z2vp/qt9p13z2vp.pdf))
- [S] Katary also notes that there is "no documentary evidence for the existence of a central office of tax collection for the state", although one is often assumed because the title "Chief Taxing Master" ("Great One of Assessments") appears in the Wilbour Papyrus. — [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)

**Old Kingdom "counts" and the flood record**
- [S] The Palermo Stone is "a major royal inscription detailing biennial assessments of various categories of wealth". — [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)
- [S] A royal tour along the Nile "recorded Egypt's economic wealth as measured in herds of livestock, arable fields, resources in precious metals, and human population. From the 2nd Dynasty onwards, a biennial cattle census (Tnwt) was added, followed by a 'census of gold and fields.'" — [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp), p. 4
- [S] "Records of the height of the annual Nile inundation were used to determine tax due on arable fields, establish the probability of meeting taxation goals, and remedy any shortfalls (Butzer 1976)." Katary adds that "the degree to which central authority managed to penetrate the countryside is unknown and probably unknowable (Eyre 1999: 37)." — [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp), p. 4
- [S] On timing, the biennial counts suggest "a two-year tax system", but how long it lasted "cannot however be ascertained". In New Kingdom texts the phrase "of every year" regularly follows the word for levy, which suggests taxes later became annual. — [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)

**New Kingdom: the Wilbour Papyrus (year 4 of Ramesses V, 20th Dynasty)**
- [S] "The Wilbour Papyrus provides assessment data for some 2800 plots of agricultural land in Middle Egypt under the charge of temples and secular institutions." Its "Text A records standard harvests used to calculate expected revenues." — [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp), p. 8–9
- [S] Smallholders "paid dues on their harvest on only a tiny portion of their plot at a fixed rate of 1½ sacks per aroura. These plots were usually 3 or 5 arouras in size (1 aroura equals 0.27 hectares or 0.66 acre)." Larger tracts worked by field labourers "incurred a tax of 30 percent of the harvest (Janssen 1986)." — [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp), p. 9
- [S] The papyrus "was thus probably a temple document rather than a document of the central government (Warburton 1997)." Scholars also dispute whether its revenues were taxes owed to the crown or something else. — [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)

**Greek and Roman observers**
- [P, but reports a legend] Herodotus (5th c. BC) relays an Egyptian tradition about a king (Sesostris): "this king distributed the land to all the Egyptians, giving an equal square portion to each man, and from this he made his revenue, having appointed them to pay a certain rent every year: and if the river should take away anything from any man's portion, he would come to the king and declare that which had happened, and the king used to send men to examine and to find out by measurement how much less the piece of land had become, in order that for the future the man might pay less, in proportion to the rent appointed: and I think that thus the art of geometry was found out." — Herodotus, *Histories* 2.109, trans. G. C. Macaulay — [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2707)
- [P] Strabo (Augustan era) describes the nilometer in his section on Syene and Elephantine: "The nilometer is a well on the bank of the Nile constructed with close-fitting stones… This is useful, not only to the farmers with regard to the water-distribution, embankments, canals, and other things of this kind, but also to the praefects, with regard to the revenues; for the greater rises indicate that the revenues also will be greater." — Strabo, *Geography* 17.1.48, Loeb Classical Library vol. VIII (1932) — [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/17A3*.html)

**Tax profile (Egypt, pharaonic to Roman)**
- *What was taxed:* the harvest of arable land, plus (in the Old Kingdom counts) livestock, gold and people. [S] [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)
- *How valued:* plot area (in arouras) × a "standard harvest" per unit area, adjusted for that year's flood height. [S] [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp); [P] [Strabo 17.1.48](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/17A3*.html)
- *By whom:* royal and temple scribes and assessors; the "Great One of Assessments" is the top assessment official named in the Wilbour Papyrus. [S] [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)
- *How often:* every two years in the Old Kingdom counts, apparently annual by the New Kingdom. [S] [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)
- *Rates (Wilbour):* 1½ sacks per aroura on a small taxed portion of smallholdings; 30% of the harvest on larger institutional tracts. [S] [Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)
- *What happened as assessments aged:* the flood made a fixed valuation unworkable, so assessment was recurrent by design. Herodotus's story describes remeasurement and a reduced tax when the river carried land away. [P-legend] [Herodotus 2.109](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2707)

### Inferences
- Egypt is the clearest early case of a **yield-based, annually adjusted** land assessment. The "value" was a forecast harvest (area × standard yield × flood factor), not a price or a rent. Because the flood moved every year, "stale valuation" in the modern sense hardly arose: the problem was reassessment capacity, not frozen values.
- The Herodotus passage is useful to a teacher as an origin story linking land measurement, tax relief and geometry. It should be presented as a Greek tradition, not a documented policy.

### Gaps
- The Ptolemaic and Roman procedure of annual field inspection, and tax relief for land the flood did not reach, is often described in the literature (e.g., Danielle Bonneau, *Le fisc et le Nil*, 1971; Andrew Monson, *From the Ptolemies to the Romans*, 2012). I could not access or verify it this session.
- Pliny the Elder's scale of flood heights in cubits (*Natural History* 5.58) and the "rope-stretchers" (land surveyors) were not verified; Perseus returned only navigation pages.
- Exact BC dates for the Palermo Stone and the Wilbour Papyrus were not given in the source accessed; only dynasty and regnal year.

## A2. Rome: the land tax (tributum soli), the census of land, and late-Roman reassessment

### Takeaway
Roman provincial land tax rested on a **census register built from owners' declarations**. Owners declared each farm by name, location, neighbours, area of arable, number of vines and olive trees, meadow, pasture and woodland, and valued it themselves. Officials checked the declarations and could grant relief when land was physically lost. Late-Roman censuses were vivid, coercive field-by-field measurements. I could not verify the 15-year indiction cycle in a source this session.

### Cited Findings
**The census form (forma censualis), Ulpian, early 3rd century, preserved in Justinian's Digest**
- [P] Latin text: "Forma censuali cavetur, ut agri sic in censum referantur. nomen fundi cuiusque: et in qua civitate et in quo pago sit: et quos duos vicinos proximos habeat. et arvum, quod in decem annos proximos satum erit, quot iugerum sit: vinea quot vites habeat: olivae quot iugerum et quot arbores habeant: pratum, quod intra decem annos proximos sectum erit, quot iugerum: pascua quot iugerum esse videantur: item silvae caeduae. omnia ipse qui defert aestimet." — *Digest* 50.15.4 pr. (Ulpian, *De censibus* bk 3) — [The Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/justinian/digest50.shtml)
- [P] S. P. Scott's 1932 English translation: real property "must be declared in such a way that the name of each tract shall be mentioned, and in what town or district it is situated; the names of the two nearest neighbors must be given, and how much land has been tilled or sowed in the last three years; … the number of vines in a vineyard; the number of jugera in an olive orchard, as well as the number of trees; where there are meadows, the quantity of hay cut from them within the last ten years… He who makes such a return must give an estimate of everything." — [Scott, *The Civil Law*, D.50.15.4](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/D50_Scott.htm)
- [U] **Translation discrepancy:** the Latin says *decem* (ten) years for arable land, but Scott gives "three years". The Latin also uses future-perfect verbs ("will have been sown / mown within the next ten years"), which some readers take as forward-looking. My own literal reading: "how many iugera of arable land will have been sown in the ten years [of the census period]". Treat Scott's "three years" as unreliable here.
- [P] On relief when the land itself changes: the assessor (*censitor*) should relieve an owner who cannot use land recorded in the public registers; "where a part of his land has been swallowed up by an earthquake, he should be relieved from taxation upon it by the assessor. If his vines have died, or his trees have dried up, it is unjust that… he should be included". But if the owner himself cut down trees or vines, he must still declare the number that existed at the census (Latin: "nihilo minus eum numerum profiteri iubetur, qui fuit census tempore"). — *Digest* 50.15.4.1 — [Scott translation](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/D50_Scott.htm); [Latin](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/justinian/digest50.shtml)

**A late-Roman census in action (hostile Christian source)**
- [P, polemical] Lactantius, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors*, ch. 23, describes a tetrarchic census: "Surveyors having been spread abroad, and occupied in a general and severe scrutiny"; "Each spot of ground was measured, vines and fruit-trees numbered, lists taken of animals of every kind". He also describes torture of family members and slaves to extract declarations and says repeated surveys raised the levy. — [New Advent (ANF vol. 7)](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0705.htm)

**Citizen tributum (background)**
- [S, dated] Smith's *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities* (1875; article by Leonhard Schmitz) describes the Republican *tributum* as "a direct tax upon objects without any regard to their produce, like a land or house tax", usually "one for every thousand of a man's fortune" and raised to three per thousand in Cato's time. Citizens were exempted after the Macedonian war until 43 BC; afterwards it was "imposed according to the discretion of the emperors". — [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Tributum.html)
- [U] **Date conflict:** as extracted, Smith's article dates the exemption to after the Macedonian war in "147 BC". The date usually given in modern accounts, 167 BC after Aemilius Paullus's victory, could not be checked this session. Unresolved.

**Tax profile (Roman provincial land tax, as far as verified)**
- *What was taxed:* provincial agricultural land, described by use class (arable, vineyard, olive, meadow, pasture, timber). [P] [D.50.15.4](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/justinian/digest50.shtml)
- *How valued:* by owner self-declaration of area and productive units (iugera, numbers of vines and trees), with the declarant giving his own valuation ("omnia ipse qui defert aestimet"). [P] [D.50.15.4](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/justinian/digest50.shtml)
- *By whom:* the owner declares; a *censitor* checks the declaration and grants relief for physical loss. [P] [D.50.15.4.1](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/D50_Scott.htm)
- *How often / aging:* the census register ran for a period. The ten-year reference in the census form suggests a multi-year horizon (inference). Relief was granted for physical loss (earthquake, dead vines) but not for owner-caused loss. [P] [D.50.15.4.1](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/D50_Scott.htm)

### Inferences
- Rome combined **self-assessment** with official verification, an approach that reappears in Jamaica's pre-1956 "ingivings" (section B1). Its relief rules covered **physical** change in the land, not changes in prices or rents.
- Lactantius gives a newcomer a strong scene of how intrusive area-and-count assessment could be, but it is a hostile source written to condemn persecuting emperors.

### Gaps
- **Indiction:** the 15-year indiction cycle (conventionally counted from AD 312) and its relation to periodic reassessment could not be verified. Britannica returned 403, and the LacusCurtius/Smith "Indictio" page returned 404.
- **Diocletian's *iugatio–capitatio*** (abstract units of taxable land, *iugum*, and persons, *caput*; the Syro-Roman lawbook definitions) was not verified. A. H. M. Jones, "Census Records of the Later Roman Empire," *JRS* (1953) and "Capitation and Iugatio," *JRS* 47 (1957) are the standard leads, but the Cambridge Core host could not be resolved from this session.
- Definitions of *tributum soli* and *stipendium*, Italy's immunity (*ius Italicum*) and the frequency of Augustan provincial censuses were not verified.

## A3. Imperial China: equal-field to Two-Tax (780), Ming registers, the Wanli survey and Single Whip, Qing fixed quotas

### Takeaway
Chinese fiscal history runs from **taxing allotted adults** (the Tang equal-field system with flat per-adult dues) to **taxing land and wealth where people actually lived** (Yang Yan's Two-Tax method under Emperor Dezong, conventionally 780). It then moves to **detailed parcel registers** (the Ming fish-scale registers of 1387) and to **silver levied per mu of land** (the Single Whip, implemented empire-wide in 1581). The main official histories say plainly that registers decayed: names, acreages and wealth grades no longer matched reality. The Wanli remeasurement was distorted by officials shrinking the measuring rod to inflate acreage. Under the Qing, official land-tax revenue was a **largely fixed target** while population roughly tripled.

### Cited Findings
**Tang equal-field allotment and per-adult dues (translations mine)**
- [P] *Xin Tang shu* (New Tang History) 51, Treatise on Food and Money 1: each adult (*ding*) and each male aged 18 or over received "人一頃，其八十畝為口分，二十畝為永業". That is one *qing* (100 *mu*), of which 80 *mu* were "personal-share" land returned at death and 20 *mu* were "perpetual" land. — [zh.wikisource, 新唐書 卷051](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%96%B0%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7051)
- [P] Dues were levied per adult, not per unit of land value. Grain tax (*zu*): "丁歲輸粟二斛，稻三斛，謂之租" (each adult yearly 2 *hu* of millet, or 3 *hu* of rice). Labour (*yong*): "歲二十日，閏加二日，不役者日為絹三尺" (20 days a year, 22 in a leap year; those not called up pay 3 *chi* of silk per day). A cloth levy (*diao*) was also due. — [新唐書 卷051](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%96%B0%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7051)
- [P] Registration: each village kept household self-declarations, and "歲終具民之年與地之闊陿，為鄉帳" (at year's end the people's ages and the extent of their land were set out in the township account). Under Kaiyuan 16 (728, my conversion) an edict ordered "每三歲以九等定籍" (every three years, register households in nine grades). — [新唐書 卷051](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%96%B0%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7051)
- [P] The treatise's own verdict on collapse: "自天寶以來，大盜屢起，方鎮數叛，兵革之興，累世不息" (from the Tianbao era, great rebellions repeatedly broke out and provincial commands rebelled); and "蓋口分、世業之田壞而為兼幷，租、庸、調之法壞而為兩稅" ("the personal-share and hereditary fields collapsed into engrossment by the powerful; the *zu-yong-diao* laws collapsed into the Two Taxes"). — [新唐書 卷051](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%96%B0%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7051)

**Yang Yan's Two-Tax method (*liangshui fa*)**
- [P] Yang Yan's diagnosis of decayed registers: "丁口轉死，非舊名矣；田畝移換，非舊額矣；貧富升降，非舊第矣" ("the registered adults have moved or died, so the names are no longer the old ones; fields have changed hands, so the acreages are no longer the old figures; the rich and poor have risen and fallen, so the grades are no longer the old ones"; translation mine). — *Jiu Tang shu* 118, biography of Yang Yan — [zh.wikisource, 舊唐書 卷118](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%88%8A%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7118)
- [P] Principles: "凡百役之費，一錢之斂，先度其數而賦於人，量出以制入" ("estimate expenditure first, then assess it on the people: measure outgoings to set incomings"); "戶無主客，以見居為簿；人無丁中，以貧富為差" ("households are registered where they actually live, whether native or immigrant; persons are graded by wealth, not by adult/minor status"). Collections were twice a year: "夏稅無過六月，秋稅無過十一月" (summer tax by the 6th month, autumn tax by the 11th). The land-tax base was pegged to the cultivated acreage of Dali 14 (779). — [舊唐書 卷118](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%88%8A%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7118)
- [U] The page says only that Emperor Dezong "approved and implemented it" (德宗善而行之). The conventional date of 780 (Jianzhong 1) is not stated on the page accessed.

**Ming registers (translations mine; reign-year conversions mine)**
- [P] Yellow Registers, Hongwu 14 (1381): "詔天下編賦役黃冊，以一百十戶為一里…" (households grouped 110 to a *li* for registers of taxes and services), so called because the copies sent to the Ministry of Revenue had yellow covers. — *Ming shi* 77 — [zh.wikisource, 明史 卷77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777)
- [P] Fish-scale map registers, Hongwu 20 (1387): "區設糧長四人，量度田畝方圓，次以字號，悉書主名及田之丈尺，編類為冊，狀如魚鱗，號曰魚鱗圖冊" ("each district appointed four tax captains, who measured each field's shape and dimensions, numbered the fields in sequence, recorded the owner's name and the field's measurements, and compiled them into registers that looked like fish scales, called fish-scale map registers"). — [明史 卷77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777)
- [P] Registered land totals in the *Ming shi*: Hongwu 26 (1393): **8,507,623 *qing***; Hongzhi 15 (1502): "only" **4,228,058 *qing***. After the Wanli survey: **7,013,976 *qing***, "視弘治時贏三百萬頃" (3 million *qing* more than in the Hongzhi period). — [明史 卷77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777)
- [P] The Wanli survey under Zhang Juzheng: "帝用大學士張居正議，天下田畝通行丈量，限三載竣事" (the emperor adopted Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng's proposal that all fields in the realm be measured within three years). And the criticism: "然居正尚綜核，頗以溢額為功。有司爭改小弓以求田多，或掊克見田以充虛額" ("but Zhang prized strict verification and treated surpluses over quota as merit; officials vied to shrink their measuring bows to find more land, or squeezed existing fields to fill fictitious quotas"). — [明史 卷77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777)

**Single Whip (*yitiao bianfa*)**
- [P] "一條鞭法者，總括一州縣之賦役，量地計丁，丁糧畢輸於官" (the Single Whip gathered all the land taxes and services of a department or county, measuring land and counting adults). All levies were "悉並為一條，皆計畝徵銀，折辦於官，故謂之一條鞭" ("combined into a single item, all levied in silver reckoned per *mu* of land and commuted for payment to officials, hence the name"). "嘉靖間，數行數止，至萬曆九年乃盡行之" (tried and halted several times in the Jiajing era; implemented everywhere only in Wanli 9, i.e. 1581). — *Ming shi* 78 — [zh.wikisource, 明史 卷78](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B778)
- [P] The register–reality gap: when "田鬻富室，產去糧存，而猶輸丁賦" (fields were sold to rich households, the property was gone but the tax-grain obligation stayed with the seller), and the abuses of shifting and "scattering" tax liabilities "相沿不改" (continued unchanged). — [明史 卷78](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B778)

**Qing: a fixed land-tax target**
- [S] "By the 18th century, the Qing had largely consolidated most poll taxes into a fixed-target land tax despite their territorial and population expansion. Overall, the land tax accounted for 70% of total taxation." Official expenditure averaged about 36 million silver taels, with a standard deviation of only 3.2, for 1662–1849, while "the population nearly tripled and the territory under control nearly doubled". Per-capita revenue fell, "reflecting the combined effect of a fixed revenue target accompanied by explosive population expansion". — Debin Ma & Jared Rubin, "The Paradox of Power," LSE Economic History WP 261 (2017), pp. 6–7 — [PDF](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/75218/1/WP261.pdf)
- [S] The Yongzheng emperor's reforms "from 1724 increased surcharges to land taxes and essentially legitimized previously 'illegal' local extractions", but "had to be largely abandoned toward the end of the 18th century" (citing Madeleine Zelin, *The Magistrate's Tael*, 1984). Chang Chung-li estimated unofficial income of officials above the provincial level at 63 million taels, "81% of the total official tax quota around 1884". — [Ma & Rubin 2017](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/75218/1/WP261.pdf)

**Tax profile (China, verified points)**
- *Tang (to the 8th century):* taxed adults at flat rates (grain, cloth, labour), with land allotted per adult; registers compiled at village level each year and households graded every three years (from 728). [P] [新唐書 51](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%96%B0%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7051)
- *Two-Tax:* assessed on households where resident, graded by wealth, with land tax pegged to 779 acreage; collected in summer and autumn; total set by expenditure needs ("measure outgoings to set incomings"). [P] [舊唐書 118](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%88%8A%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7118)
- *Ming:* parcel registers measured by district tax captains (1387); national remeasurement in the Wanli era, distorted by quota pressure; Single Whip converted levies to silver per *mu* (1581). [P] [明史 77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777), [明史 78](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B778)
- *Qing:* fixed official revenue target with large unofficial surcharges. [S] [Ma & Rubin 2017](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/75218/1/WP261.pdf)

### Inferences
- Yang Yan's sentence about names, acreages and grades no longer matching is perhaps the clearest pre-modern statement of the **aging-assessment problem**. It anticipates the modern pattern: a register that is not revised drifts away from reality and the burden shifts to whoever cannot escape it.
- The Ming totals show that registered acreage is a **political quantity**: it halved between 1393 and 1502 in the record, then rose by 3 million *qing* in the Wanli remeasurement, partly through officials shrinking measuring rods. A newcomer should not read these as true cultivated areas (inference; see Gaps).
- The Single Whip moved China toward an **area-based money tax** on land, but the base was still recorded acreage with fixed quotas, not value.

### Gaps

Ma & Rubin cite Huang only on political institutions.

## A4. Mughal India: Todar Mal's *zabt* under Akbar, the measuring rod, the ten-year settlement, and what replaced it

### Takeaway
Akbar's *zabt* measured land with a standardized rod, classified it by how continuously it was cultivated, estimated an **average yield** for each crop, claimed **one-third** of that yield for the state, and converted the claim into **cash rates**. Those rates came from a **ten-year average** of past collections. This was an early attempt to keep assessments stable but tied to recent reality. In Bengal the Mughals never applied measurement-based *zabt*; revenue there was a summary assessment on zamindars. In the 18th century Bengal moved toward chakla-based collection and, under the British, revenue farming.

### Cited Findings
All Ain-i-Akbari quotations are from Abu'l Fazl, *Ain-i-Akbari*, vol. II, trans. H. S. Jarrett, 2nd ed. corrected and annotated by Jadunath Sarkar (Calcutta: Royal Asiatic Society of Bengal, Bibliotheca Indica no. 271, 1949), Digital Library of India scan — [archive.org PDF](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf). The scan is OCR'd and has errors; I have silently corrected obvious OCR noise.

**The measuring instrument (Ain IX, "The Tanab", pp. 66–67)**
- [P] "The Tanab (tent rope) was in Hindustan a measure of hempen rope twisted which became shorter or longer according to the dryness or moisture of the atmosphere. It would be left in the dew and thus fraudfully moistened. Oftentimes it would be employed in the early morning when it had got damp and had shrunk, and by the end of the day it had become dry and had lengthened. In the former case, the husbandmen suffered loss, in the latter the royal revenues were diminished. In the 19th year of the Divine era, the jarib was made of bamboos joined by iron rings. Thus it is subject to no variation, and the relief to the public was felt everywhere while the hand of dishonest greed was shortened." — [Ain-i-Akbari II](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)
  - [U] The OCR renders the regnal year as "lQth"; I read it as "19th". The Western date (c. 1574–75, counting Akbar's Ilahi era from his 1556 accession) is my conversion.
- [P] (Ain X, "The Bigha") The bigha was 60 × 60 gaz = 3,600 square gaz. The hemp tanab of 60 gaz "in the twisting … shrank to 56", and a bigha measured by hemp was smaller than one measured by bamboo: "This makes a difference of 10 bigha in a hundred." — [Ain-i-Akbari II](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)

**Classification and the state's share (Ain XI, pp. 68–69)**
- [P] Land classes: *Polaj* (cultivated every season), *Parauti* (left fallow for a time to recover), *Chachar* (fallow three or four years), *Banjar* (uncultivated five years or more). "Of the two first kinds of land, there are three classes, good, middling and bad. They add together the produce of each sort, and a third of this represents the medium produce, one-third part of which is exacted as the royal dues." Sher Khan's (Sher Shah Sur's) revenue schedule is mentioned as the lowest rate of assessment, and "for the convenience of the cultivators and the soldiery, the value was taken in ready money." — [Ain-i-Akbari II](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)

**The Ten Years' Settlement (Ain 15, pp. 94–95)**
- [P] Before the reform, officials "annually engaged in noting the current prices and reporting them to His Majesty, and taking the gross produce and estimating its value, they determined the rates of collection, but this mode was attended with considerable inconvenience." In the 16th regnal year Muzaffar Khan and Rajah Todar Mull made a fresh settlement through the *qanungos*, who were local revenue record-keepers. Then: "From the beginning of the 15th year of the Divine era to the 24th, an aggregate of the rates of collection was formed and a tenth of the total was fixed as the annual assessment; but from the 20th to the 24th year the collections were accurately determined and the five former ones accepted on the authority of persons of probity." — [Ain-i-Akbari II](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)
- [S] Moreland & Yusuf Ali (1918) treat the Ain as the official theory of Akbar's revenue system; they say "practice tended to approximate to theory" and call the reform "one of his greatest administrative achievements". — W. H. Moreland & A. Yusuf Ali, "Akbar's Land-Revenue System as described in the 'Āin-i-Akbari'," *JRAS* 50(1) (1918): 1–42 — [Cambridge Core (abstract only)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/i-akbars-landrevenue-system-as-described-in-the-ainiakbari/121BA844BCE99268E323862D83682F75)

**Bengal: no measurement, then reorganization**
- [S] The Todarmal Settlement of Bengal (1582) "had initiated the zamindari system in the far-flung subah of Bengal"; Akbar's 1582 system of direct settlement with cultivators was not feasible there. Zamindars' payments were set by *nasaq*, a summary assessment decided "neither by a proper measurement of land nor by an assessment of the produce thereof." — [Banglapedia, "Zamindar"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)
- [S] Murshid Quli Khan's *mal-zamini* reorganization (1722) cut Bengal's divisions to 13 *chaklahs* from 34 *sarkars*; the *chaklahdars* were "officials, not owners". Under Warren Hastings, zamindars were displaced by "the highest bidding ijaradars or revenue farmers", with zamindars receiving a 10% allowance (*malikana*). The quinquennial farming system failed and expired in 1777. — [Banglapedia, "Zamindar"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)

**Tax profile (Akbar's zabt, as described in the Ain)**
- *What was taxed:* cultivated land by crop and season, in measured *bighas*. [P] [Ain IX–XI](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)
- *How valued:* area (bamboo *jarib*) × average yield (mean of good/middling/bad land) × one-third state share, commuted to cash. [P] [Ain XI](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)
- *By whom:* imperial revenue officials using *qanungo* records; Todar Mal and Muzaffar Khan in charge in the 16th regnal year. [P] [Ain 15](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)
- *How often updated:* earlier annual price-reporting was replaced by rates fixed from a **ten-year aggregate** (years 15–24 of the reign, one-tenth of the total as the annual assessment). [P] [Ain 15](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)
- *Where it did not apply:* Bengal, which used summary *nasaq* assessment on zamindars. [S] [Banglapedia](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)

### Inferences
- Zabt reads like a modern **mass-appraisal schedule**. It used standard units (Ilahi gaz, bamboo jarib), a classification of land, crop-specific standard yields and a moving-average price component (the ten-year aggregate). That damped year-to-year volatility without freezing values for ever, a middle path between Egypt's annual reassessment and Bengal's 1793 perpetual fix.
- The dew-soaked rope is an excellent teaching anecdote: the **measuring instrument itself** was a site of fraud in both directions, and standardizing it was framed as protecting both peasant and treasury.

### Gaps
- Irfan Habib, *The Agrarian System of Mughal India 1556–1707* (1963; 2nd ed. 1999), on the geographic extent of zabt (core provinces only), the later dominance of *jagir* assignment and revenue farming (*ijara*), and the 17th–18th-century "jagirdari crisis" was **not verified** (search budget exhausted; no open text).
- The meaning of "dahsala" as used by later historians, the nineteen-years' rates tables (Ain 14) and the *dastur-ul-amal* cash-rate schedules were seen in the Ain but not analysed.
- Banglapedia's "Todar Mal" page could not be located (404 at two titles).

## A5. British India: the Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793) versus temporary and periodic settlements

### Takeaway
In 1793 the East India Company fixed Bengal's land revenue demand on zamindars **"for all time to come"**, made them proprietors, and enforced payment by auctioning defaulters' estates (the "sunset law"). In the first years a large share of estates changed hands. Later, as rents and cultivation grew while the state's demand stayed fixed, zamindari income rose and sales became rare. The British abandoned the permanent model for the rest of India in favour of periodic settlements. Economists dispute the long-run effects; a prominent study links landlord-based areas to lower post-independence investment.

### Cited Findings
- [S] "Cornwallis concluded the permanent settlement with zamindars in March 1793." Zamindars, recognized as proprietors without payment, had to pay a revenue demand that would not change "for all time to come", payable in twelve monthly instalments (*kists*) at the district collectorate. Their judicial powers were removed. — [Banglapedia, "Zamindar"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)
- [S] The Revenue Sale Law: Regulation 14 of 1793 provided for recovery of arrears by public auction of defaulters' land. Land could be sold at the end of any month in which an instalment was in default, so sales were "as inevitable and as certain as the sun-set". Zamindars "could never claim any remission of revenue on account of any natural calamity." — [Banglapedia, "Revenue Sale Law, 1793"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Revenue_Sale_Law,_1793)
- [S] Scale of turnover: "Within seven years, nearly half of the landed property of zamindars changed hands." Within a decade large estates, including Rajshahi, Nadia, Bishnupur, Dinajpur and largely Burdwan, were broken up and transferred, and "the law courts were flooded with litigation". Regulation 7 of 1799 replaced month-end sales with year-end auctions. — [Banglapedia, "Revenue Sale Law, 1793"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Revenue_Sale_Law,_1793); also "Hundreds of zamindari estates were sold under the operation of the Sunset Law" — [Banglapedia, "Zamindar"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)
- [S] Effect of a frozen demand: from the mid-19th century, rising rents and population growth increased zamindari income "while the fixed government demand stayed the same", so default sales became rare in the late 19th century. — [Banglapedia, "Revenue Sale Law, 1793"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Revenue_Sale_Law,_1793)
- [S] The Settlement "did not include all the lands of Bengal". Temporarily settled estates were governed by Regulation VII of 1822 and Regulation IX of 1833, with revenue "variable and periodically revised". Lease terms "ranged from minimum five years to maximum hundred years". The 1879 Sundarbans wasteland rules set a 40-year original lease, then re-assessment every 30 years. "The rent level of temporarily settled estates was generally much higher than that of the zamindari estates." — [Banglapedia, "Temporary Settlement"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Temporary_Settlement)
- [S] Within about ten years the government concluded that "the permanent settlement was not yielding the results expected from the system… The idea of permanent settlement was thus abandoned for the rest of British India." — [Banglapedia, "Temporary Settlement"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Temporary_Settlement)
- [S] Later history: Bengal Tenancy Act 1885; the Floud Commission recommended abolition. — [Banglapedia, "Zamindar"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar). [U] **Date conflict on abolition:** "Zamindar" says abolished under the East Bengal State Acquisition and Tenancy Act **1950**; "Temporary Settlement" says the East Bengal State Acquisition Act abolished it in **1951**. The difference is possibly between enactment and operation; unresolved.
- [S, peer-reviewed; disputed] Long-run consequences: districts where the British gave property rights in land to landlords rather than cultivators "had lower agricultural investment and productivity" after independence and invested less in health and education; the authors argue that "differences in historical institutions lead to very different policy choices." — Abhijit Banerjee & Lakshmi Iyer, "History, Institutions, and Economic Performance: The Legacy of Colonial Land Tenure Systems in India," *American Economic Review* 95(4) (2005): 1190–1213 — [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0002828054825574)
  - [U] A published re-examination exists, by Vegard Iversen, Richard Palmer-Jones and Kunal Sen in the *Journal of Development Studies* (2013); the reference is from memory and should be verified. I did **not** access it, so its findings are not reported. The writer should present Banerjee–Iyer as influential but contested.

**Tax profile (Permanent Settlement)**
- *What was taxed:* zamindari estates (the revenue right over villages), not individual plots. [S] [Banglapedia](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)
- *How valued:* a fixed money demand (*jama*) per estate, set once at settlement and never revised. [S] [Banglapedia](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)
- *Updates:* none by design; there was no remission even for natural calamity. [S] [Banglapedia RSL](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Revenue_Sale_Law,_1793)
- *Enforcement:* auction of estates for arrears, monthly from 1793 and year-end from 1799. [S] [Banglapedia RSL](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Revenue_Sale_Law,_1793)
- *What happened over time:* heavy early turnover, then growing zamindari surplus as rents rose under a fixed demand, then abolition in 1950/51. [S] [Banglapedia](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Revenue_Sale_Law,_1793)

### Inferences
- The Permanent Settlement is the **limiting case of a frozen valuation**. Fixing the assessment for ever made it harsh at first, when collection pressure and auctions were intense, and increasingly light later, as land values and rents rose and the gain went to the zamindars and their under-tenure holders rather than the state.
- The British reaction (periodic resettlement, often on multi-decade terms) is the institutional ancestor of statutory revaluation cycles.

### Gaps
- The primary text of Bengal Regulation I of 1793 (the proclamation's wording and the total *jama*) was **not located**: the Bangladesh laws page tried was a different Act, and searches were exhausted.
- Ryotwari (Thomas Munro, Madras), the Bombay survey settlements and the North-Western Provinces (mahalwari) settlements under Regulation VII of 1822 and IX of 1833, including term lengths and assessment bases, were **not verified** for all-India; only the Bengal temporary-settlement context above is sourced.
- Ranajit Guha, *A Rule of Property for Bengal* (1963), on Physiocratic influence on the settlement's designers, was not accessed.

## A6. Ottoman Empire: tahrir surveys and their decline

### Takeaway
From the 15th century, the Ottoman state periodically surveyed each province and recorded adult males, approximate land in use and the **expected** tax yield of every village (an average of the last three years), to assign revenues to timar holders and officials. More than 1,500 registers survive. After the 16th century new general surveys of already-held lands became uncommon. The reasons and the replacement institutions could not be verified this session.

### Cited Findings
- [S] "The Ottoman government obtained current information on the empire's sources of revenue through periodic registers called tahrir defterleri." They were used "as official registers to establish legal claims to land, assess the empire's expected tax revenues, and appropriate some of the revenues to the military and administrative officials as remuneration for their services." — Metin M. Coşgel, "Ottoman Tax Registers (Tahrir Defterleri)," UConn Economics WP 2002-47 (rev. Feb. 2003), published in *Historical Methods* 37 (2004): 87–100 — [PDF](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf), pp. 1, 4
- [S] Detailed (*mufassal*) registers "recorded for each fiscal unit the names, numbers, and legal status of adult males, approximate amounts of land in use, and estimates of tax revenues from all productive resources and activities", and are "similar to the English Domesday book but wider in spatial and temporal coverage." "Over 1,500 of them have survived." The oldest in the Turkish archives is that of Albania, dated **1431–32**. — [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf), p. 4–5
- [S] Valuation rule: "The enumerators were indeed instructed to record not the amounts of taxes collected during the year of the survey but the expected amounts based on the averages of the last three years." — [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf), p. 8
- [S] At the start of each province's register a law code (*kanunname*) "laid down the basic tax regulations of the province and specified the rates at which each resource was to be taxed". A sample village entry values tithes in kind at set prices, e.g. "Wheat: 120 kile, at 9 akçe per kile, 1080 akçe". — [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf), notes and Appendix A
- [S] Decline: "the practice of carrying out new surveys of the tax revenues of previously conquered lands became uncommon after the sixteenth century", though new registers were still made for newly conquered or reconquered lands (e.g., Kamaniçe, dated 1681). — [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf), note 2

**Tax profile (tahrir system)**
- *What was taxed:* households (adult males), land in use, and produce through tithes in kind and dues; recorded as expected revenue per village. [S] [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf)
- *How valued:* a three-year average of expected yields, priced at set rates in akçe. [S] [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf)
- *By whom:* state-appointed enumerators working under the provincial *kanunname*. [S] [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf)
- *How often:* "periodic"; fresh general surveys became uncommon after the 1500s. [S] [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf)

### Inferences
- Like Akbar's ten-year aggregate, the Ottoman three-year average was a deliberate **smoothing** device. Its weakness was that the registered expected yield stayed fixed until the next survey, and after the 16th century resurveys stopped for most of the empire.

### Gaps
- The typical interval between surveys (often said to be about 20–30 years), the shift to *avarız* household registers, and the rise of tax farming (*iltizam*) and life-term tax farms (*malikane*, conventionally from 1695) were **not verified**. Cambridge Core (Coşgel & Miceli, *JEH* 2005) could not be resolved from this session, and the search budget was exhausted.

## A7. England: the Domesday survey (1086) and the geld

### Takeaway
William I ordered the survey at Christmas 1085 at Gloucester, soon after levying a heavy geld of 72 pence per hide. The commissioners asked how many hides each shire contained, who held land, what stock was on it, what the king's dues were and how much each holding was worth in 1066 and 1086. Domesday is a **valuation of landed property**, not a census. Its hides were assessment units used for the geld.

### Cited Findings
- [P] Geld: in the Chronicle's 1083 annal, "after mid-winter, the king ordained a large and heavy contribution over all England; that was, upon each hide of land, two and seventy pence." — *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, trans. J. Ingram — [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/657). 72 pence = 6 shillings (12d. to the shilling). Because it was "after mid-winter", the levy probably fell in early 1084 (inference).
- [P] Commissioning (1085 annal): at midwinter in Gloucester the king held "very deep consultation with his council, about this land; how it was occupied, and by what sort of men. Then sent he his men over all England into each shire; commissioning them to find out 'How many hundreds of hides were in the shire, what land the king himself had, and what stock upon the land; or, what dues he ought to have by the year from the shire.'" They were also to record what each landholder had "either in land or in stock, and how much money it were worth." — [ASC](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/657)
- [P] The famous complaint: "So very narrowly, indeed, did he commission them to trace it out, that there was not one single hide, nor a yard of land, nay, moreover (it is shameful to tell, though he thought it no shame to do it), not even an ox, nor a cow, nor a swine was there left, that was not set down in his writ." — [ASC](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/657)
- [S, official] "The survey was ordered by William the Conqueror at Christmas 1085" and carried out in 1086. It was "a survey and valuation of landed property", not a census. Commissioners held sworn inquests in local courts and asked each question for three dates: the state of affairs "as it had been on the last day of the reign of Edward the Confessor (5 January 1066)" (abbreviated TRE), the date the land was granted by William, and 1086. Recorded items include hides, plough teams, classes of people, woodland, meadow, pasture, mills, fisheries and values in 1066 and 1086. — [The National Archives, research guide "Domesday Book"](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/domesday-book/)

**Tax profile (geld as surveyed in 1086)**
- *What was taxed:* land assessed in **hides** (fiscal units). [P] [ASC 1083](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/657)
- *How valued:* the geld was a flat rate per hide (72d. in 1083/84). Domesday separately recorded annual **values** of holdings (1066 vs 1086) and plough capacity. [P] [ASC](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/657); [S] [TNA](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/domesday-book/)
- *By whom:* royal commissioners taking sworn local evidence. [S] [TNA](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/domesday-book/)

### Inferences
- Domesday captures two kinds of valuation side by side: an inherited **conventional assessment** (hides) and **current annual values**. That gap is exactly what later revaluation debates are about.

### Gaps
- The long scholarly debate over whether Domesday was mainly a **geld book** or a feudal register, the evidence that hidage was often an old conventional ("beneficial") assessment out of line with Domesday values, the fact that Domesday was never revised, and the decline of the geld (commonly said to be last levied in 1162) were **not verified**. Britannica "Danegeld" returned 403; the TNA "making of Domesday" page returned 404; the search budget was exhausted.

## A8. France: the Napoleonic parcel cadastre (law of 15 September 1807) and the Physiocrats' single tax

### Takeaway
The finance law of 15 September 1807 launched a **parcel-by-parcel cadastre**: every plot mapped, classed and given a taxable revenue, as the base for the land tax. It took until about 1850 to complete in mainland France. Its great weakness was **immutability**: plans and valuations were not kept up to date. From 1850 communes could revise at their own cost, and a general renovation came only with the law of 16 April 1930. Earlier, the Physiocrats (1750s–60s) had argued for a **single tax on the net product of land**. They remained an influential idea rather than a working tax.

### Cited Findings
- [S, official archive guide] "15 septembre 1807 : la naissance du Cadastre 'napoléonien'"; "1807-1850 : réalisation du plan napoléonien". "Le gros défaut du Plan Napoléonien était son absence de mise à jour, selon le principe de l'immuabilité du Plan. Déjà les cantons qui l'avaient réalisés depuis plus de 30 ans obtiennent en 1850 l'autorisation le réviser mais à leurs frais." "La loi du 16 avril 1930 prescrivit alors une révision exceptionnelle des évaluations foncières et pose le principe d'une rénovation générale du cadastre." — Archives départementales de la Creuse, research guide — [PDF](https://archives.creuse.fr/fichier/256725/23620)
- [U, search-result snippets only; pages not read in full] Another departmental archive describes the cadastre as a fiscal document "afin de permettre le calcul de l'impôt sur les terres et les bâtiments". The *Encyclopaedia Universalis* and the Orléans archives say the work was finished around 1850 for mainland France and later in recently annexed territories. The Indre-et-Loire archives give 1808 for the start of parcel-plan work, 1811 for the *Recueil méthodique* (the cadastral instruction manual), and a law of 7 August 1850 allowing communes to request revision at their own expense. Departmental completion dates varied, e.g. Haute-Vienne 1809–1842 and Isère 1808–1838. Leads: [Archives de Touraine](https://archives.touraine.fr/document/l-etablissement-du-cadastre-napoleonien); [Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/cadastre/); [Orléans archives](https://archives.orleans-metropole.fr/fichier/16108/200)
- [S, lead] A scholarly volume on European cadastres exists: *De l'estime au cadastre en Europe. Les systèmes cadastraux aux XIXe et XXe siècles* (IGPDE) — [OpenEdition](https://books.openedition.org/igpde/10793). Not read this session.

**Physiocrats (idea only)**
- [S] The Physiocrats held that, because land is the only source of net wealth, "the burden of all taxes ultimately bears down on the landowner", so a direct tax on land rents was the efficient choice. The History of Economic Thought website dates Quesnay's *Tableau économique* to both 1758 and 1759 in different places, and treats Du Pont's *Physiocratie* (1767) as the definitive statement. — [HET, "The Physiocrats"](https://www.hetwebsite.net/het/schools/physioc.htm)
- [S] The marquis de Mirabeau "was the primary architect of the Physiocrats' 'single tax' doctrine", set out in *Théorie de l'impôt* (1760). The book was controversial. He was "hauled off to jail at Vincennes in December 1760" and on release was "warned to stay confined to his rural estates in Bignon and refrain from writing again". — [HET, "Mirabeau"](https://www.hetwebsite.net/het/profiles/mirabeau.htm)
- [U] The HET school page says the single tax was "instituted" during Turgot's tenure as controller-general (1774–76) and reversed after his fall. I could **not corroborate** this. It runs against the usual description of the single tax as never implemented, and the brief treats it as an idea only. Unresolved; the writer should not rely on it without checking.

**Tax profile (Napoleonic cadastre as tax infrastructure)**
- *What was mapped and valued:* every parcel of land and building, for the land tax. [S] [Creuse](https://archives.creuse.fr/fichier/256725/23620)
- *How often updated:* in principle not at all ("immuabilité du Plan"). From 1850, cantons whose cadastre was over 30 years old could revise at their own cost; general renovation only from 1930. [S] [Creuse](https://archives.creuse.fr/fichier/256725/23620)

### Inferences
- France shows the **cost of a one-off cadastre**: it took about 43 years (1807 to about 1850) to complete, and was then frozen. By the time the last communes were finished, the earliest valuations were decades old. This is the same aging problem seen in Ming China and modern Kenya.

### Gaps
- The *contribution foncière* of 1790/91 and its apportionment method (a quota divided among departments, communes and owners), the abandoned 1802–1807 cadastre "par masses de culture", Napoleon's often-quoted remark linking the cadastre to the Civil Code, and Roger Kain & Elizabeth Baigent, *The Cadastral Map in the Service of the State* (1992) were **not verified**.
- Quesnay's own *impôt unique* texts and Turgot's actual reforms were not verified.

## B1. Jamaica: property tax on unimproved land value

### Takeaway
Jamaica has taxed land on **unimproved value** (land alone, ignoring buildings and crops) under the Land Valuation Act of 1956. Before that, under the 1901 Valuation Law, owners declared their own values ("ingivings"). The National Land Agency (NLA) values; Tax Administration Jamaica (TAJ) collects. The law calls for revaluation every five years, but general revaluations have come roughly every ten years (1974, 1983, 1992/93, 2002, 2013). Implementation lagged: the 2013 values took effect only on 1 April 2017. Bills in 2026 still rest on 2013 values. An island-wide revaluation of about 900,000 parcels began on 1 July 2026, aimed at fiscal year 2027/28. Reported compliance has been around 50–60%.

### Cited Findings
**Origins and design**
- [S] Under the **1901 Valuation Law** the tax comprised a quit rent of 1 penny per acre, a house tax and a crop tax. Owners had to supply three values ("ingivings"): gross present value, net annual rental value, and "the value of the land in its natural and unimproved state". — W. J. McCluskey & R. C. D. Franzsen, *Land Value Taxation: A Case Study Approach*, Lincoln Institute WP01WM1 (2001), pp. 47–48 — [PDF](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf)
- [S citing primary] The **Land Valuation Act 1956** replaced this. Hansard (Session 1956–57 No. 3) records two aims. The first was a base that "did not tax a person in the efforts he put into the land", taxed "values created by the community at large" and "discouraged the withholding of land from use". The second was to replace a system that relied on "voluntary declaration of value by the owner", lacked inspections and "would lead to competitive under assessment", with "failure to undertake a general revaluation". — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), p. 48
- [S] The Act requires the Commissioner of Valuation to value "the unimproved and improved value of every parcel of land in each district" (parishes as districts). The Commissioner sets the valuation date and the Minister brings a roll into effect. Appeals go to a Valuation Board (now the Revenue Court) and then the Court of Appeal. Although two bases are determined, "the rolls were published with only one set of values i.e. unimproved values." — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), p. 48
- [S] Definition in practice: unimproved value is the capital sum the fee simple might realize if the statutory improvements did not exist; original clearing is ignored. For improved land, the assessed value is the greater of a comparable vacant site's value or improved value minus improvements. Highest and best use is applied. — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), p. 51

**Who values, and the legal cycle**
- [S, official] "new valuations should be conducted every five years by the National Land Agency", using NLA and private-sector valuation surveyors. Value is based on unimproved land value, "disregarding the value of any improvements, such as building and crops". About 750,000 properties are on the National Valuation Roll (page dated © 2016). — [Ministry of Local Government, "Land Valuation"](https://localauthorities.gov.jm/land-valuation)
- [S, official] The Commissioner of Land Valuations (NLA Land Valuation Division) assesses value. Objections must be lodged within 60 days of the Notice of Valuation, on grounds set out in **Section 34 of the Land Valuation Act**. The objector must pay tax on "75% of the valuation as assessed by the Commissioner of Land Valuations" (or the declared value, if greater) while the objection is pending. "Objections may not be made on grounds such as 'the tax is too high'." — [NLA, "Land Valuation"](https://www.nla.gov.jm/content/land-valuation)

**Revaluation history: law versus practice**
- [S] "The legislation provides for 5-yearly cycles however revaluations have taken place in 1974, 1983 and 1993. The next one is planned for 2000/01." Revaluations were manual with no mass appraisal. "Tax rates are set in the revaluation year and remain fixed until the next revaluation, this results in buoyancy problems as the revenues are eroded by inflationary pressures." About 630,000 parcels. — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), pp. 50–51
- [U] **Date conflict:** the Ministry of Local Government says the 2002 valuation was "the first valuation to be undertaken in Jamaica in ten years", the previous one being **1992**; Lincoln 2001 says **1993**. Possibly valuation date versus roll effective date; unresolved. — [localauthorities.gov.jm](https://localauthorities.gov.jm/land-valuation); [Lincoln 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf)
- [U] Phase-in of the 1956 system (parish by parish, culminating in an island-wide roll by 1974) is widely stated but **not verified** this session.

**The 2013 valuation and its delayed implementation (primary source)**
- [P] Finance Minister Audley Shaw, statement to Parliament, **11 April 2017**: "there had been a long delay in undertaking the land valuation—the last one was in 2002, and such valuations are to be done every five years. We are 15 years overdue." — [Ministry of Finance PDF](https://mof.gov.jm/wp-content/uploads/speech-to-parliament-by-hmfps-on-property-taxes-april-11-2017.pdf), p. 1–2
- [P] "In 2013 when the valuations were done, the then Government opted not to implement them… Instead of implementing the new valuations, they increased property tax rates on the old 2002 values." Rates rose from 0.75% of 2002 values over $300,000 to 1.5% for values of $100,000–$1 million and 2.0% above $1 million. The previous government had also "Gazetted the new property tax rates without securing Parliamentary approval", hence the **Property Tax (Validation and Indemnity) Act 2017** "to provide validation for the collections of property taxes since 2013". — [MOF 2017](https://mof.gov.jm/wp-content/uploads/speech-to-parliament-by-hmfps-on-property-taxes-april-11-2017.pdf), pp. 4–5
- [P] Scale of value change: unimproved land in 2013 was worth "approximately $1.9 trillion dollars (or 130 percent of our total national income, or GDP), which compared with $567 billion in 2002. This is an increase in land values averaging 235 percent." — [MOF 2017](https://mof.gov.jm/wp-content/uploads/speech-to-parliament-by-hmfps-on-property-taxes-april-11-2017.pdf), p. 5
- [P] Because "the Land Valuation Act is also very clear that we cannot change the valuation results", the government cushioned the new values by cutting rates, first to 0.8%–1.3%, then (April 2017) to a flat $1,000 on values up to $400,000 plus eight bands "ranging from a low of 0.5% to a high of 0.9%". As a result, 448,360 (58%) of 776,487 properties saw a reduction or no change. Revenue yield was expected at J$6.5 billion, given "low rates of compliance of around 57 percent", against J$8.6 billion under the earlier-announced rates. Arrears were about J$13.5 billion. — [MOF 2017](https://mof.gov.jm/wp-content/uploads/speech-to-parliament-by-hmfps-on-property-taxes-april-11-2017.pdf), pp. 2, 7–10
- [P] Reform proposal: amend the Land Valuation Act "to allow for annual increases in land valuations based on an index such as the annual consumer price index in the intervening years between full land valuations." — [MOF 2017](https://mof.gov.jm/wp-content/uploads/speech-to-parliament-by-hmfps-on-property-taxes-april-11-2017.pdf), p. 6. [U] Whether this was ever enacted was not verified.

**Current schedule and administration (TAJ)**

The rate schedule "as at April 1, 2017" is: first J$400,000, flat J$1,000; next 400,000 at 0.50%; next 700,000 at 0.55%; next 1,500,000 at 0.60%; next 1,500,000 at 0.65%; next 2,500,000 at 0.70%; next 5,000,000 at 0.75%; next 18,000,000 at 0.80%; above J$30,000,000 at 0.90%. ### Inferences
- **Timeline of valuation age:** values dated 2013 were billed from FY2017/18 through at least FY2026/27, so they are about 13 years old by 2026–27. The 2002 values were billed until 2017, about 15 years. The pattern is a statutory five-year cycle with actual intervals of about ten years and an implementation lag of up to four years.
- Jamaica illustrates the politics of **revaluation shock**. Valuations cannot be changed by politicians, so governments adjust **rates** instead: raising them on stale values (2013) or cutting them to cushion new values (2017). The 2017 proposal to index values to inflation between revaluations was a response to that shock.
- In both 1956 and 2017 the stated rationale for an unimproved-value base included taxing community-created value and discouraging idle land (Hansard via Lincoln). Lincoln also records the counter-concerns: narrow, less buoyant base and higher nominal rates (see B4). The notes take no position.

## B2. Kenya: site value rating, aged valuation rolls, and the National Rating Act 2024

### Takeaway
Kenya adopted **unimproved site value rating** in Nairobi in the early 1920s, copying the 1916 Transvaal ordinance and Australian/New Zealand practice. It was consolidated in the Valuation for Rating Act (Cap. 266, 1956) and Rating Act (Cap. 267, 1963). Rolls were legally due every five years (ten from 1991) but were rarely redone. Nairobi billed on a roll dated **1982** for about four decades, raising the rate on residential site value from 2.25% (1982–87) to 34% (2014), then 25% (2015), to keep revenue up. A 2019 **draft** roll with a rate of 0.115% of current site value has been used from about 2025, with caps tied to 2022 bills. The National Rating Act 2024 is now the legal basis for rates. Its transition deadline falls around December 2026, and counties are being taken to court over whether they have valid rolls.

### Cited Findings
**Origins**
- [S] Property tax began in **1900 in Mombasa** on annual rental value, and in **1901** Nairobi adopted annual rental value. "However, because few properties had been developed, unimproved site value rating was introduced in **1928** (Gachuru and Olima 1998; Olima 2005)." Before independence in 1963, Nairobi generated 45% of its revenue from rates. — Franzsen & McCluskey (eds), *Property Tax in Africa* (Lincoln Institute, 2017), ch. 15 "Kenya", p. 232 — [PDF](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)
- [S] **Conflicting chronology:** annual rental value "was found to be inadequate" and was replaced (1908) by flat rating. In the early 1920s site rating was introduced "following the example of Australia and New Zealand", and "the early Kenyan legislation was based on the 1916 rating ordinance of the former Transvaal province of South Africa". The **Rating of Unimproved Site Value Ordinance 1921** "became operative in **1923** when the first valuation roll for Nairobi was prepared". It was repealed in 1956 by the Local Government (Valuation and Rating) Ordinance, which led to the **Valuation for Rating Act 1956 (Cap 266)** and **Rating Act 1963 (Cap 267)**. — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), pp. 16–17. [U] 1921/1923 (Lincoln 2001) versus 1928 (Lincoln 2017): unresolved.
- [S] The original reasons for unimproved site value: to encourage development of small towns, discourage speculative holding "especially by absentee landlords", and be "easy and simple to administer, especially with a limited number of trained valuers." — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), p. 17

**Legal bases under Cap. 266/267**
- [S] A rating authority could use "area rating; agricultural rental value rating; unimproved land value rating (unimproved site value rating); or unimproved land value rating plus improvement rating". "Most cities and towns opted for unimproved site value". Of the previous 175 local authorities, 102 levied rates: 75 by valuation and 27 by a combination of area and valuation rating. — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), ch. 15, p. 234 (the book cites the revised editions as "Rating Act 1986 (Chapter 267)" and "Valuation for Rating Act 1984 (Chapter 266)")
- [S quoting statute] Value of unimproved land under s. 8(2): "the sum which the freehold in possession free from encumbrances … might be expected to realize at the time of valuation if offered for sale on such reasonable terms and conditions as a bona fide seller might be expected to impose, and if the improvements, if any, thereon, therein or thereunder had not been made." — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), p. 234
- [S] Rates up to 4% of value could be set without central approval; higher rates needed central approval. — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), p. 236

**Aged rolls: law versus practice**
- [S] "Before 1991, valuation rolls were prepared every five years (Syagga 1994). Since 1991, the legislation has provided for a 10-year cycle," with annual supplementary rolls. "In practice, valuation rolls are not maintained; several of the current rolls date back to the early 1980s." "Nairobi City County is using a valuation roll last revalued in **1982**; Machakos, in **1983**; and Mombasa, in **1991**." More recent rolls: Kisumu (2008), Nyeri (2009), Kiambu (2014). "The legislative requirement that general revaluations be undertaken every 10 years has never been achieved." The book adds that this "cannot be attributed to the lack of valuers". — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), pp. 235–236
- [U, snippet] "The currently used Valuation Roll was prepared in **1980** and was implemented in **1982**. Property owners have been paying rates on unimproved site at between 25 and 34 per cent depending on location." — [The Standard (snippet; not opened)](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/business/real-estate/article/2001404551/land-rates-in-nairobi-set-to-rise-in-new-valuation-system)
- [S] "Given the static valuations, the Nairobi County Council can increase revenues only by increasing the tax rate." Nairobi residential rates as a percentage of unimproved site value per year:

**Arguments recorded in Kenyan literature (both sides)**

The caps tied to 2022 bills mean the new values are being phased in, not fully applied.

## B3. South Africa: from local choice (site, flat or composite rating) to market value of land plus buildings (MPRA 2004)

### Takeaway
Before 2004, South African municipalities rated property under four provincial ordinances and could choose **site rating** (land value only), **flat rating** (land plus buildings together) or **composite rating** (land and buildings at different rates). Site rating dominated in the old Transvaal (Gauteng and neighbouring provinces) from 1916. The Local Government: Municipal Property Rates Act 6 of 2004 (MPRA) imposed one national base, the **market value of land and buildings as a single value**. It requires general revaluations at least every four years (metros) or five years (local municipalities), with limited extensions.

### Cited Findings
**Before 2004**
- [S] A property tax "was first introduced in the Cape of Good Hope colony in **1836**". "For almost a century" property taxes were levied under four provincial ordinances, under which municipalities "generally could select one of three possible tax bases: a land value tax (called 'site rating'), a tax on land and buildings collectively (called 'flat rating'), or a tax on land and buildings separately (called 'composite rating')." — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), ch. 26 "South Africa", pp. 380–381
- [S] After Union (1910) "the principal form of municipal rating used was based on capital improved value". "In **1912** a Provincial Commission was set up in the Transvaal to investigate rating", recommending rates on site value. "In **1916** land value taxation was introduced in the old Transvaal province, apparently to stimulate property development and to counter land speculation. The Transvaal Ordinance was to act as the model or template for much of the rest of the country." Differentiation between land and buildings became possible in the Cape in **1917** and Natal in **1925**. — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), p. 11–12
- [S] By about 2000: "Of the some 500 urban municipalities about one third utilise site rating (Bell and Bowman, 1998). Site rating is predominant in Gauteng, Mpumalanga, the Northern Province and North West, flat rating in the Eastern Cape, Northern Cape and Western Cape and composite rating in the Free State. In KwaZulu-Natal both site rating and composite rating are used extensively." Rural land was then untaxed. — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), pp. 12–13
- [S] The draft national bill's definition of site value assumed current use rather than highest and best use, so "the future of site rating is not absolutely secure". — [McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), p. 15

**MPRA 2004 and rollout**
- [S] The power to rate comes from s. 229 of the 1996 Constitution. The MPRA "was promulgated on **July 2, 2005**". It became operative in a municipality only once that municipality had adopted a rates policy and prepared its first MPRA roll. The transitional period was four years (2006–2009), "later extended to six years". "Only 27 municipalities (fewer than 10 percent) had implemented their first valuation under the MPRA by July 1, 2007. The vast majority implemented the MPRA in 2008 or 2009, and only a few local municipalities implemented the MPRA in 2010 or 2011." District municipalities may not levy rates. — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), p. 381
- [S] Base: "Under the MPRA, the property tax is levied on owners of immovable property based on the 'market value,' comprising land and buildings as one composite value." It now extends to rural properties, including commercial and, in principle, subsistence farms, and to certain public infrastructure. The first ZAR 15,000 of residential market value is excluded. — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), pp. 383–384
- [S quoting statute] Market value is "the amount the property would have realized if sold on the date of valuation in the open market by a willing seller to a willing buyer." Valuers must be registered under the Property Valuers Profession Act 47 of 2000. Physical inspection is optional, and "comparative, analytical and other systems or techniques may be used, including aerial photography and computer-assisted mass appraisal (CAMA)" (s. 45(2)(b)). — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), p. 385
- [S] **Valuation cycles:** "Until the law was amended with effect from **July 1, 2015**, a valuation cycle was four years, with a possible extension of one further year." Now metros have a maximum of four years (+1 in exceptional circumstances) and local municipalities a maximum of five years (+2). "The City of Cape Town operates on a three-year cycle." — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), p. 386
- [S] Revenue: the recurrent property tax was 1.3% of GDP in 2013, 4.51% of total tax revenue (ZAR 44 bn of ZAR 975 bn), and about 15–17% of municipal operating revenue in 2008/09–2012/13. — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), pp. 381–383

**Tax profile (South Africa)**
- *Before 2004:* local choice of site value, improved value or split rates; mostly urban; valuation by municipalities with physical inspection; no external quality control. [S] [Lincoln 2017 Table 26.3](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)
- *After MPRA:* uniform market value of land plus buildings; urban and rural; mass valuation allowed; general valuations every 4 (metros) or 5 (local) years; rates and relief set annually in a local rates policy with compulsory community participation. [S] [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)

### Inferences
- South Africa is a case of **leaving site value rating by statute**: a national uniformity reform replaced the Transvaal-derived land-only base with total market value and a hard revaluation cycle. Debates about whether this was better or worse lie outside these notes. The notes record only that the reform's stated principles were "certainty, uniformity, equity, and simplicity" ([Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), p. 381).

### Gaps

- When Johannesburg and Pretoria (Tshwane) implemented their first market-value rolls, and evidence on the effects of switching from site rating, were not verified.

## B4. Other Global South cases of land / site value taxation (brief)

### Takeaway
Solidly sourced in this session: **Namibia's** national land tax on commercial farms (unimproved site value, first roll 2002/2004), and the general statement that unimproved-value systems operate in **Fiji** and the **Solomon Islands** as well as Jamaica. **Tanzania** is a counter-case: it taxes buildings only, because land belongs to the state. **Mexicali (Mexico), Papua New Guinea and Belize** could not be verified.

### Cited Findings
- [S] General: "An unimproved land value system is also encountered in developing countries, such as Fiji, Jamaica, and the Solomon Islands (Franzsen and McCluskey 2005). In Africa, Kenya and Namibia levy a tax on unimproved land, as do a number of Francophone countries, such as the Central African Republic and Côte d'Ivoire." — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), pp. 9–10
- [S] Both sides in brief: a land-only system "requires significantly less information and skill to set up and maintain" and "is less costly to maintain", and some argue it is "more progressive (Bahl 2009)". "However, because the base is narrower and less buoyant, and because significant wealth inherent in buildings is excluded, higher nominal tax rates are required, which may be politically problematic (Bahl 2009)." It also "is not consistent with the property transfer tax, which is based on total values". — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), p. 9
- [S] **Namibia:** "Namibia historically had no tax on farmland. In 2004, as part of the postindependence efforts to achieve land reform, the government introduced the commercial farm agricultural land tax under the Agricultural (Commercial) Land Reform Second Amendment Act No. 2 of 2001." The regulations in force are the Land Valuation and Taxation Regulations of 2007. "The first valuation roll, with a 2002 valuation date, was completed and implemented in 2004." The tax is "based on the unimproved site value (USV)". Rates, "not amended since its introduction in 2004", are 0.75% of USV for a single Namibian-owned farm and 1.75% for a foreign-owned farm, plus 0.25 percentage points for each additional farm. There are more than 30 value zones. A 2012 provisional roll had "legal and technical issues", but the introduction "was well planned and communicated and must be viewed as a success". — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), ch. 21, pp. 317–322
- [S] **Tanzania (counter-case):** rates are governed by the Urban Authorities (Rating) Act 1983 and Local Government Finance Act 1982 and are "based on the market value of improvements". "In practice, land value is excluded from the ratable value of property since the Land Act of 1999 provides that all land in Tanzania is the property of the state"; owners pay land rent instead. Unvalued properties pay flat rates set by bylaw, about 50% below value-based bills. — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), ch. 28, pp. 412–413
- [U, snippet] A Franzsen conference paper reportedly tabulates Nairobi and Pretoria as site-only, and Gaborone, Maseru and Windhoek as site plus improvements; in Malawi only land value is used in practice. — [PRRES paper (not opened)](https://www.prres.org/uploads/516/1272/Franzsen_Property_assess_for_rating_purposes_in_south_East_Africa.pdf)

### Inferences
- Outside Jamaica and Kenya, land-only bases in the Global South appear mainly where buildings are few or hard to value (rural farmland in Namibia) or where colonial-era rating laws copied Australian/New Zealand or Transvaal models. Where the state owns the land (Tanzania), the logic flips to taxing buildings.

### Gaps
- **Mexicali (Baja California) 1990s land-value-only property tax:** not verified; no source reached before the search budget ran out. Do not use without a source (a Lincoln Institute case study by Manuel Perló Cohen is a lead from memory, unverified).
- **Papua New Guinea, Belize** (land tax on unimproved value), and details for **Fiji** (e.g. Suva rating) were not verified.

## C2. Plain-language concepts a newcomer needs

### Takeaway
Short definitions, tied where possible to the sources above.

### Cited Findings
- **Assessment / valuation:** deciding how much each taxpayer's land is "worth" for tax purposes. Pre-modern states often assessed **expected yield** (Egypt, Mughal zabt, Ottoman tahrir) or simply **area by land class** (Rome's census form, the Ming per-*mu* silver levy). Modern rating uses **capital (sale) value**. — sources in A1–A8, B1–B3
- **Valuation roll (register, cadastre):** the official list of every taxable parcel and its value. Examples include the fish-scale registers (Ming), the tahrir defters (Ottoman), the cadastre (France) and the valuation roll (Jamaica, Kenya, South Africa). — [明史 77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777); [Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf); [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)
- **Revaluation cycle / general revaluation:** the legal interval at which all values must be redone, e.g. Jamaica 5 years, Kenya 10 years from 1991, South Africa 4–5 years. **Supplementary roll:** an interim update for new or changed parcels. — [localauthorities.gov.jm](https://localauthorities.gov.jm/land-valuation); [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)
- **Unimproved value / site value:** the value of the land as if no buildings or other improvements existed. "Site value" sometimes keeps on-site works such as levelling or drainage, while "unimproved value" may exclude even clearing and filling. **Land value taxation (site value taxation):** a property tax "in which land is the only taxable object, and the value of improvements is ignored". — [Lincoln 2017 glossary](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf), pp. 5–6
- **Rates / rating:** the Commonwealth term for local property tax. **Site rating** taxes land only; **flat rating** taxes land and buildings together; **composite or split-rate rating** taxes land and buildings separately at different rates. — [Lincoln 2017 glossary](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf); [Lincoln 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf)
- **Area rating / flat rates:** a tax set by area, use or location rather than by value. It is used where valuation capacity is lacking (rural Kenya; Tanzania's unvalued properties; Nairobi's flat-rate zones). — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf); [Kenyans.co.ke](https://www.kenyans.co.ke/news/126828-nairobi-sets-2027-land-rates-new-valuation-rules-take-effect)
- **Market value (modern):** "the amount the property would have realized if sold on the date of valuation in the open market by a willing seller to a willing buyer" (South Africa MPRA). — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)
- **Mass appraisal / CAMA:** valuing many properties at once with statistical models rather than one-by-one inspection; permitted under the MPRA. — [Lincoln 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)
- **Self-assessment / declaration:** the owner states the value or attributes, and officials check. Examples are Rome ("the declarant shall value everything himself") and Jamaica's pre-1956 "ingivings". — [D.50.15.4](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/justinian/digest50.shtml); [Lincoln 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf)
- **Fixed quota / fixed demand:** the state fixes the total or per-estate sum and does not update it, e.g. the Permanent Settlement's demand "for all time to come" and the Qing "fixed-target land tax". — [Banglapedia](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar); [Ma & Rubin](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/75218/1/WP261.pdf)
- **Tax farming (*ijara*):** selling the right to collect revenue to the highest bidder, as in Bengal under Hastings. — [Banglapedia](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)
- **Coverage, assessment, tax and collection ratios:** respectively, the share of parcels on the roll, how current or accurate values are, how rates are set, and the share of billed tax actually collected. This is the diagnostic framework used for Jamaica and Kenya. — [Lincoln 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf), p. 51
- **Units:** *aroura* ≈ 0.27 ha (Egypt) — [Katary](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp). *iugerum* (Roman area unit) — [D.50.15.4](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/justinian/digest50.shtml). *qing* = 100 *mu* (China) — [新唐書 51](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%96%B0%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7051). *bigha* = 60 × 60 gaz, measured with the *jarib* (Mughal) — [Ain X](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf). *hide* (English geld unit) — [ASC](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/657).

### Inferences
- A unifying idea for teaching: every system had to answer **what to measure** (people, area, yield, rent, price), **who measures** (owner, local notables, state officials, professional valuers) and **how often**. Most of the trouble in these sources arises when the answer to the third question becomes "rarely" or "never".

## C3. Scenes and anecdotes (well-sourced)

### Takeaway
Short scenes that bring the valuation story to life, each tied to a source.

### Cited Findings
1. **The dew-soaked rope (Mughal India).** Hemp measuring ropes "would be left in the dew and thus fraudfully moistened". Damp, shrunken rope in the morning hurt peasants; dry, lengthened rope by evening hurt the treasury. Akbar replaced it with "bamboos joined by iron rings". The hemp-based bigha differed by about "10 bigha in a hundred". — [Ain-i-Akbari II, Ains IX–X](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)
2. **Shrinking the bow (Ming China).** Under pressure to show surplus acreage in Zhang Juzheng's remeasurement, "officials vied to shrink their measuring bows to find more land, or squeezed existing fields to fill fictitious quotas" (my translation). — [明史 77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777)
3. **"Not even an ox, nor a cow, nor a swine" (England 1085).** The Chronicler's indignation at the thoroughness of William's commissioners. — [ASC](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/657)
4. **Measuring "each spot of ground" (late Rome).** Lactantius's account of surveyors counting vines and trees, with torture to extract declarations. A hostile source. — [Lactantius ch. 23](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0705.htm)
5. **"As inevitable and as certain as the sun-set" (Bengal 1793).** Estates auctioned for a missed monthly instalment; nearly half of zamindari property changed hands within seven years. — [Banglapedia RSL](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Revenue_Sale_Law,_1793)
6. **Geometry born of the flood (Herodotus).** The king's men remeasured plots the Nile had eaten so the farmer would "pay less, in proportion to the rent appointed". — [Herodotus 2.109](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2707)
7. **The well that forecast taxes (Strabo).** "The greater rises indicate that the revenues also will be greater." — [Strabo 17.1.48](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/17A3*.html)
8. **Yang Yan's lament (Tang, 779/780).** The names, acreages and wealth grades on the registers were "no longer the old ones". — [舊唐書 118](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%88%8A%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7118)
9. **"We are 15 years overdue" (Jamaica, 11 April 2017).** A finance minister tells Parliament that the previous government shelved the 2013 values, raised rates on 2002 values instead, and gazetted the rates without parliamentary approval, requiring a Validation and Indemnity Act. — [MOF 2017](https://mof.gov.jm/wp-content/uploads/speech-to-parliament-by-hmfps-on-property-taxes-april-11-2017.pdf)
10. **Nairobi's 34% rate (2014).** On a roll frozen since 1982, the city's annual rate climbed to 34% of site value before a new draft roll reset it to 0.115%. — [Lincoln 2017 Table 15.3](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf); [Kenyans.co.ke](https://www.kenyans.co.ke/news/126828-nairobi-sets-2027-land-rates-new-valuation-rules-take-effect)
11. **A philosopher jailed over taxes (France, December 1760).** Mirabeau, architect of the single tax, was "hauled off to jail at Vincennes" and confined to his estates. — [HET](https://www.hetwebsite.net/het/profiles/mirabeau.htm)
