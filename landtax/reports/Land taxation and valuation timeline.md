# Land taxes rise, freeze and return

Land has been taxed for as long as there have been states, and on 4 November 2026 it is still taxed almost everywhere: mostly through annual property taxes on land and buildings together, more rarely through taxes on the value of land alone, and also through public ownership and leasing, through charges on rises in land value, and through wealth taxes that borrow property valuations. The thread that runs through five thousand years is **valuation**: the official decision about what each piece of land is "worth" for tax purposes. Every land tax has had to answer three questions. What is measured: area, expected harvest, rent, or sale price, and land alone or land with its buildings? Who measures it: scribes, owners, local committees, state valuers, or now statistical models? And how often is the measurement renewed, in law and in practice? The answer to the third question explains more of this history than any other. Again and again a valuation is made, it ages, renewing it turns out to be politically costly, and the tax drifts away from the values it was meant to follow until a revaluation, a court ruling, a fiscal crisis or abolition resets it.

The earliest states measured what they could see. Egyptian scribes forecast harvests from field areas and the height of the Nile flood; Roman owners declared and valued their own vines and olive trees; Tang and Ming officials kept household and parcel registers; William I's commissioners recorded what each English holding was worth in 1066 and 1086; Akbar's officers measured fields with bamboo rods and set cash rates from ten-year averages. The registers aged quickly. A Tang minister complained around 780 that the names, acreages and wealth grades on the books no longer matched reality. England's land tax of 1692 hardened within six years into fixed county quotas, so a 1690s assessment remained the legal basis of a charge until 1963. Bengal's Permanent Settlement of 1793 fixed the revenue demand "for all time to come".

In the nineteenth century France, Prussia, Denmark and Meiji Japan mapped every parcel and gave it an official yield or value, then mostly left those values fixed. Meanwhile economists from the French Physiocrats to Henry George (1879) argued that land rent is created by the community rather than the owner and could be taxed without discouraging work or building. Between the 1880s and the 1920s New Zealand, the Australian colonies and Commonwealth, Denmark, German cities, Britain, western Canada, Pennsylvania, South Africa, Kenya and a German colony in China tried to tax land value separately from buildings, or to tax increases in land value. That required a new and harder kind of valuation, of land "as if unbuilt", and courts, war and the 1913 land bust undid several attempts.

Between the wars and after 1945 most national land value taxes retreated. Britain switched to taxing "development value" created by planning permission, three times, and each version was repealed by the next government. Japan rebuilt its system on official land prices, Taiwan wrote Sun Yat-sen's "equalisation of land rights" into its constitution, and public landowners from Canberra to Singapore collected land value as rents or lease premiums. From the 1970s many valuations froze: West Germany on 1964 values, France on 1970 rents, Belgium on 1975 rents, England and Scotland on 1991 prices, California on 1975 values plus at most 2% a year. Canberra abolished its land rent, New Zealand its national land tax, and several European wealth taxes ended amid disputes over how real estate was valued.

Since about 2009 courts, auditors and algorithms have forced reckonings. Germany's Constitutional Court struck down the 1964 values and Germany revalued about 36 million properties for 2025; Denmark's valuation system collapsed and was rebuilt as a statistical model; Estonia revalued its land after 21 years; Jamaica and Kenya struggled with rolls a decade or four decades old; England prepared to value its most expensive homes by algorithm for a new surcharge. Australia's capital territory and two of its states tried swapping stamp duty for land-based taxes (New South Wales for only six months), Korea rolled back its plan to bring official values up to 90% of market prices, and China's revenue from selling land-use rights roughly halved. The report takes no position on whether land value taxation is desirable; where effects are disputed it sets out the strongest evidence on each side.

## Before 1500: states taxed what they could measure, which was harvests, fields and hides

The oldest assessments did not ask what land would sell for. They asked what it would yield. In Egypt, records of the height of each year's Nile inundation "were used to determine tax due on arable fields, establish the probability of meeting taxation goals, and remedy any shortfalls", according to Sally Katary's survey of the evidence ([Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)) [secondary]. The Palermo Stone records biennial royal counts of herds, fields, gold and people, and by the New Kingdom levies were described as "of every year" (same) [secondary]. The Wilbour Papyrus, from year 4 of Ramesses V in the twelfth century BC, gives assessment data for about **2,800 plots** in Middle Egypt: smallholders paid a fixed **1½ sacks per aroura** (an aroura is about 0.27 hectares) on a small portion of their plots, while large tracts worked by labourers owed **30% of the harvest** (same) [secondary]. Strabo, writing under Augustus, explained that the nilometer at Elephantine served the prefects "with regard to the revenues; for the greater rises indicate that the revenues also will be greater" ([Strabo 17.1.48](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/17A3*.html)). Herodotus passed on an Egyptian tradition that a king's men remeasured any plot the river had carried away "in order that for the future the man might pay less", and guessed that geometry was born this way ([Herodotus 2.109](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2707)); it is a legend, not documented policy. Egypt's "valuation" was therefore area multiplied by a standard yield and adjusted for the flood, renewed every year or two. Because the river changed every year, a frozen valuation in the modern sense hardly arose. Katary warns that the documents are "one of a kind" and that how far central authority reached into the countryside "is unknown and probably unknowable" ([Katary 2011](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9p13z2vp)) [secondary].

Rome added **self-assessment**, in which the owner states the facts and the value and officials check them. Ulpian's early-third-century census form, preserved in Justinian's *Digest*, required each owner to declare a farm's name, district and two nearest neighbours, its sown arable, the number of vines, the olive groves and trees, meadow, pasture and woodland, and ended *omnia ipse qui defert aestimet*: the person declaring values everything himself ([Digest 50.15.4](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/justinian/digest50.shtml)). An assessor (*censitor*) was to relieve an owner whose land had been "swallowed up by an earthquake" or whose vines had died, but an owner who cut down his own trees still had to declare the number standing at the census ([Scott translation, D.50.15.4](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/D50_Scott.htm)). Relief followed physical change in the land, not changes in prices or rents. A hostile Christian writer, Lactantius, described a census under the Tetrarchy in which "each spot of ground was measured, vines and fruit-trees numbered", with family members tortured to extract declarations ([Lactantius, ch. 23](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0705.htm)).

Chinese dynasties moved from taxing people to taxing land, and their histories state the problem of ageing registers more clearly than any other source. Under the Tang "equal-field" system each adult man was allotted 100 *mu* of land and paid flat dues per adult, not per unit of land value: 2 *hu* of millet, twenty days' labour a year and cloth ([Xin Tang shu 51](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%96%B0%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7051)). From 728 households were to be regraded into nine classes every three years (same). After the great rebellions that began in the Tianbao era (742–756) the registers collapsed, and the minister Yang Yan wrote a sentence that could stand over every later chapter of this report: the registered adults "have moved or died, so the names are no longer the old ones; fields have changed hands, so the acreages are no longer the old figures; the rich and poor have risen and fallen, so the grades are no longer the old ones" ([Jiu Tang shu 118](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%88%8A%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7118); translation from the notes). His **Two-Tax method**, conventionally dated 780 [unresolved for the exact year], registered households where they actually lived, graded them by wealth, pegged the land tax to the cultivated acreage of 779, collected in summer and autumn, and set the total by first estimating expenditure: "measure outgoings to set incomings" (same). The early Ming rebuilt the paper base, with Yellow Registers of households from 1381 and, from 1387, "fish-scale" registers in which four tax captains in each district measured every field's shape and dimensions and recorded its owner ([Ming shi 77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777)). The Ming history's own totals show how political a registered area could be: **8,507,623 *qing* in 1393** and "only" **4,228,058 *qing* in 1502** (same; a *qing* is 100 *mu*).

Norman England combined a conventional tax unit with a new survey of current values. In the winter of 1083–84 William I levied a **geld**, a land tax, of "two and seventy pence" on each **hide**, a fiscal unit of land; at Christmas 1085 in Gloucester he sent commissioners into every shire to find "how many hundreds of hides were in the shire" and what each landholder had "either in land or in stock, and how much money it were worth" ([Anglo-Saxon Chronicle](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/657)). The chronicler complained that "not even an ox, nor a cow, nor a swine was there left, that was not set down in his writ" (same). Domesday Book (1086) recorded values for 1066 and for 1086 alongside hides and plough teams; the National Archives calls it "a survey and valuation of landed property", not a census ([TNA](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/domesday-book/)) [secondary]. Domesday thus set an inherited assessment, the hide, beside current annual values, which is the gap every later revaluation debate is about. Whether hidage had drifted far from real values, and when the geld lapsed, could not be checked [unresolved].

The Ottoman state began its own survey tradition in this period. Its **tahrir** registers recorded, village by village, the adult males, the land in use and the expected tax revenue from every resource; more than 1,500 survive, the oldest in the Turkish archives being Albania's of **1431–32**, and the economist Metin Coşgel describes them as "similar to the English Domesday book but wider in spatial and temporal coverage" ([Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf)) [secondary].

## 1500–1800: registers hardened into quotas and fixed demands

Sixteenth-century China shows the two remedies for an aged register: measure again, or simplify the tax so that the register matters less. Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng persuaded the Wanli emperor that "all fields in the realm be measured within three years" ([Ming shi 77](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B777)). The result was **7,013,976 *qing***, by the history's reckoning three million more than in the Hongzhi era, but it adds that Zhang "treated surpluses over quota as merit; officials vied to shrink their measuring bows to find more land, or squeezed existing fields to fill fictitious quotas" (same; notes' translation). The **Single Whip** reform, applied everywhere from 1581, folded the land taxes and labour services of each county into one item, "all levied in silver reckoned per *mu* of land" ([Ming shi 78](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B778)). It moved China toward an area-based money tax, but recorded acreage and quotas stayed the base: when fields were sold to the rich, "the property was gone but the tax-grain obligation stayed" with the seller (same). Under the Qing the land tax supplied about **70% of all taxation**, yet official spending averaged about 36 million silver taels with little variation from 1662 to 1849 while the population nearly tripled, "reflecting the combined effect of a fixed revenue target accompanied by explosive population expansion" ([Ma & Rubin 2017](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/75218/1/WP261.pdf)) [secondary]. Officials filled the gap with surcharges (same) [secondary].

Akbar's **zabt** system in Mughal India is unusually well documented, because Abu'l Fazl's *Ain-i-Akbari* describes it from the inside. The old hemp measuring rope "would be left in the dew and thus fraudfully moistened": damp and shrunken in the morning, dry and stretched by evening, so that either "the husbandmen suffered loss" or "the royal revenues were diminished". In the nineteenth regnal year (about 1574–75, read from damaged print [unresolved]) it was replaced by "bamboos joined by iron rings", a standard rod that "is subject to no variation" ([Ain-i-Akbari, vol. II](https://ia801404.us.archive.org/1/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.502490/2015.502490.ain-i-akbari-of_text.pdf)). Land was classed by how continuously it was cultivated, from *polaj* (every season) to *banjar* (idle five years or more); the yields of good, middling and bad land were averaged, the state took one-third of that average, and the claim was converted into cash (same). Officials had previously reported current prices every year, which "was attended with considerable inconvenience"; after a fresh settlement by Muzaffar Khan and Raja Todar Mal in the sixteenth regnal year, the rates of collection for regnal years 15 to 24 were aggregated and "a tenth of the total was fixed as the annual assessment" (same). This ten-year average damped year-to-year swings without freezing values for ever, a middle path between Egypt's annual reassessment and later permanent fixes. Bengal never received measurement: its **zamindars**, the hereditary landholders who collected revenue, paid a summary assessment decided "neither by a proper measurement of land nor by an assessment of the produce thereof" ([Banglapedia, "Zamindar"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)) [secondary].

The Ottomans used a similar smoothing device and then let it lapse. Surveyors were instructed to record "not the amounts of taxes collected during the year of the survey but the expected amounts based on the averages of the last three years", priced at rates set in each province's law code; but new general surveys of lands already held "became uncommon after the sixteenth century" ([Coşgel](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2002-47r.pdf)) [secondary].

Denmark built a national cadastre graded by soil quality. A **cadastre** (Danish *matrikel*) is the official register, and later map, of every land parcel. From 1662 each farm's dues were converted into **hartkorn** ("hard grain"), a notional measure of grain-producing capacity rather than a price. After a survey of all cultivated land in 1681–83 "on the Swedish model", Christian V's Great Matrikel of 1688 graded arable as good, middling or poor and converted area into *tønder hartkorn* ([Lex: jordbonitering](https://lex.dk/jordbonitering); [Lex: matrikel](https://lex.dk/matrikel_-_register_og_kortv%C3%A6rk_over_ejendomme)) [secondary]. The grading was done by selected farmers "who were not allowed to value land in their own parishes", an early rule against local capture ([Lex: jordbonitering](https://lex.dk/jordbonitering)) [secondary]. A cadastre tax of 1 rigsdaler 84 skilling per *tønde hartkorn* per quarter ran from 1692 to 1813 ([Lex: hartkorn](https://lex.dk/hartkorn)) [secondary]. By the early 1800s a new national grading had become "pressing … because the 1688 matrikel had not been maintained" ([Lex: jordbonitering](https://lex.dk/jordbonitering)) [secondary].

England's land tax shows how a valuation can stop mattering. The Act of 1692 charged four shillings in the pound, a nominal 20%, on the assessed annual values of land, personal estate and public offices, with local commissioners named for every county and borough ([London Metropolitan Archives via AIM25](https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/land-tax-commissioners-6)). It was levied as a rate on value only from 1693 to 1696; "from 1698 to 1798 each county or borough was given a fixed sum to collect" (same). This made it a **quota tax** (or apportioned tax), in which each area's total is fixed and then shared among owners, so revaluing land changes nothing for the Treasury. The annual rentals in the assessment books stayed frozen at 1690s levels or were left out entirely ([Bedfordshire Archives](https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/Using_Our_Collections/Collection-Guides/Maps-and-Land-Records/LandTaxRecords.aspx)). In 1798 William Pitt made that year's charge "a perpetual charge, subject to redemption or purchase", meaning owners could extinguish it with a lump sum, which by 1950 stood at **twenty-five times the annual charge** ([TNA, C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030)). Redemption became compulsory in certain circumstances, such as a change of owner, from 1950, and "the Finance Act 1963 abolished all unredeemed land tax with effect from 25 March of that year" (same). A 1690s assessment had underpinned a legal charge for about 270 years.

The same century produced an influential argument for taxing land rent alone. The French **Physiocrats** held that land is the only source of net wealth, so "the burden of all taxes ultimately bears down on the landowner" and a single direct tax on land rents was the efficient choice ([HET, "The Physiocrats"](https://www.hetwebsite.net/het/schools/physioc.htm)) [secondary]. The marquis de Mirabeau, chief architect of this "single tax", published *Théorie de l'impôt* in 1760 and was "hauled off to jail at Vincennes in December 1760" ([HET, "Mirabeau"](https://www.hetwebsite.net/het/profiles/mirabeau.htm)) [secondary]. One claim that Turgot actually instituted a single tax in 1774–76 runs against the usual account that it was never applied and could not be corroborated [unresolved].

The century ended with an assessment fixed for ever. In March 1793 Lord Cornwallis concluded the **Permanent Settlement** of Bengal: zamindars became proprietors, and their revenue demand would not change "for all time to come" ([Banglapedia, "Zamindar"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Zamindar)) [secondary]. Arrears were recovered by auctioning the defaulter's estate at the end of any month in which an instalment was missed, sales "as inevitable and as certain as the sun-set", and no remission was allowed "on account of any natural calamity"; within seven years "nearly half of the landed property of zamindars changed hands" ([Banglapedia, "Revenue Sale Law, 1793"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Revenue_Sale_Law,_1793)) [secondary]. As rents and population grew while the state's demand stayed fixed, zamindari income rose and default sales became rare by the late nineteenth century (same) [secondary]. Within about a decade the British concluded that the settlement was not yielding the expected results and abandoned the permanent model elsewhere in favour of settlements revised periodically, on terms of five to a hundred years, and in the Sundarbans from 1879 with reassessment every thirty years ([Banglapedia, "Temporary Settlement"](https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Temporary_Settlement)) [secondary]. Banerjee and Iyer's study of the long-run legacy links districts where the British gave land rights to landlords rather than cultivators to lower agricultural investment and productivity after independence ([AER 2005](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0002828054825574)) [secondary]; the notes flag a published re-examination they could not read, so the finding is influential but contested [unresolved].

## 1800–1880: cadastres mapped every parcel, and economists began calling land rent unearned

France's finance law of 15 September 1807 launched a parcel-by-parcel cadastre in which every plot was mapped, classed and given a taxable revenue for the land tax. It took until about 1850 to finish in mainland France, and its "great defect", in the words of one departmental archive, "was its absence of updating, according to the principle of the immutability of the Plan": from 1850 cantons whose plans were more than thirty years old could revise them at their own cost, and a general renovation came only with a law of 16 April 1930 ([Archives de la Creuse](https://archives.creuse.fr/fichier/256725/23620)) [secondary]. France shows the cost of a one-off cadastre: by the time the last communes were mapped, the first entries were decades old.

The German states followed, Bavaria (1811), Württemberg (1821) and Baden (1854) assessing land by type of cultivation and soil quality ([Bundestag research service, WD 4-3000-026/18](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/547158/6ff041fce870e40344bd7424a2863154/WD-4-026-18-pdf.pdf)) [secondary]. On 21 May 1861 Prussia adopted a land tax for the whole state, assessed on each parcel's estimated cadastral **net yield** (*Reinertrag*: the normal annual income from the land after costs), with buildings taxed separately on their use value (same; [1882 cadastral handbook, TIB](https://goobi.tib.eu/viewer/fullscreen/886575389/379/LOG_0076/)). The figures commonly quoted for its total yield and rate could not be checked [unresolved]. In 1891–93 Prussia handed the land tax essentially to the municipalities ([WD 4-3000-026/18](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/547158/6ff041fce870e40344bd7424a2863154/WD-4-026-18-pdf.pdf)) [secondary], and a law of 1893 let them levy special land taxes on *gemeiner Wert*, market value, which became the legal hook for later municipal taxes on rising land values ([Diefenbacher 2022](https://doi.org/10.3790/vjh.91.1.69)) [secondary].

Denmark regraded all its soils between 1806 and 1826 on a scale on which the best land scored 24. The national benchmark was a freeholder's plot at Karlslunde, sand, clay and humus "to a depth of over 18 inches … sloping well to the south", against which every other field in the country was rated ([Lex: jordbonitering](https://lex.dk/jordbonitering)) [secondary]. The new matrikel took effect in 1844 and stayed the tax base until 1903 (same) [secondary].

Japan rebuilt a whole country's land tax on official values. The Meiji **Land Tax Reform Act**, promulgated on 28 July 1873, replaced rice tribute assessed on whole villages with a cash tax of **3% of an assessed land value**, paid by the individual owner ([NTA Tax Museum](https://www.nta.go.jp/about/organization/ntc/sozei/tokubetsu/h14shiryoukan/02a.htm)). Owners surveyed their own parcels, village representatives compiled registers and maps, and two-person prefectural teams checked each plot while the owner read out the marker stake ([NTA](https://www.nta.go.jp/about/organization/ntc/sozei/network/184.htm)). Because a market price for all land was judged impossible to obtain quickly, the law used a "statutory land value" built by **capitalisation**: one year's estimated net yield divided by an interest rate to give a capital sum. Seed and fertiliser costs were fixed at 15% of the harvest and the interest rate capped at 7% for owner-farmed land, "measures that raised land values" ([Kotobank](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%9C%B0%E7%A7%9F%E6%94%B9%E6%AD%A3)) [secondary]. In the official worked example, land and village taxes together took 34% of the crop (same) [secondary]. Resistance was fierce: after the Ise riot of December 1876, in which more than 50,000 people were punished ([Kotobank](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E4%BC%8A%E5%8B%A2%E6%9A%B4%E5%8B%95-432657)) [secondary], the rate was cut to 2.5% in January 1877 ([NTA, tax history vol. 1](https://www.nta.go.jp/about/organization/ntc/sozei/01chiso/kaidai.htm)). The land tax raised **¥43.3 million in FY1881, 70.2% of government revenue** (same). Values were supposed to be revised every five years, but the 1884 Land Tax Ordinance deleted that chapter so that "legally, the results of the reform were fixed" (same). Later "special revisions" only ever lowered values, and by 1898 the government itself said sale prices had reached "two to ten times or more" the ledger values (same). One Miyagi pear orchard, valued under the early rules on its actual pear crop rather than as barley land, was rated at eight times its neighbours; a special revision was refused in 1884 and granted only in 1887 (same).

In the British settler colonies local taxation began as English-style **rates**: a local property tax on **annual value**, the yearly rent a property could fetch. New Zealand's ordinances of the 1840s were "in effect an adoption of the 'English system'", and its Rating Act 1876 allowed annual value only ([McCluskey, Grimes & Timmins 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf)) [secondary]; the Cape Colony introduced a property tax in 1836 ([Franzsen & McCluskey 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)) [secondary].

The idea of taxing land value *as such* was gathering force. McCluskey and colleagues link New Zealand's later turn to land value to depression-era support for J. S. Mill's view that the "unearned increment" (a rise in land value the owner did nothing to create) should be shared, and for Henry George ([McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf)) [secondary]. In 1877 the German economist Adolph Wagner urged the Verein für Socialpolitik to tax "accidental" gains in urban land values, without winning its majority ([Diefenbacher 2022](https://doi.org/10.3790/vjh.91.1.69)) [secondary]. In 1879 Henry George published *Progress and Poverty* in San Francisco, giving reformers a programme, to take land rent through taxation and exempt buildings, and a slogan, the "single tax"; within fifteen years a single-tax club from Iowa had founded a colony on his theories at Fairhope, Alabama ([Fairhope Single Tax Corporation](https://www.fairhopesingletax.com/fairhope-history)) [secondary].

The first land taxes in Australasia came just before George's book and valued land by what it could carry, not what it would fetch. Victoria's **Land Tax Act 1877** fell on "landed estates", reportedly those over 640 acres and £2,500, to break up large holdings; a Commissioner of Land Tax classed land by sheep-carrying capacity, first-class land "capable of carrying two or more sheep to the acre" being "assessed at £4 per acre" ([Papers Past extracts](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/OW18790201.2.7.4)) [snippet]. New Zealand's **Land Tax Act 1878** levied a halfpenny rate after a penny was rejected and came into force on 1 January 1879 ([Waikato Times extract](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/WT18780829.2.13); [Manawatu Herald extract](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/MH18790107.2.7)) [snippet]. It prompted the colony's first valuation rolls but was replaced in December 1879 by a general property tax on capital value, land and improvements together ([McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf)) [secondary]. In 1882 New Zealand councils were allowed to rate on capital value instead of annual value, partly because "undeveloped land and land held for speculative purposes had no rental value but a definite market or capital value" (same) [secondary].

## 1880–1925: reformers separated land from buildings, then courts, war and a land bust tested them

The decades either side of 1900 gave "land value" its modern technical meaning: the price the bare site would fetch if the buildings and the owner's other improvements did not exist. Different laws call it **unimproved value**, **site value** or **land value**, with small differences over whether works such as clearing, draining or levelling count as part of the land. All of them are counterfactuals: in a built-up street the valuer must estimate the price of something nobody is selling. The period's reforms took four forms. Some taxed land value every year at a separate or higher rate than buildings. Some exempted buildings from local rates. Some taxed the **increment**, the rise in a plot's value between two dates, usually when it was sold. And some kept the land in public hands and leased it.

### New Zealand and Australia built national land-value machinery

In 1891 New Zealand's Liberal government under Ballance replaced the property tax with a land tax, a graduated tax on large estates and an income tax; improvements were partly exempted in 1891 and fully the next year or in 1893, the sources conflicting ([McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf)) [secondary; unresolved]. A 1924 parliamentary paper traces the Act to South Australia's Land and Income Tax Act of 1884 ([AJHR 1924](https://dynamic-rsnz.natlib.govt.nz/imageserver/parliamentary/P29pZD1BSkhSMTkyNC1JLjEuMTEzNCZnZXRwZGY9dHJ1ZQ==)) [snippet], commonly called the first Australian tax on unimproved value [unresolved]. Absentee owners paid 50% more on the graduated scale from 1903 ([1903 amendment](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1903/37/en/latest/)) [snippet]. Two laws of 1896 reshaped valuation. The Rating on Unimproved Value Act let councils rate land value alone, but only after a poll of ratepayers, and only another poll could abandon it; the Government Valuation of Land Act defined unimproved and improved value, the improved value being "in essence the self-created value, while the unimproved value was that value created by the community" ([McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf)) [secondary]. A Valuation Department created on 17 October 1896 then produced a single government roll for both the national land tax and local rates ([Wellington City Archives](https://archivesonline.wcc.govt.nz/nodes/view/7993); [Press, 1932](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19320521.2.109)) [snippet].

New South Wales (1895) and Western Australia (1907) adopted land taxes that the Commonwealth later cited as models in the margins of its own Act ([Land Tax Assessment Act 1910](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf)). The Commonwealth's **Land Tax Act 1910**, assented on 16 November 1910, was "An Act to impose a Progressive Land Tax upon Unimproved Values" ([Land Tax Act 1910](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00021/asmade/1910-11-16/text/original/word)). Resident owners deducted **£5,000** and paid marginal rates rising smoothly from 1d to **6d in the pound** (about 0.42% to 2.5%); absentees, defined as people not resident in Australia, had no deduction and paid up to 7d (same; [LTAA s 11](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf)). Its definition of unimproved value became a template across the region: "the capital sum which the fee simple of the land might be expected to realize if offered for sale on such reasonable terms and conditions as a bona fide seller would require, assuming that the improvements (if any) thereon … had not been made" ([LTAA s 3](https://legislation.gov.au/C1910A00022/asmade/1910-11-17/text/original/pdf)). Owners valued their own land in annual returns; the Commissioner could make his own valuations or borrow the states'; appeals went to the courts; and section 48 let the Commonwealth buy any land whose value an owner had understated by 25% or more, paying only the owner's own figure plus improvements and 10% "by way of an allowance for compulsory dispossession", a clause whose margin credits New Zealand (same). Treasury describes the tax as "a form of wealth tax and … a means to break up large tracts of under-utilised land" ([Treasury 2006](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/economic-roundup-winter-2006/a-brief-history-of-australias-tax-system)) [secondary].

Australia also chose public ownership for its new capital. In January 1901 Edmund Barton promised that land in the federal territory "will not be sold … The land will be let for considerable terms but with periodical re-appraisement so that the revenues thus obtained will assist the cost of creating the Commonwealth Capital" ([1988 House of Representatives report](https://aphref.aph.gov.au/house/committee/reports/1988/1988_pp409.pdf)). When the first Canberra leases were auctioned in 1924, bidders named a capital value and paid **5% of it as the first year's land rent** (same).

### Germany turned the debate toward increments, and tried them in China

German land reformers moved from George's single tax toward taxing increments. The Bund für Bodenbesitzreform was founded in 1888 with George listed as a member; under Adolf Damaschke, chair of the renamed Bund deutscher Bodenreformer from 1898 until 1935, it dropped the single tax for "more realistic measures": increment taxes, municipal land purchase and **ground leases** (*Erbbaurecht*, long leases of publicly or privately owned land) ([Diefenbacher 2022](https://doi.org/10.3790/vjh.91.1.69)) [secondary]. About **470 German municipalities** levied increment taxes before 1911. They were charged when land changed hands, on the sale price minus the earlier purchase price, at progressive rates, often only if the land had been held under five years (built) or ten (vacant) ([Bundestag research service, WD 4-3000-133/20](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/817898/af9a120db39ad0dfc3274152b3d0f2e9/WD-4-133-20-pdf-data.pdf)) [secondary]. Because these taxes used recorded prices, they needed no periodic valuation of land at all, which made them easy to adopt locally but tied their yield to the turnover of the property market. The Reich nationalised the tax on 14 February 1911, sharing the yield 50% Reich, 10% states and 40% municipalities, then withdrew in July 1913 in favour of a tax on growth in total wealth (same) [secondary]. A recent study concludes that it "neither fulfilled the land reform goal of combating speculation nor generated enough revenue" ([Grotegut 2022, abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bpj:jbwige:v:63:y:2022:i:1:p:169-197:n:7)) [secondary]. The movement's most durable success was constitutional: Article 155 of the 1919 Weimar Constitution required that a rise in land value arising "without any labour or capital expenditure on the plot" be made "usable for the community" ([Diefenbacher 2022](https://doi.org/10.3790/vjh.91.1.69)) [secondary].

The German naval colony of Kiautschou (Qingdao) became the reformers' showcase. From the occupation in November 1897 all land transfers needed German approval; the administration bought land at an average of 0.05 Mark per square metre in 1898 and auctioned building plots from October 1898 to spring 1900 at an average of 1.744 Mark ([Matzat 1998](https://tsingtau.org/wp-content/uploads/neues_zu_schrameier_tsingtau_org.pdf)) [secondary]. Wilhelm Schrameier's ordinance of 2 September 1898 levied an annual **6% tax on land value excluding improvements**, to be reassessed from recorded sales ("best every 3 years" in his draft), rising by three points every three years up to 24% if a plot stayed unbuilt, plus a tax of **one-third of the net increase in value** at sale or at least every 25 years ([Silagi 1984](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3486727); [Warner, DHM](https://www.dhm.de/archiv/ausstellungen/tsingtau/katalog/auf1_6.htm); [Matzat 1998](https://tsingtau.org/wp-content/uploads/neues_zu_schrameier_tsingtau_org.pdf)) [secondary]. Valuation was easy because the state was the first seller: the auction price became the first assessed value. Land taxes were about a quarter of the colony's regular revenue in 1901–03, but in 1908 its own revenues of 1.7 million Mark stood against a Reich subsidy of 9.7 million ([Matzat 1998](https://tsingtau.org/wp-content/uploads/neues_zu_schrameier_tsingtau_org.pdf) [secondary]; [1908 budget law, via Wikisource](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesetz,_betreffend_die_Feststellung_des_Haushalts-Etats_f%C3%BCr_die_Schutzgebiete_auf_das_Rechnungsjahr_1908)), and the increment tax yielded "minimal" sums. Its record is disputed. Silagi, writing with Georgist funding, called the ordinance "extraordinarily successful"; the historian Matzat rejects Silagi's claim that George inspired it; the architectural historian Warner found rents 30–50% higher than in Shanghai; and a contemporary editor dismissed by the governor accused the administration of speculation "in the grand style" (sources as above) [secondary; disputed]. Schrameier later advised Sun Yat-sen in Canton, where he died in 1926, and the Nationalist land law of 1930 drew on his programme ([Silagi 1984](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3486727); [Warner, DHM](https://www.dhm.de/archiv/ausstellungen/tsingtau/katalog/auf1_6.htm)) [secondary]. Sun had already proposed that owners declare their own land values, the state tax them at that value and reserve a right to buy at it, so that "the price fixes itself" ([Chinese Wikipedia, 平均地權](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-tw/%E5%B9%B3%E5%9D%87%E5%9C%B0%E6%AC%8A), quoting his 1912 speech) [unresolved].

### Denmark valued land separately and taxed it

Denmark built a land-value system that, much changed, still operates. The law of 15 May 1903 replaced hartkorn with taxation of market value: owners filled in forms, local valuation councils drew up lists and a superior council heard complaints, and the first general valuation, as of 1 July 1904, produced **357,751 valuations worth DKK 5,358 million** ([Statistics Denmark 1906](https://rss.dst.dk/pubfile/20984/skyld1904); [Statistics Denmark 1964](https://rss.dst.dk/pubfile/20969/vurd1960)). A law of 22 December 1915 then ordered the councils to fix separately "what the land could be assumed to be worth in an unbuilt state, given its nature and location and an economically good use", a definition still essentially in force a century later ([Statistics Denmark 1919](https://rss.dst.dk/pubfile/20981/skyld1916); [Engberg report 2014](https://www.skm.dk/media/Skatteministeriet/Dokumenter/PDF%27er/forbedring-af-ejendomsvurderingen_web.pdf)). At that first separate valuation, on 1 July 1916, land made up **46.5% of all property value**: 55.4% in the countryside, 37.5% in Copenhagen and 31.5% in provincial towns ([Statistics Denmark 1919](https://rss.dst.dk/pubfile/20981/skyld1916)). The law of 7 August 1922 levied a state **land tax** (*grundskyld*) of 1.5‰ of land value and 1.1‰ on the rest of property value, with a DKK 10,000 deduction per property ([Statistics Denmark 1926](https://rss.dst.dk/pubfile/20979/skyld1924)); a municipal *grundskyld* followed under a law of 31 March 1926 and became the durable core of Danish land taxation ([Den juridiske vejledning, C.H.4.3](https://tax.dk/jv/ch/C_H_4_3.htm)). Farmland was valued as if part of "a medium-sized farm in medium-good cultivation" ([Engberg report](https://www.skm.dk/media/Skatteministeriet/Dokumenter/PDF%27er/forbedring-af-ejendomsvurderingen_web.pdf)), a rule the Danish encyclopedia Lex says puts production land at about a third of its market value ([Lex: grundskyld](https://lex.dk/grundskyld)) [secondary]. The Georgist Retsforbundet (Justice Party), founded in 1919, entered parliament in 1926 with 1.3% of the vote ([Lex: Retsforbundet](https://lex.dk/Retsforbundet)) [secondary].

### Britain's 1909 "Domesday" of land values was undone by the courts and the war

Britain attempted to value every parcel of land in the kingdom. Lloyd George's "People's Budget" proposed an **Increment Value Duty** of 20% on any rise in site value, charged on sale, lease or death; a **Reversion Duty** of 10% on the benefit to a landlord when a lease ended; and an **Undeveloped Land Duty** of ½d in the pound a year (about 0.2%) on the capital site value of undeveloped land, all measured from a "datum line" of 30 April 1909 ([HC Deb, 11 May 1909](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1909/may/11/land-value-duties); [TNA research guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)). The Finance (1909–10) Act passed on 29 April 1910 ([HC Deb, 30 April 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/apr/30/land-duties)), and the Inland Revenue set up a Valuation Office for England and Wales (1910) and Scotland (1911) to value every parcel in the kingdom, separating the bare site from buildings, timber and owners' improvements ([TNA guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/valuation-office-survey-land-value-ownership-1910-1915/)). More than **10 million owners' forms** went out and 9.6 million had come back by 31 March 1912; staff peaked at **4,882** in August 1914; and by 30 September 1915 the office had made **7,784,000 provisional valuations** covering 56 million acres worth £5.2 billion ([HC Deb, 14 July 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties); [HC Deb, 4 May 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/may/04/reduction-of-staff-land-valuation)). Its 95,579 field books survive in the National Archives ([TNA, IR 58](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C9396)).

The fieldwork largely succeeded; the law did not. On 28 February 1914 Mr Justice Scrutton decided the Norton Malreward and Chells cases against the Crown, and valuations for the undeveloped land duty on farmland were suspended ([HC Deb, 21 April 1914](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1914/apr/21/valuation-of-agricultural-hereditaments)). In July 1914 the House of Lords split evenly in the Lumsden case, leaving a builder liable to increment duty although the bare site's value had not risen, and the government's promised repair fell with its Revenue Bill ([HC Deb, 23 July 1914](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1914/jul/23/land-taxes-lumsden-case)). War then cut the staff to 1,983 by November 1915. The three repealed duties yielded only **£1,339,000** in total, while the separate Mineral Rights Duty produced £5.8 million by 1931 ([HC Deb, 30 April 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/apr/30/land-duties)); one printed Hansard figure of £21 million cannot be reconciled with these and may be a transcription error [unresolved]. In April 1920 Austen Chamberlain announced repeal of duties that were "unworkable" and "produced hardly any revenue", hoping taxpayers would "let bygones be bygones" over small refunds ([HC Deb, 19 April 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/apr/19/land-values-duties)), and £228,591 was repaid without interest ([HC Deb, 27 October 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1920/oct/27/land-values-duties-repayments)). The cost of the valuation was never officially stated: a backbencher's figure of "something like £2,000,000" is only a claim made in debate ([HC Deb, 14 July 1920](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1920/jul/14/clause-49-repeal-of-land-valves-duties)), and a recent essay's assertion that administration cost four times the yield gives no source ([Works in Progress](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/the-failure-of-the-land-value-tax)) [secondary; unresolved]. The Valuation Office survived; its successor still values British property.

### North America exempted buildings, and the 1913 bust tested the base

North American reformers needed a way to put a separate, published value on every urban lot, and William A. Somers of St Paul supplied one. His 1901 book argued that while a city has no shared opinion of individual lots, it does have a shared opinion "of the relative value of streets". Citizen committees fixed a **unit value**, the value of one foot of street frontage at a standard depth in the middle of each block, marked it on public maps, and converted it into a value for every lot with printed depth and corner tables; buildings were valued separately at the "cost of reproduction" ([Somers 1901](https://archive.org/details/valuationofreale00somerich)). Cleveland's mayor Tom L. Johnson, a Georgist, opened a "Tax School" in 1901 that hung "a map sixteen feet square and comprising one whole ward" in City Hall for public argument and recomputed the values of the city's 101,000 parcels, whose assessments had ranged "from two per cent. of to sixty-eight per cent. above" market value; a property owner and Republican leader sued on 8 October 1902 and an injunction ended the school ([Johnson, *My Story*](https://archive.org/details/mystory0000toml)). Johnson's memoir is a partisan account.

Western Canadian cities went further. Vancouver assessed buildings at 50% from 1895 and 25% from 1906 and exempted them entirely in 1910, while British Columbia law forbade municipalities to tax improvements at more than half their value and, by Robert Murray Haig's 1915 count, 39 of its 61 municipalities exempted them fully ([Haig 1915](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft)). Edmonton's 1904 charter exempted improvements; a local editor told Haig that "the single tax in Edmonton is an accident … The citizens, meeting together to decide what to tax, decided to tax the other fellow", since most vacant land belonged to non-residents (same). Calgary cut building assessments step by step from 1909, and in 1912 Alberta put all its towns on a land-only basis "at one long leap" (same). In the United States, Houston's elected tax commissioner J. J. Pastoriza assessed land at about 70% and buildings at 33⅓%, later 25%, without any legal authority, telling voters: "If you like the plan, vote for me; if you don't, vote against me" (same). A Texas appeals court enjoined the **Houston Plan** on 25 May 1915 because the state constitution demands that "Taxation shall be equal and uniform", a **uniformity clause** ([City of Houston v. Baker](https://hallapproved.com/tx/cases/appeals/1915/4148821)). Pennsylvania did by statute what Houston did by **fractional assessment** (assessing different property at different percentages of value): Act No. 147 of 15 May 1913 required Pittsburgh and Scranton to separate "the buildings on land and the land exclusive of the buildings" and to cut the city tax rate on buildings in steps of a tenth, roughly every three years, until it reached half the land rate in 1925, a **split-rate** or "graded" tax ([Haig 1915](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft)).

Then the western Canadian land boom collapsed in 1913. Haig, commissioned by New York City in 1914 to investigate, found unpaid taxes at the end of 1913 of $576,933.97 in Saskatoon and $689,969.19 in Vancouver, less than half of Edmonton's 1914 taxes paid by mid-March, and noted that "Calgary has not had a tax sale of land since 1911, Vancouver since 1909 and Edmonton never!" ([Haig 1915](https://archive.org/details/exemptionofimpro00haiguoft)). His conclusions were cautious. Before-and-after comparisons in such dynamic cities "invite all sorts of statistical disasters"; the shift had worked where "land values were increasing enormously", so that "most of the shock has been absorbed by new land values", but had "very undesirable effects where its introduction has involved a material decrease in the tax base"; and in the large cities low rates had not "seriously interfered with" speculation (same). Claims of success were equally hard to verify: Pastoriza called Houston's results "magical", but Haig's investigator sat for hours in the permit office and "heard no applicant plead the pressure of taxation" (same). Calgary's city treasurer recalled in 1969 that when "inflated land prices used as a basis of assessment collapsed", the cities "reverted to taxing improvements to stave off bankruptcy" ([Arscott 1969](https://cooperative-individualism.org/arscott-f-g_site-taxation-1969-may.pdf)) [secondary]; the exact years of reversal are unresolved.

### Japan revalued house plots, and southern Africa and Jamaica took up site rating

Japan's frozen 1870s values were finally confronted for urban land. A 1906 committee judged the five-yearly revaluation promise of 1873 a "dead letter", and the Residential Land Value Revision Act of 1910 reset house-plot values at **ten times annual rental value**, the multiple derived by capitalising rent ([NTA, tax history vol. 1](https://www.nta.go.jp/about/organization/ntc/sozei/01chiso/kaidai.htm)). Tax offices drew up rents and **464 elected committees with 4,178 members** reviewed them; when the results came out in December 1911, values had risen **4.44 times** on average, yet total residential land tax barely moved, from ¥16.12 million to ¥16.04 million, because the reform was designed to redistribute the burden rather than raise it ([NTA essay](https://www.nta.go.jp/about/organization/ntc/sozei/network/302.htm)). After the 1923 earthquake, Tokyo's Reconstruction Bureau valued plots quickly with a street-by-street **route-value** method modelled on Cleveland's, the ancestor of Japan's present-day route prices ([NTA, tax history vol. 2](https://www.nta.go.jp/about/organization/ntc/sozei/02chiso/kaidai.htm)).

**Site value rating**, the local taxation of land value alone, spread through southern and eastern Africa from the Transvaal. A provincial commission investigated rating in 1912, and in 1916 land value taxation was introduced in the Transvaal, "apparently to stimulate property development and to counter land speculation", becoming "the model or template for much of the rest of the country" ([McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf)) [secondary]. Kenya's early legislation copied the Transvaal ordinance and Australian and New Zealand practice: Nairobi, which had rated on annual rental value from 1901 and then on flat rates, introduced unimproved site value rating to encourage development, discourage speculation "especially by absentee landlords" and keep administration simple with few trained valuers, though the sources date it to 1921–23 or to 1928 (same; [Franzsen & McCluskey 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)) [secondary; unresolved]. Jamaica's Valuation Law of 1901 asked owners to supply three figures themselves, called "ingivings": gross present value, net annual rental value and "the value of the land in its natural and unimproved state" ([McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf)) [secondary].

## 1925–1970: depression and war froze the bases, and planners turned to betterment charges and public leaseholds

### The interwar years fixed values and rolled back national land taxes

Weimar Germany's answer to hyperinflation shows how a tax can be built on a deliberately frozen base. Inflation had wiped out most mortgage debt on pre-war buildings, so from 1 April 1924 the states had to levy a **Hauszinssteuer** (house-rent tax) to claw back part of owners' windfall, assessed either on the gold-mark "peace rent" of July 1914 or as a surcharge on the state property tax; rates ran from 16% of that rent in Waldeck to 36% in Bavaria in 1925 ([Bundestag research service, WD 4-3000-133/20](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/817898/af9a120db39ad0dfc3274152b3d0f2e9/WD-4-133-20-pdf-data.pdf)) [secondary]. It supplied more than 10%, and later 20%, of state and municipal revenue; in 1942 owners were made to buy it out with ten years' payments, as if it would have run until 1956, raising almost 8 billion Reichsmarks for the war (same) [secondary]. Meanwhile the 1934 Valuation Act introduced **unit values** (*Einheitswerte*), a single official tax value for each property fixed at a "main assessment" date and meant to approximate market value then. The first main assessment was as of 1 January 1935, and the law promised a new one every six years ([BVerfG, 1 BvL 11/14](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Entscheidungen/DE/2018/04/ls20180410_1bvl001114.html)). A Reich land tax law of 1936 gave the revenue to the municipalities, a price-stop decree of November 1936 froze land prices for more than twenty years, and in 1944 the national increment tax was repealed and replaced by a 2% surcharge on transfer tax, at which point "the idea of a special taxation of land value increments was practically abandoned" ([WD 4-3000-026/18](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/547158/6ff041fce870e40344bd7424a2863154/WD-4-026-18-pdf.pdf); [WD 4-3000-133/20](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/817898/af9a120db39ad0dfc3274152b3d0f2e9/WD-4-133-20-pdf-data.pdf)) [secondary].

Britain tried once more to tax the value of all land. On 4 May 1931 Philip Snowden moved a tax of **1d in the pound a year (about 0.42%) on the capital value of every unit of land in Great Britain**, valued as if bare of buildings, with a valuation as from 1 August 1931 of 10–12 million units, revaluation every five years ([HC Deb, 4 May 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/may/04/land-values-tax-1)). Snowden called it "more simple and more general" than the 1909 scheme and hoped the valuation would later let local rates shift onto site values (same). After the 1931 financial crisis the National Government suspended the valuation in early 1932 before any tax was levied, and in 1934 a Commons committee agreed its repeal by 232 votes to 56, Neville Chamberlain calling it "useless lumber" ([HC Deb, 5 June 1934](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1934/jun/05/clause-25-repeal-of-land-value-tax)). One provision survived, the duty to show sale and lease documents to the Revenue, because price information was "of first importance in saving public money" (same). In the repeal debate an MP claimed that land on the Becontree estate valued at £7,000 for rating had sold for £295,000 (same); the figure is a claim made in debate, not a checked record.

Elsewhere national land taxes were cut back. New Zealand abolished its graduated land tax on large estates in 1931, at an estimated cost of £320,000, and its government proposed restoring it in 1936 ([NZ Herald, 1936](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/NZH19360805.2.124)) [snippet; unresolved]. Canberra's leaseholders had their rents cut by 20%, backdated to 1 July 1931, and the minister could write down the **unimproved capital value** of their blocks for up to five years from 1933 ([City Area Leases Ordinance 1935](https://www.legislation.act.gov.au/DownloadFile/ord/1935-1/19350104-47760/PDF/1935-1.PDF)). The same 1935 ordinance lengthened the interval between rent re-appraisals from ten years to twenty, and from then on rents were fixed at 5% of assessed value, with any higher bid paid as an up-front cash **premium** (same; [1988 House report](https://aphref.aph.gov.au/house/committee/reports/1988/1988_pp409.pdf)).

Japan moved its whole land tax onto rental value. A field survey of 1918–21 and the Land Rental Value Survey Act of 1926 gave every parcel in Japan a rental value by December 1927, valuing zones of similar land together instead of plot by plot; the Land Tax Act of 1931 made registered rental value the base for all land at 3.8%, revisable every ten years ([NTA, tax history vol. 2](https://www.nta.go.jp/about/organization/ntc/sozei/02chiso/kaidai.htm)). Officials chose rent because it "best expresses the land's use value" and is "comparatively easy to survey" (same). Only one ten-yearly revision ever happened, in 1938; the next was overtaken by war and inflation, and rental values were abolished in 1950 (same).

Denmark kept its land tax but struggled with its companion tax on increments. Land values fell at the 1927 and 1932 valuations as farm values declined ([Statistics Denmark 1964](https://rss.dst.dk/pubfile/20969/vurd1960)). The **grundstigningsskyld** (1926–1965) taxed the rise in land value above a base-date valuation, on the reasoning that owners had already paid for past increases but future socially created ones should go to the public; it was eventually abolished as too complicated to administer, partly because of an adjustment meant to exempt purely inflationary rises ([Lex: grundstigningsskyld](https://lex.dk/grundstigningsskyld)) [secondary]. The valuation rules took care that the tax itself did not depress the values on which it was charged: land was to be valued "without regard to the increment tax" ([Statistics Denmark 1964](https://rss.dst.dk/pubfile/20969/vurd1960)). In interwar Estonia, cities could tax land and buildings separately, and Tallinn as a rule revalued every five years ([Sultanjants 2020](https://digikogu.taltech.ee/en/Download/fc77e15a-37dc-4c9c-8383-e692c97643ac)) [secondary].

### After 1945, planners taxed betterment and states built new valuation systems

After 1945 the debate turned to who should own the value that public decisions create, under the heading of **betterment**: the rise in land value caused by planning permission, rezoning or public investment, sometimes called **development value**. Denmark's Land Tax Commission, appointed in 1948, reported on 14 October 1954 that because land rent "arises solely from scarcity of and demand for land … it seems natural that the whole land rent should accrue to the society that creates it", and drafted a state land rent of 4% of land value ([Folketingstidende 1956–57, L 105](https://folketingstidende.dk/samling/19561/lovforslag/L105/19561_L105_som_fremsat.pdf)). Retsforbundet, at its peak of 8.2% of the vote in 1950, proposed in January 1957 that owners could ask the state to buy out their land value with 4% bonds and then pay a 4% annual land rent, with the proceeds used to cut customs and consumption duties (same; [Lex: Retsforbundet](https://lex.dk/Retsforbundet)) [secondary for the vote share]. The party then sat in the "triangle government" of 1957–60, but the notes could not confirm any land-tax law it achieved [unresolved]. What did pass was a 1956 law requiring a general valuation "in 1956 and every fourth year thereafter" ([Statistics Denmark 1964](https://rss.dst.dk/pubfile/20969/vurd1960)). By the 1960 valuation land was **25.1% of total property value**, against 46.5% in 1916 (same). The state land tax faded from the revenue records after 1965–66, leaving the municipal layer ([StatBank SKAT](https://www.statbank.dk/SKAT)). A new **release levy** (*frigørelsesafgift*), proposed in March 1969, taxed the uplift when farmland was rezoned for urban or summer-house use ([Folketingstidende 1968–69, L 33](https://folketingstidende.dk/samling/19681/lovforslag/L33/19681_L33_som_fremsat.pdf)).

Britain built three successive taxes on development value, and each was abolished by the next Conservative government. The Town and Country Planning Act 1947 set up a Central Land Board to assess **development charges** and created a £300 million fund to compensate owners for the loss of development rights ([HC Deb, 7 July 1947](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1947/jul/07/clause-1-the-central-land-board)); the charge is widely described as 100% of the uplift, but the rate could not be checked [unresolved]. By January 1952 the Board's staff were handling "nearly 950,000" claims on the fund while collecting £3.6 million of charge in 1951 ([HC Deb, 29 January 1952](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1952/jan/29/development-charge-revenue)); £12.3 million in cash had been paid by October 1952 ([HC Deb, 24 October 1952](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1952/oct/24/development-charge-payments)). The minister Ernest Marples called the charge "the worst part" of the Act, citing arbitrary assessment and 39 of 197 Brighton building licences handed back ([HC Deb, 4 July 1952](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1952/jul/04/town-and-country-planning-development)); it was ended by the 1953 planning legislation, whose exact dates could not be confirmed [unresolved]. The Land Commission Act 1967 introduced a **betterment levy** of 40% of development value, measured above a base linked to existing-use value ([HL Deb, 27 February 1969](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1969/feb/27/the-land-commission-and-betterment-levy)). Labour said revenue "was rising steeply to £65 million" when it was abolished in 1971, by 255 votes to 217, after a Conservative pledge; ministers argued it accelerated land price rises and that capital gains tax sufficed ([HC Deb, 9 March 1971](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1971/mar/09/abolition-of-betterment-levy-and)). In both schemes, and in the 1976 tax described below, valuers had to estimate two values per transaction, the value with permission and the value without, a heavier and more contestable task than one market value.

British local rates, meanwhile, were levied on occupiers on the annual rental value of land and buildings together. Revaluations took effect in the mid-1950s and about 1963, and the 1973 revaluation was carried out by Inland Revenue valuation officers on open-market rental value ([HC Deb, 5 April 1955](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1955/apr/05/valuation-for-rating-revaluation); [HC Deb, 14 February 1973](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1973/feb/14/rating-revaluation); [HC Deb, 24 November 1971](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1971/nov/24/rate-assessments-revaluation)). The 1692 land tax was finally made compulsorily redeemable in 1950 and abolished in 1963 ([TNA, C1030](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C1030)).

Australia ended its federal land tax in 1952. Treasury attributes this to the tax's declining effectiveness as wealth diversified beyond land, exemptions for primary producers that confined it to urban property, revenue that swung with town property markets, and poor integration with income tax ([Treasury 2006](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/economic-roundup-winter-2006/a-brief-history-of-australias-tax-system)) [secondary]; whether it ended in June 1952 or by an abolition act of 1953 is unresolved. In New Zealand, by contrast, land-value rating "had become the dominant system" by the Second World War and stayed so into the 1980s, helped by the rule that a ratepayers' poll was needed to abandon it ([McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf)) [secondary].

Taiwan made land value taxation constitutional. The Nationalist Land Law was promulgated on 30 June 1930 and took effect in 1936 ([Laws & Regulations Database](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawHistory.aspx?pcode=D0060001)). Article 143 of the 1947 Constitution states that "private land shall pay tax according to its value, and the government may purchase it according to its value", and that where land value has increased "not through labour or capital, the State shall levy a land value increment tax, to be shared by the people" ([ROC Constitution](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=A0000001)). The Act for the Implementation of Equalisation of Urban Land Rights followed on 26 August 1954, and land values prescribed in 1964 became the statutory starting point for measuring increments ([Equalisation of Land Rights Act, history](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawHistory.aspx?pcode=D0060009); [Act, Art. 38](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=D0060009)).

Post-war Japan rebuilt its system around official land prices. On the 1949 recommendation of the American Shoup Mission, the 1950 reform abolished the land and house taxes and created a municipal **fixed asset tax** on land, houses and depreciable business assets, justified on the benefit principle that owners use municipal services ([MIC chronology](https://www.soumu.go.jp/main_content/000998640.pdf); [MIC overview](https://www.soumu.go.jp/main_sosiki/jichi_zeisei/czaisei/czaisei_seido/149767_08.html)). National valuation standards followed in the early 1960s, and from 1955 the National Tax Agency set **route prices** (*rosenka*), values per square metre attached to each street, for inheritance and gift tax in six large cities ([NTA, vol. 2](https://www.nta.go.jp/about/organization/ntc/sozei/02chiso/kaidai.htm)). The Land Price Publication Act of 1969 created an annual **published land price**: a Land Appraisal Committee obtains appraisals from "two or more real estate appraisers" for each standard site and publishes a "normal price" as at 1 January, for land valued as if any buildings were absent ([e-Gov, Act No. 49 of 1969](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/344AC0000000049)). The first publication, in 1970, covered 970 sites around Tokyo, Osaka and Nagoya; by 1975 there were 15,010 nationwide ([MLIT](https://www.mlit.go.jp/common/001333701.pdf)).

West Germany restored the land tax in 1951, but its valuation base kept ageing ([WD 4-3000-026/18](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/547158/6ff041fce870e40344bd7424a2863154/WD-4-026-18-pdf.pdf)) [secondary]. When the Federal Building Code of 1960 lifted the land-price stop, a higher tax on vacant building-ready plots, later called **Grundsteuer C**, applied in 1961 and 1962, rising from a normal 10‰ base rate to 30‰ for plots left unbuilt; it was abolished in 1964, retroactively from 1963, as "not a suitable means" of influencing the building-land market and amid constitutional doubts that the federal tax court later did not confirm ([WD 4-3000-133/20](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/817898/af9a120db39ad0dfc3274152b3d0f2e9/WD-4-133-20-pdf-data.pdf)) [secondary]. A new main assessment of unit values as at **1 January 1964** was ordered in 1965 and took until 1974 to apply; a 1970 law then left the date of the next one to "a special law", which, as the Constitutional Court noted in 2018, "has not been passed to this day" ([BVerfG](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Entscheidungen/DE/2018/04/ls20180410_1bvl001114.html); [BVerfG press release 21/2018](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/DE/2018/bvg18-021.html)). Austria's land and valuation laws of 1955 used the same unit-value design ([BMF Austria](https://www.bmf.gv.at/themen/steuern/immobilien-grundstuecke/grundbesitzabgaben-einheitsbewertung/grundsteuer.html)).

Governments that owned the land collected its value directly. Canberra switched to bidding solely for up-front premiums from 1962, with rents re-appraised only "during the twentieth year … and during each twentieth year thereafter" ([Explanatory Memorandum, 1961](https://www.legislation.act.gov.au/DownloadFile/es/db_42717/19610713-49290/PDF/db_42717.PDF)). Because of those twenty-year gaps, the economist Max Neutze later wrote, "long before 1970, most rents had lagged well behind 5 per cent of the current market value" ([1988 House report](https://aphref.aph.gov.au/house/committee/reports/1988/1988_pp409.pdf)). Amsterdam, which since 1896 had issued municipal land "as a rule" in **ground lease** (*erfpacht*) so that "the increase in land value accrues to the community", moved in 1915 to continuous leases whose annual rent (*canon*) was reset to current land value at the end of each long period, and from 1966 indexed the canon to inflation within periods of fifty years ([Amsterdam council inquiry 2021](https://www.gemeente.nu/content/uploads/sites/5/2021/06/Raadsonderzoek-erfpachtbeleid-Amsterdam.pdf)). Singapore went the other way, acquiring land: the state owned 44% of it in 1960, a 1964 amendment to the Planning Act introduced a **Development Charge** on the uplift from planning permission, the Land Acquisition Act followed in 1966 and the land-sales programme in 1967 ([Phang 2024](https://dbedt.hawaii.gov/hcda/files/2024/08/GEN-09-04-24_ITEM-2_Professor-Sock-Yong-Phang-Presentation.pdf) [secondary]; [Centre for Liveable Cities](https://knowledgehub.clc.gov.sg/publications-library/the-government-land-sales-programme-turning-plans-into-reality)).

Former British colonies consolidated site value rating. Jamaica's Land Valuation Act of 1956 replaced owners' declarations, which according to Hansard "would lead to competitive under assessment", with official valuations of unimproved value, aiming to tax "values created by the community at large" and to discourage "the withholding of land from use" ([McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf)) [secondary]. Kenya consolidated its rating law in the Valuation for Rating Act (1956) and the Rating Act (1963), under which most towns chose unimproved site value (same; [Franzsen & McCluskey 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)) [secondary].

Annual **net wealth taxes**, on a person's assets minus debts, also depended on property valuations. They spread across Europe from the Swiss cantons (1840–1970), Norway (1892), Denmark (1903), Sweden (1911, as a separate tax from 1947), Finland (1919) and Luxembourg (1934) to West Germany (1952), Austria (1954) and the Netherlands (1965) ([OECD 2018](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/04/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_g1g89919/9789264290303-en.pdf); [Du Rietz & Henrekson](https://www.ifn.se/media/apndv2ws/wp1000.pdf)) [the Swedish dates secondary]. Sweden took real estate at its official tax-assessed value (same) [secondary], and West Germany, from 1974, at 140% of the 1964 unit values ([BVerfG 1995, 2 BvL 37/91](https://www.servat.unibe.ch/dfr/bv093121.html)). Whatever ailed the land valuation would ail the wealth tax too.

## 1970–2008: freezes, caps and abolitions as market values outran official ones

### Valuation dates froze across Europe and North America

Much of Europe stopped revaluing property for its recurrent taxes in these decades, and some of the dates set then are still in use. West Germany's 1964 unit values were first applied in 1974 and never replaced; after reunification the eastern states kept values based on 1 January 1935, with a floor-area substitute where none existed ([BR-Drs. 515/16](http://www.bundesrat.de/SharedDocs/drucksachen/2016/0501-0600/515-16.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=1)). Austria's land tax still rests on unit values for non-farm property as at **1 January 1973** ([BMF Austria](https://www.bmf.gv.at/themen/steuern/immobilien-grundstuecke/grundbesitzabgaben-einheitsbewertung/einheitsbewertung.html)). French dwellings are valued by reference to the rental values of **1 January 1970** (1975 in the overseas departments) ([BOFiP](https://bofip.impots.gouv.fr/bofip/4889-PGP.html/identifiant=BOI-IF-TFB-20-10-10-20121210)). Belgium's "cadastral income", a notional net rent, refers to rents actually charged on **1 January 1975**, set in the last general revaluation (or "perequation") of 1975–80; it has been indexed to consumer prices since 1991, which left assessed values "on average below half of the market value" by 2012 ([Gäbele 2010](https://www.fig.net/resources/proceedings/fig_proceedings/fig2010/papers/ts03j/ts03j_gabele_vanderschueren_4628.pdf); [European Commission 2012](https://ec.europa.eu/economy_finance/publications/occasional_paper/2012/pdf/ocp119_en.pdf)). Italy revalued its cadastral incomes in 1990 by reference to 1988–89; the law foresaw revision every ten years, "but this has never been carried out", and by 2011 home market values averaged **3.73 times** the tax base, so the government raised its multiplier by 60% instead ([ANSA via Blitzquotidiano](https://archivio.blitzquotidiano.it/cronaca-italia/ansa-focus-casa-rendite-37-volte-sotto-mercato-ferme-da-89-1064459)) [secondary]. **Indexation**, raising every value by the same percentage, protects revenue against inflation but preserves whatever relative distortions existed at the base date; only a **revaluation**, re-estimating every value at a new date, changes who pays what. The European Commission summed up the politics in 2012: "any revaluation of properties creates losers and winners, with losers often being very vocal", producing what the Commission, citing the Mirrlees Review, called a "tyranny of the status quo" ([European Commission 2012](https://ec.europa.eu/economy_finance/publications/occasional_paper/2012/pdf/ocp119_en.pdf)).

Britain froze its domestic values twice. The 1973 rating revaluation was the last for homes in England and Wales ([HC Deb, 24 November 1971](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1971/nov/24/rate-assessments-revaluation)); no later one was found before domestic rates were replaced, first by the community charge or "poll tax", a charge on each adult (in Scotland from 1 April 1989), and then by **council tax** from 1 April 1993 ([HL Deb, 9 April 1987](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1987/apr/09/abolition-of-domestic-rates-etc-scotland); [VOA Council Tax Manual](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/council-tax-manual/section-1-introduction-and-essential-background)). Council tax places each dwelling, according to the same manual, in one of eight **bands** by its estimated sale value on **1 April 1991**, the **antecedent valuation date** (the date at which values are estimated, often years before a list takes effect). England's first revaluation, due in 2007 on 2005 values, was postponed, and the Council Tax (New Valuation Lists for England) Act 2006 left the date of any new list to ministerial order (same; [CT(NVLE) Act 2006](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/7/section/1/enacted)); the notes found no such order. Wales did revalue, on 2003 values with effect from 1 April 2005: **33.3% of dwellings moved up at least one band and 8.0% down**, with 63.5% moving up in Cardiff but only 6.8% in Blaenau Gwent ([VOA data](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80c92be5274a2e8ab52281/Band_changes_due_to_2005_reval_in_Wales_final.xls)). Business property, by contrast, has been revalued on rental value every five years since 1990 ([NDR Act 2023, Explanatory Notes](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/53/notes/division/3/index.htm)). Northern Ireland valued homes at **1 January 2005** capital values, operative from 2007, and bills homes worth more than £400,000 as if they were worth £400,000 ([DoF NI](https://www.finance-ni.gov.uk/topics/valuation-lists); [DoF NI consultation 2023](https://minutes.belfastcity.gov.uk/documents/s112576/Appendix%202%20-%20DoF%20revenue%20raising%20consultation%20domestic.pdf)).

California turned freezing into constitutional design. **Proposition 13**, approved on 6 June 1978, rolled assessments back to 1975 market values, capped the tax rate at 1% plus voter-approved debt, and limited annual growth in assessed value to 2% or inflation if lower, with reassessment to market value only on a change of ownership or new construction ([California Board of Equalization, Pub. 29](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf)). It "converted the market value-based property tax system to an acquisition value-based system" (same): an **acquisition-value** (or base-year) system taxes a property on its purchase price indexed by a capped factor, so neighbours with identical homes pay different amounts according to when they bought. County property tax revenue fell from $10.3 billion in 1977–78 to $5.04 billion the next year, and the courts upheld the measure in 1978 and, at the US Supreme Court, in 1992 (same). The Legislative Analyst found that 16% of properties sold in 1977–78 against 5% in 2014–15, with Proposition 13 "some part" of the decline, and that "the longer a property is owned, the wider this gap tends to grow" ([LAO 2016](https://lao.ca.gov/reports/2016/3497/common-claims-prop13-091916.pdf)). Florida's 1992 "Save Our Homes" amendment capped annual increases in a homestead's assessed value at the lower of 3% or consumer price inflation, resetting to "just value" on sale ([Florida House analysis, 2026](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/Analyses/h0001z.SAC.PDF); [enrolled CS/HJR 1-F](https://flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/BillText/er/PDF)). Such **assessment caps** limit growth in the valuation itself, unlike **rate caps** such as California's 1%. In Pennsylvania, where 67 counties decide when to reassess, seven had not done so since at least the mid-1980s by 2010, and in 2009 the state Supreme Court found that Allegheny County's use of a base year, as implemented, violated uniformity ([Lincoln Institute, Pennsylvania](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf)) [secondary].

### Some governments abandoned land-value taxes while others created them

New Zealand dismantled its national land tax. By 1989 it carried a $175,000 exemption that the government planned to remove as a first step ([Press, 1989](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19890728.2.12.29)) [snippet], and the Land Tax Abolition Act 1990 ended it "on and from the year of payment commencing on 1 April 1992" ([Land Tax Abolition Act 1990](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1990/92/en/latest/)) [snippet]. Local rating shifted too. After the poll requirement ended in 1976, nine of the 57 councils that rated land value switched to **capital value** (land plus buildings) between 1989 and 2000, beginning with Dunedin, and "no authorities have switched to land value rating" ([McCluskey et al. 2002](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/681_mccluskey_grimes.pdf)) [secondary]. In 1998 the government's Valuation New Zealand was split into a regulator, the Office of the Valuer-General, and a state-owned provider, Quotable Value, so that councils now buy valuations in a market audited by the regulator ([Wellington City Archives](https://archivesonline.wcc.govt.nz/nodes/view/7993)) [snippet].

Canberra ended the rent that Barton had promised. From 1 January 1971 the Commonwealth stopped charging land rent and abolished periodic re-appraisals; buyers instead paid reserve prices set to recover the cost of acquiring and developing land, and general rates were raised to levels comparable with other capitals ([Explanatory Memorandum, 1970](https://www.legislation.act.gov.au/DownloadFile/es/db_40743/19701217-46859/PDF/db_40743.PDF)). Rent "was reduced to five cents payable on demand" ([1988 House report](https://aphref.aph.gov.au/house/committee/reports/1988/1988_pp409.pdf)). A lessee changing a lease's permitted use now paid a **betterment charge of 50% of the increase in value**, less $1,500 (same). The minister insisted that "the system of land tenure was in no way affected"; Neutze judged that view one that "may have been legally correct but was economically nonsensical", and described the 50% as "essentially an arbitrary fraction"; in 1988 a parliamentary committee recommended charging the full premium value instead (same). The territory revived optional land rent for home-buyers in 2008, with increases capped ([Land Rent Bill 2008, Explanatory Statement](https://legislation.act.gov.au/DownloadFile/es/db_32377/20080508-36556/PDF/db_32377.PDF)).

Britain's third tax on development value, **Development Land Tax**, received royal assent in July 1976 at a basic rate of 80% ([Hansard index](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/acts/development-land-tax-act-1976); [HC Deb, 11 July 1978](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1978/jul/11/development-land-tax-extention-of)). It was cut to 60% in 1979, averaged "about £40 million a year" and in its last year cost "£1 to every £13 of tax" to collect; abolishing it in 1985, by 184 votes to 117, the minister called it "extremely complicated and at a punitive rate", while Labour replied that there was no evidence it had reduced housing starts ([HC Deb, 8 May 1985](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1985/may/08/clause-87)). Whether such taxes deterred development was disputed in the debates themselves and was never settled, because each scheme was ended by a change of government.

Pennsylvania's split-rate tax spread and then contracted, leaving a body of studies whose findings are contested. Once third-class cities were authorised to set separate rates, Harrisburg adopted them in 1975; coterminous school districts were allowed to from 1993 and boroughs from 1998, and the number of users peaked in 2000 ([Lincoln Institute, Pennsylvania](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pa_july_2020_final.pdf)) [secondary]. Pittsburgh "dramatically increased the tax rate applied to land (but not structures)" in 1979–80 ([Oates & Schwab 1997, abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ntj/journl/v50y1997i1p1-21.html)) [secondary], and Aliquippa from 1988 taxed land at more than seven times the building rate ([Governing 2019](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html)) [secondary]. Then the 2000–01 county reassessment raised Pittsburgh's land taxes by 81% and building taxes by 43%; after thousands of appeals the city scrapped the split rate in 2001, prompting the Lincoln Institute's Joan Youngman to observe: "If you fail to revalue, you'll have a revolt because you get sticker shock" (same) [secondary]. On effects, Oates and Schwab found Pittsburgh's building activity rose significantly relative to other Midwestern cities in the 1980s; Plassmann and Tideman, comparing fifteen split-rate cities with similar single-rate cities from 1972 to 1994, found more construction; Banzhaf and Lavery found more housing units rather than bigger ones; and Yang's 2014 working paper found effects in the same direction but larger, about 6.8% on a comparable measure ([Yang 2014](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf); [Banzhaf & Lavery 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v67y2010i2p169-179.html)) [secondary]. Against them, an earlier cross-section of 27 Pennsylvania cities by Mathis and Zech (1982) found no significant effect, Oates and Schwab themselves could not separate the reform from other events in a single city, and all the studies rely on a few mostly declining cities that chose to adopt the tax ([Yang 2014](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/yang_wp14zy1.pdf)) [secondary]. The size and cause of the effect therefore remain disputed; one scholar called the Pennsylvania record "a compelling example of the limited role that evidence often plays in policy decisions" ([Governing 2019](https://www.governing.com/archive/gov-land-tax.html)) [secondary].

Japan's land bubble produced two holding taxes, each introduced as prices rose and suspended as they fell. A local **special land holding tax** (1973) was charged on large holdings at their acquisition cost rather than any assessed value ([Tokyo Metropolitan Tax Bureau](https://www.tax.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/kazei/real_estate/special)). A national **land value tax** took effect on 1 January 1992 at 0.3% (0.2% in its first year) of large holdings valued at inheritance-tax route prices; its yield fell from ¥605 billion in 1993 to ¥160 billion in 1997 as land prices dropped, and it was suspended "for the time being" from 1998, though the Act remains on the statute book ([Cabinet Office Tax Commission paper, 2002](https://www.cao.go.jp/zei-cho/history/1996-2009/gijiroku/kiso/2002/pdf/kiso14i.pdf); [e-Gov](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/403AC0000000069)). The special land holding tax was likewise suspended from FY2003 ([MLIT](https://www.mlit.go.jp/yosan/yosan03/zeisei03g/zeisei03g_.html)). The lasting change was to valuation: from FY1994 municipal fixed-asset values for residential land were set at about **70% of published land prices**, with years of "burden adjustment", a phase-in that raises each plot's taxable base only a slice at a time toward its new value ([MIC overview](https://www.soumu.go.jp/main_sosiki/jichi_zeisei/czaisei/czaisei_seido/149767_08.html)). Route prices for inheritance tax sit at about 80% of published-price-based values ([NTA 2026](https://www.nta.go.jp/information/release/kokuzeicho/2026/rosenka/index.htm)), a margin meant to avoid over-taxation if prices fall ([Kotobank](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%B7%AF%E7%B7%9A%E4%BE%A1)) [secondary]; the year that rule began is unverified.

South Korea built a national official land price system in 1989–90 to serve new redistributive taxes. Licensed appraisers valued about **300,000 standard parcels** as at 1 January 1990, and some 23.9 million other private parcels were then priced from them with a comparison table of multipliers for road access, distance to stations, slope and use, so that the government "put a price on land even in remote mountain areas" ([Seoul Shinmun, 12 April 1990](https://m.seoul.co.kr/news/1990/04/12/19900412005002); [Seoul Shinmun, 20 August 1990](https://m.seoul.co.kr/news/1990/08/20/19900820005001)) [press]. Officials expected the new prices to reach 80–90% of market value, against 35–40% for the old tax bases (same). Three "public concept of land" laws of 1989 used them: a tax on excess land value gains, a ceiling of 200 *pyeong* (about 661 m²) on residential land per household in the largest cities, and development charges. The Constitutional Court ruled on 29 July 1994 that the excess-gains tax was "incompatible with the Constitution", objecting to taxing "unrealised, notional income" and criticising the land-price rules behind it, in a case brought by a Seoul owner whose leased lot had been treated as idle ([Seoul Shinmun, 30 July 1994](https://m.seoul.co.kr/news/1994/07/30/19940730001002)) [press]. The ceiling law was repealed in 1998 and declared unconstitutional in 1999 ([Seoul Shinmun, 30 April 1999](https://m.seoul.co.kr/news/1999/04/30/19990430023002)) [press]. In 2005 Korea added a national Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax on large aggregated holdings; in 2008 the court struck down its aggregation by household, prompting refunds of about ₩500 billion ([Seoul Shinmun, 14 November 2008](https://m.seoul.co.kr/news/economy/2008/11/14/20081114003006)) [press].

Post-communist Estonia created a land-only tax. Its Land Tax Act took effect on 1 July 1993, taxing land alone at 0.5% for the municipality plus 0.5% for the state, to encourage efficient use of restituted and privatised land, raise local revenue and deter holding land idle "if owning land cost nothing" ([Sultanjants 2020](https://digikogu.taltech.ee/en/Download/fc77e15a-37dc-4c9c-8383-e692c97643ac)) [secondary]. With help from the OECD and the Finnish and Danish governments, a market-value mass valuation was carried out in 1993, 1996 and 2001; fewer than 3% of taxpayers contested the 1996 results, and from 1996 all revenue went to municipalities (same) [secondary]; the land board confirms the three valuation years ([Maa-amet](https://maaruum.ee/sites/default/files/documents/2024-12/Maa%20korraline%20hindamine%20voldik%202022.pdf)). Under the old law valuation was not periodic but ordered by the government when market values had changed significantly ([Sultanjants 2020](https://digikogu.taltech.ee/en/Download/fc77e15a-37dc-4c9c-8383-e692c97643ac)) [secondary], and the **2001 values stayed the tax base until the end of 2023** ([Ministry of Finance guidance 2023](https://agri.ee/sites/default/files/documents/2023-09/KOVidele%20uute%20maamaksum%C3%A4%C3%A4rade%20kehtestamise%20juhend%202023.pdf)). Other new systems of the 1990s ranged across the spectrum: Finland's municipal property tax (1993) values land from area-price maps and buildings at replacement cost, separately ([Verohallinto](https://www.vero.fi/syventavat-vero-ohjeet/ohje-hakusivu/48219/kiinteistojen-arvostaminen-kiinteistoverotuksessa7/)); Czechia's (1993) taxes floor and land area with no valuation at all ([Act No. 338/1992](https://www.zakonyprolidi.cz/cs/1992-338)); and the Netherlands' WOZ law of 1994 underpins municipal valuations of every property that are now renewed each year ([Wet WOZ](https://wetten.overheid.nl/BWBR0007119/)). Taiwan consolidated its land value and increment taxes in a Land Tax Act of 1977 ([Land Tax Act history](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawHistory.aspx?pcode=G0340096)).

### Public landowners switched from annual rents to up-front sales

Hong Kong's handover settlement wrote a land-rent formula into treaty. Under the 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration, leases expiring before 1997 could be extended to 2047 "without additional premium" at an annual rent of **3% of rateable value**, new land grants were capped at 50 hectares a year, and a lease modification would cost a premium "equal to the difference" in land value between the old and new conditions ([Joint Declaration, Annex III](https://www.cmab.gov.hk/en/issues/jd5.htm)); Article 121 of the Basic Law carried the 3% rent past 1997 ([Basic Law](https://www.basiclaw.gov.hk/en/basiclaw/chapter5.html)). **Rateable value**, "the estimated annual open market rental value … assuming that the property was then vacant and was to let", also carries rates of 5%, and is revalued every year at a 1 October reference date ([Audit Commission 2009](https://www.aud.gov.hk/pdf_e/e53ch02.pdf)). Mainland China began selling time-limited **land-use rights** while keeping state ownership: on 1 December 1987 Shenzhen auctioned a 50-year right to 8,588 m² for 5.25 million yuan, and on 12 April 1988 the Constitution was amended to allow such transfers ([Shenzhen Municipal Government](https://www.sz.gov.cn/szstoryjp/202301/content/post_10372285.html)). Regulations of 19 May 1990 set maximum terms of 70 years for housing, 50 for industry and 40 for commerce, allowed sale by agreement, tender or auction, and provided that land and buildings revert to the state "without compensation" at expiry ([State Taxation Administration database](https://fgk.chinatax.gov.cn/zcfgk/c100010/c5192953/content.html)). The price is fixed once, at sale. Singapore's state share of land rose to 76% by 1985 and about 90% by 2005 ([Phang 2024](https://dbedt.hawaii.gov/hcda/files/2024/08/GEN-09-04-24_ITEM-2_Professor-Sock-Yong-Phang-Presentation.pdf)) [secondary].

### Nordic governments capped and froze the link to value

Denmark kept valuing but stopped charging on current values. In 1998 it replaced the income tax on homeowners' imputed rent with a **property value tax** (*ejendomsværdiskat*) of 1% of the home's value from 2000 ([Den juridiske vejledning, C.H.4.2.1](https://tax.dk/jv/ch/C_H_4_2_1.htm)). The "tax freeze" of 2001–02 then charged it on the lowest of the current value, the 2001 value plus 5%, or the 2002 value, and capped growth in the land tax base at 7% a year ([Engberg report 2014](https://www.skm.dk/media/Skatteministeriet/Dokumenter/PDF%27er/forbedring-af-ejendomsvurderingen_web.pdf)). In 2003 the state took valuation over from municipal councils "partly to raise quality and consistency" ([Rigsrevisionen 2013](https://www.rigsrevisionen.dk/Media/4/C/den-offentlige-ejendomsvurdering.pdf)). By 2014, **95% of homeowners paid property value tax on their 2001 or 2002 valuation**, and in 2013 only one in ten paid land tax on the current valuation ([Engberg report](https://www.skm.dk/media/Skatteministeriet/Dokumenter/PDF%27er/forbedring-af-ejendomsvurderingen_web.pdf); [Tillid til ejendomsvurderingerne, 2013](https://regeringen.dk/media/ag4jilfk/tillid_til_ejendomsvurderingerne.pdf)). Sweden values every property at 75% of market value, split into land value and building value, with general assessments every six years and simplified ones in between ([Fastighetstaxeringslag 1979:1152](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/fastighetstaxeringslag-19791152_sfs-1979-1152/)). After capping bills at 4% of household income (2001) and freezing the base (2006), it replaced the state property tax on homes from 2008 with a municipal fee of SEK 6,000 or 0.75% of assessed value if lower, because the old tax was "unfair and lacks popular support and legitimacy" and rose unpredictably with market values; the opposition protested that "castle and cottage should not be taxed alike", since the fee fell to 0.10% of value on a SEK 6 million villa ([Riksdag committee report 2007/08:SkU10](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/betankande/reformerad-beskattning-av-bostader_gv01sku10/html/); [Lag 2007:1398](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-20071398-om-kommunal-fastighetsavgift_sfs-2007-1398/)). Norway made property tax a municipal option in 1975; under the law's current text homes are taxed on 70% of value at no more than 4‰ ([Eigedomsskattelova](https://lovdata.no/dokument/NL/lov/1975-06-06-29)).

### Wealth taxes retreated, several over unequal valuations

Twelve OECD countries levied annual net wealth taxes in 1990; only four did in 2017 ([OECD 2018](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/04/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_g1g89919/9789264290303-en.pdf)). The OECD lists efficiency costs, capital flight, narrow avoidable bases and high costs relative to small revenues, and adds that "partly as a consequence of valuation issues, many of these hard-to-value assets have been exempted" (same). Germany's ended because of land valuation. On 22 June 1995 the Constitutional Court held that applying one rate to real estate at 140% of 1964-based unit values and to other assets at current values breached equality, and set a deadline of 31 December 1996 ([BVerfG, 2 BvL 37/91](https://www.servat.unibe.ch/dfr/bv093121.html)). No reform found a majority, so the tax has not been levied since 1 January 1997 although the Act remains on the statute book ([Kube 2021](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/833422/a776a6209e9c97f66c820a0ae8fad354/04-Kube.pdf)). The same day the court struck down inheritance-tax valuation on the same ground ([BVerfG, 2 BvR 552/91](https://www.servat.unibe.ch/dfr/bv093165.html)), and in 2006 it found that the replacement shortcut valued built property at anything "from under 20% to over 100%" of market value ([BVerfG press release 11/2007](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/DE/2007/bvg07-011.html)). Sweden, abolishing its wealth tax from 2007, cited "non-uniform valuation rules", an estimated SEK 500 billion held abroad, and the fact that "many small-house owners have come to pay wealth tax because of rapidly rising tax-assessed values" ([Prop. 2007/08:26](https://lagen.nu/prop/2007/08:26)). Denmark abolished its wealth tax in 1997 and Spain zeroed its own with a 100% credit in 2008 ([OECD 2018](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/04/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_g1g89919/9789264290303-en.pdf)).

### Rolls aged in Kenya and Jamaica, and South Africa left site rating

Kenya's rating law required general revaluations every five years, and from 1991 every ten, but "the legislative requirement … has never been achieved": Nairobi was still billing on a roll revalued in **1982**, Machakos on one from 1983 and Mombasa from 1991 when the Lincoln Institute surveyed them ([Franzsen & McCluskey 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)) [secondary]. "Given the static valuations," the city could raise revenue "only by increasing the tax rate", so Nairobi's residential rate climbed from **2.25% of site value in 1982–87 to 17% in 2007–13 and 34% in 2014** (same) [secondary]. Jamaica's Land Valuation Act also called for five-yearly revaluations, but they came roughly every decade, in 1974, 1983, 1992 or 1993 [unresolved] and 2002; tax rates were fixed between revaluations, so inflation eroded revenue, and collection was "in the region of 60%" in the 1990s ([McCluskey & Franzsen 2001](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/100_McCluskeyFranzen01.pdf)) [secondary]. In South Africa about a third of some 500 urban municipalities still used site rating around 2000, chiefly in the former Transvaal (same) [secondary]. The Municipal Property Rates Act, promulgated on 2 July 2005, replaced local choice with a single national base, the **market value of land and buildings as one composite value**, permitted computer-assisted mass appraisal, extended rating to rural land and required general revaluations at least every four or five years ([Franzsen & McCluskey 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)) [secondary]. Tanzania, where all land belongs to the state, taxes only buildings, while owners pay land rent (same) [secondary]. Namibia moved the other way for farmland, introducing a tax on the unimproved site value of commercial farms with a 2002 valuation date, implemented in 2004 at 0.75% for a single Namibian-owned farm and 1.75% for a foreign-owned one (same) [secondary].

## 2009–2026: courts, auditors and algorithms forced a reckoning with old values

### Germany and Denmark rebuilt their valuations under legal and audit pressure

Germany's frozen values were ended by the courts. Acting on referrals from the federal tax court ([BR-Drs. 515/16](http://www.bundesrat.de/SharedDocs/drucksachen/2016/0501-0600/515-16.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=1)), the Constitutional Court on 10 April 2018 held the unit-value rules incompatible with equality "at least since the beginning of 2002": clinging to 1964 "leads to serious and widespread unequal treatment in the valuation of real property", which no administrative saving could justify ([BVerfG press release 21/2018](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/DE/2018/bvg18-021.html)). Its examples were vivid: under 1964 equipment tables, a thermostat-controlled oil or gas heating system counted as "very good equipment", and more than half the housing stock post-dated the valuation ([BVerfG](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Entscheidungen/DE/2018/04/ls20180410_1bvl001114.html)). A new law was due by the end of 2019 and the old values could be used until the end of 2024 (same).

A 2019 constitutional amendment then let each state deviate from the federal rules from 2025, and Germany emerged with three valuation philosophies ([Basic Law, Art. 105](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/gg/art_105.html); [Art. 72](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/gg/art_72.html)). Eleven states use the federal model, which values land as area times the **standard land value** (*Bodenrichtwert*, the average value per square metre of unbuilt land in a zone, set at least every two years by independent local valuation committees from the record of every land sale) and values homes by an income method using statutory average rents (business property by a cost method); it is to be revalued every seven years, and an owner who proves that the result exceeds market value by at least 40% can have it lowered ([BewG § 247](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bewg/__247.html); [§ 221](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bewg/__221.html); [§ 220](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bewg/__220.html); [BauGB § 196](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bbaug/__196.html)). Baden-Württemberg taxes **land value only**, ignoring buildings, making it the closest German case to a classic land value tax ([PwC on the state finance court](https://blogs.pwc.de/de/steuern-und-recht/article/243959/grundsteuer-b-nach-dem-landesgrundsteuergesetz-baden-wuerttemberg-ist-verfassungsmaessig/)) [secondary]. Bavaria taxes area alone, €0.04 per square metre of land and €0.50 per square metre of floor space ([BayGrStG](https://www.gesetze-bayern.de/Content/Document/BayGrStG/true)), while Hesse, Lower Saxony and Hamburg adjust area by a location factor or location class that does not follow prices, Lower Saxony's tax office promising "no automatic increases in value from rising prices" ([Lower Saxony tax office](https://lstn.niedersachsen.de/steuer/grundsteuer/grundsteuer-b-grundvermogen-209755.html)). Owners of about **36 million properties** filed declarations for a valuation date of 1 January 2022, with deadlines extended into 2023 ([BFH, II R 25/24](https://www.bundesfinanzhof.de/en/entscheidungen/entscheidungen-online/decision-detail/STRE202620008/); [Haufe](https://www.haufe.de/steuern/finanzverwaltung/grundsteuererklaerungen-in-deutschland-noch-nicht-abgegeben_164_601820.html)). In 2025, the first year, total revenue was "revenue-neutral" at **€16.1 billion**, but receipts rose 10.4% in Bavaria and fell 4.2% in North Rhine-Westphalia, and individual bills shifted within towns; the new optional surcharge on vacant building plots raised just €5.8 million nationwide ([Destatis, 15 October 2026](https://www.destatis.de/DE/Presse/Pressemitteilungen/2026/10/PD26_369_712.html); [Destatis Realsteuervergleich 2025](https://www.destatis.de/DE/Themen/Staat/Steuern/Steuereinnahmen/Publikationen/Downloads-Realsteuern/statistischer-bericht-realsteuervergleich-2141010257005.xlsx?__blob=publicationFile&v=3)). The federal tax court upheld the federal model in November 2025, calculating that even a 50% error in the statutory rent would change the Cologne plaintiffs' annual bill by about €59, and upheld Baden-Württemberg's land-value model in April 2026; four constitutional complaints were pending in autumn 2026 and hearings on the Hesse and Hamburg models were set for 11 November 2026 ([BFH press release 078/25](https://www.bundesfinanzhof.de/de/presse/pressemeldungen/detail/bundesfinanzhof-haelt-grundsteuer-bundesmodell-fuer-verfassungskonform/); [BFH, pending cases](https://www.bundesfinanzhof.de/de/anhaengige-verfahren/im-fokus-grundsteuer/); [BFH press release 058/26](https://www.bundesfinanzhof.de/de/presse/pressemeldungen/detail/terminvorschau-muendliche-verhandlung-in-verfahren-zum-hamburger-grundsteuergesetz-und-hessischen-grundsteuergesetz-am-mittwoch-11-november-2026)).

Denmark's system failed in public. The National Audit Office reported in August 2013 that land-value zones were correctly drawn in only **17 of 98 municipalities**, and that of houses sold in late 2011, 41% had been valued above their sale price and only 25% fell within the legal target band ([Rigsrevisionen 2013](https://www.rigsrevisionen.dk/Media/4/C/den-offentlige-ejendomsvurdering.pdf)). Valuations were suspended and the 2011 and 2012 values carried forward ([Implementation Centre report 2016](https://regeringen.dk/media/xpfbadgj/nye-og-mere-retvisende-ejendomsvurderinger.pdf)). The Engberg committee found in 2014 that the tax authority's model valued 62.9% of houses within 20% of their sale price, that every averaging model overvalued the cheapest homes and undervalued the dearest, and that land "as if unbuilt" had to be inferred from scarce evidence: about 1,300–1,400 vacant house plots sold freely each year nationwide, and just **128 in Copenhagen in five years** ([Engberg report](https://www.skm.dk/media/Skatteministeriet/Dokumenter/PDF%27er/forbedring-af-ejendomsvurderingen_web.pdf)).

The rebuilt system values land statistically from vacant-plot sales, purchases for demolition and estate agents' valuations; its test values put flat land values 330% above the old figures ([Implementation Centre 2016](https://regeringen.dk/media/xpfbadgj/nye-og-mere-retvisende-ejendomsvurderinger.pdf)). A 2017 political settlement and later laws charge tax on **80% of each valuation**, a "precautionary" deduction reflecting the "natural uncertainty" of ±20% in valuing a home, and add deferral of increases, a rebate guaranteeing existing owners would not pay more, and a ceiling of 30‰ ([Den juridiske vejledning, C.H.4.3.4.1](https://tax.dk/jv/ch/C_H_4_3_4_1.htm); [Tryghed om boligbeskatningen](https://regeringen.dk/media/zjepu0dl/tryghed-om-boligbeskatningen.pdf)). From 2024 each municipality's land tax rate was capped so that its revenue matched the old rules, which cut the average rate from 27‰ to 7.4‰ ([Den juridiske vejledning, C.H.4.3.4.4](https://tax.dk/jv/ch/C_H_4_3_4_4.htm); [Lex: grundskyld](https://lex.dk/grundskyld)) [secondary for the averages]. Delivery still lagged: taxes for 2024–25 were charged on preliminary model valuations with no right of appeal, and the 2024–2027 valuations are made by indexing the 2022–23 ones with price indices, the minister calling this "the least troublesome solution for homeowners in the current situation" ([JV H.A.3.3](https://tax.dk/jv/ha/H_A_3_3.htm); [Ministry press release, 14 June 2026](https://www.skm.dk/aktuelt/presse-nyheder/pressemeddelelser/ro-om-boligskatten-ejendomsvurderinger-for-2026-og-2027-bliver-prisfremskrevet)). Recorded municipal land tax fell from DKK 30.4 billion in 2023 to 23.1 billion in 2024 despite the revenue-neutral caps, for reasons the notes could not establish [unresolved] ([StatBank SKAT](https://www.statbank.dk/SKAT)). On who ultimately bears a land tax, a study using Danish rate changes after 2007 finds land tax changes "fully capitalised" into house prices, so the owner at the time of the change bears it and later buyers pay less for the property ([Høj, Jørgensen & Schou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-5890.12163)) [secondary]; the notes found no Danish study contradicting it, which is not proof that none exists.

### Long-delayed revaluations arrived with caps and cushions

Estonia's revaluation showed how far values drift in two decades. A 2021 test valuation put the country's land at **€30 billion against €4 billion in 2001**, and the 2022 mass valuation that followed was a statistical exercise from registry and sales data with no site visits; some parcels had risen threefold and others thirtyfold, so owners of the slower-rising land had been paying "unfairly more" ([Maa-amet](https://maaruum.ee/sites/default/files/documents/2024-12/Maa%20korraline%20hindamine%20voldik%202022.pdf)). The new values took effect in 2024 with maximum rates cut by a factor of 2.5 to 5 and each bill's rise capped at 10% a year, so that a €30 bill heading for €70 would take eleven years to get there ([Ministry of Finance guidance 2023](https://agri.ee/sites/default/files/documents/2023-09/KOVidele%20uute%20maamaksum%C3%A4%C3%A4rade%20kehtestamise%20juhend%202023.pdf)). The cap was loosened to 50% or €20 in 2025 and handed to municipalities (10–100%) in 2026, when the area-based exemption for home land became a municipal relief of up to €1,000 ([Tax and Customs Board](https://www.emta.ee/eraklient/maksud-ja-tasumine/muud-maksud-ja-nouded/maamaks)). Revenue, flat at €57–59 million a year from 2012 to 2024, reached €80.5 million in 2025 ([Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/gov_10a_taxag/default/table?lang=en)). Valuation is now due every four years, with a buffer year before values apply ([Ministry of Finance guidance 2023](https://agri.ee/sites/default/files/documents/2023-09/KOVidele%20uute%20maamaksum%C3%A4%C3%A4rade%20kehtestamise%20juhend%202023.pdf)), and the 2026 round will apply from 2028 ([ERR](https://www.err.ee/1610109331/keldo-maahindamine-ei-tahenda-automaatselt-maamaksu-tousu)) [secondary].

Jamaica's government told Parliament in 2017 that "we are 15 years overdue": the 2013 valuation had not been implemented, the previous government had instead raised rates on 2002 values, and it had gazetted them without parliamentary approval, requiring a Validation and Indemnity Act ([Ministry of Finance, 11 April 2017](https://mof.gov.jm/wp-content/uploads/speech-to-parliament-by-hmfps-on-property-taxes-april-11-2017.pdf)). Unimproved land values had risen **235% to J$1.9 trillion**, about 130% of GDP; because "we cannot change the valuation results", the government cut rates to bands of 0.5–0.9% so that 58% of properties saw a reduction or no change, expected compliance of only about 57%, and proposed indexing values to inflation between revaluations (same). Bills still rested on 2013 values in 2026, when an islandwide revaluation of about 900,000 parcels began on 1 July; one economist called taxing land "on figures that are nearly a decade old … indefensible" ([Jamaica Gleaner, 12 August 2026](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/business/20260812/nla-revalue-some-900000-land-parcels-property-taxes-expected-rise)) [secondary]; reports disagree on whether it will take twelve or twenty months [unresolved]. Nairobi finally replaced its 1982 values with a 2019 **draft** roll and a rate of 0.115% of current site value, against rates of 25–34% on the old roll; its 2027 rates, gazetted in September 2026 under the National Rating Act 2024, keep 0.115%, bound each bill between its 2022 amount and twice that, and note that only about 50,000 of 250,000 parcels pay consistently ([Kenyans.co.ke, 6 September 2026](https://www.kenyans.co.ke/news/126828-nairobi-sets-2027-land-rates-new-valuation-rules-take-effect)) [secondary]. The 2024 Act reportedly requires rolls every five years and gives counties until about December 2026 to comply ([Kenya Law listing](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2024/15/eng@2025-01-03)) [unresolved], and in September 2026 a court suspended Kajiado county's land rates pending a challenge that it had no valid roll ([Mwakilishi](https://mwakilishi.com/news/2026-09-24/kajiado-landowners-get-relief-as-court-suspends-land-rates-collection)) [snippet].

Elsewhere revaluations came with offsetting rate cuts, or not at all. Finland's building values rest on a method from the 1970s and its broad land-value updates are more than fifteen years old; a reform prepared under five prime ministers was meant to reach parliament in spring 2026, which the notes could not confirm ([Eduskunta](https://www.eduskunta.fi/fi/lakihankkeiden-tietopaketit/kiinteistoverotuksen-arvostamisperusteet)) [unresolved]. Portugal revalued urban property in 2012–13 and since 2016 updates values every three years for inflation ([CIMI art. 138](https://info.portaldasfinancas.gov.pt/pt/informacao_fiscal/codigos_tributarios/cimi/Pages/cimi138.aspx); [vLex](https://vlex.pt/vid/acordao-n-0327-12-1044109692)) [secondary for the 2012–13 date]. Ireland's Local Property Tax is **self-assessed**: owners value their own homes, subject to Revenue review. Its first values, of May 2013, were extended three times, to 2021; at the revaluations of November 2021 and November 2025 the government widened the bands and cut the basic rate from 0.18% to 0.1029% and then 0.0906% ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/tax-professionals/tdm/local-property-tax/part-04/04-01.pdf)). Greece's 2018 revision of its zone-based "objective values" was found insufficient by the Council of State; the 2021 reform created 13,808 zones covering 98% of the population, raised some Mykonos zones from €1,200 to €3,800, and came with a redesigned, revenue-neutral property tax from 2022 ([Ministry of Finance Q&A via CNN Greece](https://www.cnn.gr/oikonomia/story/269016/nees-antikeimenikes-axies-oi-perioxes-me-tis-megalyteres-ayxiseis-kai-tis-megalyteres-meioseis)) [secondary]. Spain's Constitutional Court struck down the formula for its municipal tax on land-value increases three times between 2017 and 2021, holding that an objective method "detached from the reality of the property market" breached the ability-to-pay principle, and the tax was rewritten in November 2021 ([Real Decreto-ley 26/2021](https://www.boe.es/boe/dias/2021/11/09/pdfs/BOE-A-2021-18276.pdf)).

### Britain and France postponed, and England added a new top layer

Britain revalued business property more often and home values not at all. Business revaluations slipped from 2015 to 2017 and from 2021 to 2023, then became three-yearly, the latest taking effect on 1 April 2026 on rents at 1 April 2024 ([NDR Act 2023, Explanatory Notes](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/53/notes/division/3/index.htm); [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/introduction-to-business-rates/revaluation)). Wales legislated in 2024 for five-yearly council tax revaluations from 2028, but on 20 October 2026 its new government paused the revaluation and said it would defer it to the next Senedd term, citing support costs of up to £80 million in the first year; about a quarter of households would have gone up a band and a quarter down, and bands "are still based on 2003 property values" ([Welsh Government, 20 October 2026](https://www.gov.wales/council-tax-revaluation-paused-in-difficult-budget-context)). The Institute for Fiscal Studies called it "an incredibly disappointing decision", noting that values had risen more than 300% in Blaenau Gwent but less than 150% in Cardiff, a reversal of the 2005 pattern ([IFS via Wired-Gov](https://wired-gov.net/wg/news.nsf/articles/Welsh+Government+council+tax+announcement+IFS+response+23102026091500?open=)) [secondary]. England's answer was a new layer on top of the 1991 bands. The **High Value Council Tax Surcharge**, announced at Budget 2025, will charge owners of homes worth £2 million or more in 2026 between £2,500 and £7,500 a year from April 2028 ([GOV.UK policy paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/high-value-council-tax-surcharge-on-residential-properties/additional-council-tax-charge-on-high-value-properties)). For the first time since 1991, some English homes will be valued at current prices: the Valuation Office will use **automated valuation models** (AVMs, statistical models that estimate values from sales data) checked by valuers, publish a draft list in autumn 2027 and revalue every five years ([consultation document](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge/high-value-council-tax-surcharge); [consultation response](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae0e337779378370fd80a85/HVCTS_Summary_of_Responses.pdf)). The government expects about 150,000 individuals to pay; it first cited revenue of "around £430 million" a year, though its October 2026 table shows £365 million for 2028–29, and the surcharge sits in a Finance Bill clause that was not yet law on 4 November 2026 ([GOV.UK policy paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/high-value-council-tax-surcharge-on-residential-properties/additional-council-tax-charge-on-high-value-properties); [consultation document](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/high-value-council-tax-surcharge/high-value-council-tax-surcharge)).

France launched a revision of dwelling rental values in 2019, but the 2026 finance law postponed its entry into tax bases to 1 January 2031, using rents at 1 January 2028 ([DGCL](https://www.collectivites-locales.gouv.fr/index.php/gerer-les-finances-publiques-locales/fiscalite-locale/fiscalite-directe-locale/reforme-des-valeurs-locatives)); a finance-committee amendment of October 2026 to revalue each dwelling whenever it is sold, from 2028, had an unconfirmed fate at the cut-off ([Assemblée nationale](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/17/amendements/3210A/CION_FIN/CF1872)) [unresolved].

### North America tightened caps, and new laws widened permission for split rates

Ontario's 2020 province-wide reassessment was postponed for COVID-19, and a 2023 regulation extended the postponement through the end of the 2021–2024 cycle, so 2026 property taxes still rest on **1 January 2016 values** ([MPAC](https://www.mpac.ca/en/UnderstandingYourAssessment/AssessmentCycle)). Florida went further than capping valuations. A joint resolution passed in June 2026 raises the homestead exemption from non-school levies to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, cuts the cap on non-homestead assessments from 10% to 5% a year, and points to "full elimination" of non-school taxes on homesteads; the House analysis estimated the local revenue loss at $4.95 billion in 2027–28, rising to $11.86 billion a year ([enrolled CS/HJR 1-F](https://flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/BillText/er/PDF); [Florida House analysis](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026F/1F/Analyses/h0001z.SAC.PDF)). On 3 November 2026 voters approved it as Amendment 3 with **about 62%** against the 60% required, according to a media count with 99% of precincts reporting; this is not a certified result, and the official results page could not be reached ([WJXT News4Jax](https://www.news4jax.com/news/local/2026/11/03/florida-voters-to-decide-on-property-taxes-state-reserves-and-farm-equipment-exemptions/); [CBS Miami](https://www.cbsnews.com/miami/news/florida-amendment-3-property-tax-election-results-2026/)) [secondary]. Research meanwhile documented **regressive assessment**, where cheaper homes are assessed at a higher share of their market value than expensive ones. A study for Cook County's board found a $600,000 Chicago home likely carried an effective tax rate 24% lower than a $300,000 home ([ProPublica/Chicago Tribune](https://www.propublica.org/article/cook-county-residential-property-assessment-study-results)) [secondary], and a national study found Black and Hispanic residents bear a "10%–13% higher tax burden for the same bundle of public services" because assessments respond less to neighbourhood conditions than prices do ([Avenancio-León & Howard 2022](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:137:y:2022:i:3:p:1383-1434.)) [secondary].

Split-rate taxation lost its one land-only city and gained new legal footholds. Altoona, Pennsylvania, taxed land only from 2011, at 394 mills because land was a seventh of assessed value, and abandoned it from 2017 after councillors said it discouraged business and deterred neighbours from buying vacant lots ([Altoona Mirror](https://www.altoonamirror.com/news/local-news/2016/06/city-council-decides-to-cut-land-value-tax/)) [secondary]. Detroit proposed in 2023 to cut its building rate from 20 to 6 mills and tax land at 118 mills, revenue-neutrally, but the enabling bill stalled on a reported 50–50 House vote ([BridgeDetroit](https://www.bridgedetroit.com/detroits-land-value-tax-plan-explained/) [secondary]; [Michigan Legislature](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Bills/Bill?ObjectName=2023-HB-4966)). The notes found no later Michigan bill [unresolved]; Detroit's former mayor Mike Duggan ended an independent run for governor in May 2026, and Jocelyn Benson won the governorship on 3 November 2026 according to an Associated Press call, a media projection rather than a certified result ([Bridge Michigan](https://bridgemi.com/michigan-politics/michigan-governors-race-election-results-jocelyn-benson-vs-john-james/)) [secondary]. In 2026 Kentucky's legislature overrode a veto to let Louisville tax land and improvements at different rates, Virginia amended its split-rate authority (2026 Acts c. 100), which now lists eight cities and, for seven of them, requires a building rate above zero and no higher than the land rate, and New York allowed land value taxation inside transit capital-project districts in the Metropolitan Transportation Authority's region ([Kentucky HB 607](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/record/26rs/hb607.html); [Code of Virginia § 58.1-3221.1](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title58.1/chapter32/section58.1-3221.1/); [NY GML § 119-r](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GMU/119-R)). These are permissions, not taxes: the notes confirmed no US jurisdiction outside Pennsylvania billing a split rate on 4 November 2026, and which Pennsylvania municipalities still do is unresolved.

### Australia and New Zealand tried swapping transaction taxes for land taxes

Australia's Henry Review (December 2009) recommended that conveyance **stamp duty**, a one-off tax on buying property, be replaced by a broad land tax, ideally on all land, with rising marginal rates set by value per square metre and levied per holding rather than on an owner's total ([Henry Review](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-10/afts_final_report_part_1_consolidated.pdf)). Modelling for the review ranked land taxes and municipal rates among the least costly taxes per dollar raised and stamp duties among the most costly, results it called "only indicative" (same). Three governments tried versions. The Australian Capital Territory began a twenty-year shift from duty to land-value-based rates, reportedly in 2012 [unresolved for the start year], but a review reported in July 2026 that the substitution "appears to have come to a complete stop", even as the territory abolished duty for first home buyers from 1 July 2026 ([Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9303822/act-stamp-duty-phase-not-on-track-to-meet-timetable-review-says/); [Grant Thornton](https://www.grantthornton.com.au/insights/client-alerts/act-state-budget-2026-27/)) [secondary]. New South Wales let first home buyers choose an annual tax of $400 plus 0.3% of land value instead of duty, but only between January and June 2023 ([Revenue NSW](https://www.revenue.nsw.gov.au/grants-schemes/previous-schemes/first-home-buyer-choice-guide)). Victoria, from 1 July 2024, charges commercial and industrial property duty one last time on sale and then a flat **1% of unimproved land value** a year from ten years later, which the government claims could yield an economic gain of up to $50 billion ([Victorian Treasury](https://www.dtf.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-10/Commercial-and-Industrial-Property-Tax-Reform-Information-Sheet.pdf)). State land taxes, levied on owners' aggregated land above thresholds and using values set by state Valuers-General, raised **$20.0 billion in 2024–25**, up from $7.2 billion in 2015–16, against $34.4 billion from conveyance duty ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/government/taxation-revenue-australia/2024-25/55060DO001_202425.xlsx)).

In New Zealand, by contrast, Parliament required Auckland's merged council to rate on capital value from 2012, which without transition relief would have raised bills by more than 15% for 112,239 ratepayers while cutting them by more than 10% for 111,863 ([DIA, 2012](https://regulation.govt.nz/assets/RIS-Documents/ris-dia-acrar-may12.pdf)). By 2019, 71% of councils rated capital value; the Productivity Commission argued for land value, while the councils' association noted that the Commission itself accepted capital value as "a better proxy for benefit", a tension it said was "never resolved" ([Otago Daily Times](https://www.odt.co.nz/regions/wanaka/commission-councils-setting-rates-wrong) [secondary]; [LGNZ 2020](https://www.lgnz.co.nz/documents/168/Prod-Com-funding-review-analysis.pdf)). Officials estimated in 2009 that a 1% tax on all land would raise $3.8 billion a year while lowering land values by 16.7%; none was adopted ([Tax Working Group 2018](https://taxworkinggroup.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2018-09/twg-bg-3964969-rfrm-and-land-taxes.pdf)).

### East Asia argued about how close official values should sit to market prices

All three East Asian systems tax land on official values deliberately set below market. Korea's government measures the gap as a **realisation rate**, official value divided by market price, so raising it raises taxes without changing tax rates. A November 2020 plan would have lifted official values to 90% of market, land within eight years ([News Tomato](https://newstomato.com/ReadNews.aspx?no=1004797)) [press]; from 2023 the rates were instead rolled back to 2020 levels, **65.5% for land, 69.0% for apartments and 53.6% for detached houses**, and in November 2025 frozen for a fourth year ([Economist (Korea)](https://economist.co.kr/article/view/ecn202211230096); [Bizwatch, 13 November 2025](https://news.bizwatch.co.kr/article/real_estate/2025/11/13/0033)) [press]. Official prices still follow the market at that fixed ratio: 2026 standard land prices rose 3.35% across 600,000 sample parcels ([Newsis](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20251217_0003444123)) [press] and apartment prices 9.13% ([MOLIT via KDI](https://eiec.kdi.re.kr/policy/materialView.do?num=280310)). Japan publishes annual land prices for 26,000 sites (up 2.8% in 2026) and values residential land for its municipal fixed asset tax, every three years, at about 70% of them; that tax raised ¥10.08 trillion, 42.1% of municipal taxes, in FY2024 ([MLIT](https://www.mlit.go.jp/totikensangyo/content/001985434.pdf); [MIC white paper data](https://www.soumu.go.jp/menu_seisaku/hakusyo/chihou/r08data/2026data/csv/z-026.csv)).

Taiwan keeps Sun Yat-sen's design. Local governments re-assess an "announced land value" every two years (reportedly every three before 2017 [unresolved]); owners may declare between 80% and 120% of it, silence counts as 80%, and a declaration below 80% lets the government buy the land at the declared price, a right whose actual use the notes could not establish ([Equalisation of Land Rights Act, Arts. 14–16](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=D0060009)). The annual land value tax runs from 1% to 5.5% (0.2% on an owner's home, though a parallel statute still says 0.3% [unresolved]), and a land value increment tax of 20–40% is charged at sale on the rise in a separate "announced current value" ([Land Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=G0340096)). Alongside the official values, Taiwan has taxed actual sale-price gains through income tax since 2016, and since 2024 that tax has raised more than the increment tax; in 2025 the land value tax raised NT$98.1 billion (2.6% of all taxes) and the increment tax NT$68.3 billion (1.8%), down from 5.1% in 2014 ([Ministry of Finance](https://service.mof.gov.tw/public/Data/statistic/news/11412/6850_11412.xlsx); [Income Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=G0340003)).

### Public landowners saw land revenue fall

Mainland China's dependence on selling land-use rights, known as **land finance**, reversed sharply. Revenue peaked at about 8.4–8.7 trillion yuan in 2020–21, fell to **4.15 trillion yuan in 2025** (a fourth straight double-digit fall) and dropped a further 28.6% in January–August 2026 ([China News Service](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/cj/2026/01-30/10562505.shtml); [Cailianshe](https://www.cls.cn/detail/2277126); [Economic Observer via Stockstar](https://wap.stockstar.com/detail/IG2026092700000139)) [secondary]. A recurrent property tax exists only in pilots begun in Shanghai and Chongqing in 2011, based on purchase prices; a 2021 national authorisation runs for five years from the issue of pilot measures, which the notes found no evidence of ([KPMG](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmg/pdf/2011/02/china-alert-1102-06-c.pdf); [China Daily](https://cn.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202110/23/WS6173e3e7a3107be4979f44ff.html)) [secondary; unresolved]. Hong Kong's land premium fell to HK$17.5 billion in 2025–26, about 2.5% of government revenue by the notes' calculation, and the government now plans on 2% of GDP against a twenty-year average of 3.2% ([Budget speech, 25 February 2026](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202602/25/P2026022500188.htm)); for some lease modifications it now offers published "standard rates" by zone and use instead of case-by-case negotiation ([info.gov.hk](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202504/10/P2025041000564p.htm)). Singapore merged its betterment charges on 1 August 2022 into a **Land Betterment Charge** of 70% of the uplift in land value (100% in some cases), priced from a table of rates by sector and use revised every March and September, with a valuation by the Chief Valuer available on request; land-sale proceeds go into constitutionally protected reserves, and selling state land produces "no net increase in the reserves" ([Singapore Land Authority](https://www.sla.gov.sg/properties/land-betterment-charge/); [MinLaw](https://mlaw.gov.sg/news/parliamentary-speeches/2021-05-10-second-reading-speech-by-2m-edwin-tong-on-land-betterment-charge-bill); [MOF 2022](https://www.mof.gov.sg/news-resources/newsroom/impact-from-sale-of-state-land-on-reserves-and-accounting-treatment-of-cost-of-state-land-for-public-housing/)). Amsterdam, where the city owns about 80% of the land, adopted **perpetual** ground leases in 2016, ending the periodic resets to current land value; existing lessees could switch on terms based on frozen 2014–15 property values with a 35% discount, and about 166,000 applications arrived before the January 2020 deadline ([Amsterdam council inquiry 2021](https://www.gemeente.nu/content/uploads/sites/5/2021/06/Raadsonderzoek-erfpachtbeleid-Amsterdam.pdf)). The council's 2021 inquiry found that the **residual method** (land value derived as property value minus building value) amplifies every swing in house prices, that the 2.39% rent rate was "on the high side", and that total revenue would likely fall below the old system's (same); a study of sales found buyers priced in only about 40% of the present value of future lease payments in 2014–18 ([ESB 2019](https://esb.nu/kopers-in-amsterdam-houden-beperkt-rekening-met-erfpachtbetalingen/)) [secondary].

### Wealth taxes narrowed toward real estate and the very rich

Since 2018 France has taxed only real-estate wealth above €1.3 million, the IFI, self-declared at market value each 1 January with 30% off the main home; the Cour des comptes found in 2024 that it raised €1.8 billion from about 164,000 households, that self-declared values risk "under-revaluation" when prices rise, and that the threshold had not moved since 2018 ([Cour des comptes](https://www.ccomptes.fr/fr/publications/limpot-sur-la-fortune-immobiliere); [service-public.fr](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F563)). A 2% minimum tax on fortunes above €100 million, the "taxe Zucman", passed the National Assembly in February 2025 but failed in the Senate (188 to 129) and again in the 2026 budget ([Public Sénat](https://www.publicsenat.fr/actualites/economie/taxe-zucman-le-senat-rejette-la-creation-dun-nouvel-impot-sur-les-ultra-riches); [JSS](https://jss.fr/post/projet-taxe-zucman-largement-rejete-assemblee-nationale)) [secondary]. Norway values primary homes for its wealth tax at 25% of a statistically estimated value (70% above a threshold); when a new statistical housing model raised more revenue in 2026 than the promised neutral switch, the government lifted the threshold from NOK 10 million to 14 million to hand the money back ([Storting answer, 20 March 2026](https://www.stortinget.no/globalassets/pdf/dokumentserien/2025-2026/dok15-202526-1957-vedlegg.pdf)). Swiss cantons value housing at about 60% of market value and raised about 1% of GDP from wealth taxes in 2016 ([OECD 2018](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/04/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_g1g89919/9789264290303-en.pdf)). California's Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on billionaires' net worth that excludes directly held real estate, was trailing in early returns on 4 November 2026, an early media count with votes still being counted ([LAO](https://www.lao.ca.gov/ballot/2025/250481.pdf); [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/health/2026/11/california-election-2026-prop-40-41-42/); [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/news/12107038/california-billionaire-tax-trails-in-early-results)) [secondary]. The evidence on how the wealthy respond is disputed. Studies of Denmark and Colombia find large responses at the top, through accumulation, avoidance and offshore concealment, while a study of Sweden finds small elasticities driven mostly by under-reporting ([Jakobsen et al.](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24371/w24371.pdf); [Londoño-Vélez & Ávila-Mahecha](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20200319); [Seim 2017](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/course/seimAEJ17wealth.pdf)) [secondary], and France's audit office has seen more returning emigrants than departures since 2018 without claiming a causal link ([Cour des comptes](https://www.ccomptes.fr/fr/publications/limpot-sur-la-fortune-immobiliere)).

## 4 November 2026: how land is taxed today, and how it is valued

On the cut-off date land is taxed in five main ways, often side by side in one country. The most common is a **recurrent property tax on land and buildings together**: Japan's fixed asset tax, Germany's Grundsteuer (€16.1 billion in 2025, about 1.6% of all German taxes by the notes' calculation from [Destatis](https://www.destatis.de/DE/Presse/Pressemitteilungen/2026/10/PD26_369_712.html)), England's council tax, American and Canadian property taxes, Ireland's Local Property Tax, France's taxe foncière and South Africa's municipal rates. These are revalued anywhere from every year (the Netherlands, Hong Kong) to not at all since 1970 (French homes), and a few systems value nothing at all, taxing floor and land area instead: Poland, Czechia and Bavaria ([Polish Ministry of Finance](https://www.podatki.gov.pl/media/1p1hokoz/wysokość-stawek-w-opodatkowaniu-nieruchomości-w-2026-r.pdf); [Czech Act No. 338/1992](https://www.zakonyprolidi.cz/cs/1992-338); [BayGrStG](https://www.gesetze-bayern.de/Content/Document/BayGrStG/true)).

The second is an **annual tax on land value alone**, or on land at a higher rate than buildings. Australia's state land taxes raised $20.0 billion in 2024–25, about 14% of state taxation by the notes' calculation ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/government/taxation-revenue-australia/2024-25/55060DO001_202425.xlsx)); Denmark's municipal grundskyld raised DKK 23.7 billion in 2025 ([StatBank SKAT](https://www.statbank.dk/SKAT)); Taiwan's land value tax NT$98.1 billion ([Ministry of Finance](https://service.mof.gov.tw/public/Data/statistic/news/11412/6850_11412.xlsx)); and Estonia's land tax €80.5 million ([Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/gov_10a_taxag/default/table?lang=en)). Baden-Württemberg, Jamaica, Nairobi and other Kenyan counties, Namibia's commercial farms, Fiji, the Solomon Islands and some francophone African countries ([Franzsen & McCluskey 2017](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/property-tax-in-africa-full_0.pdf)) [secondary], a minority of New Zealand councils and some Pennsylvania cities, whose current number is unresolved, belong here too. Finland and Sweden value land and buildings separately within a broader tax; in Finland land supplies about 28% of 2026 property tax ([Verohallinto](https://www.vero.fi/tietoa-verohallinnosta/tilastot/kiinteistoverotilastoj/ennakkotietoa-kiinteistoverotuksesta-2026/)).

The third taxes **increases in land value or the betterment from public decisions**: Taiwan's increment tax, Singapore's Land Betterment Charge, Victoria's windfall gains tax on rezoning ([State Revenue Office](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/windfall-gains-tax)), Spain's municipal plusvalía and China's land appreciation tax, which raised 410 billion yuan in 2025 ([China News Service](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/cj/2026/01-30/10562505.shtml)) [secondary]. Britain abolished its last tax on development value in 1985. The fourth is **public ownership**, with land value collected as lease premiums, ground rents and conveyance fees in Hong Kong, Singapore, mainland China, Amsterdam and Canberra. The fifth is **wealth taxation** that counts real estate, in Norway, Spain, the Swiss cantons, France (real estate only), Colombia and Argentina, almost always at values below market ([OECD 2018](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/04/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_g1g89919/9789264290303-en.pdf)).

Valuing land on its own is now a codified craft, and its limits are part of the debate. The professional standard of the International Association of Assessing Officers calls sales comparison, using sales of vacant land, "the primary approach to land valuation", and where such sales are scarce lists alternatives: **allocation** (a typical land share of total value), **extraction** (sale price minus the depreciated cost of the building), anticipated use and land-residual capitalisation ([IAAO 2025](https://www.iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Mass_Appraisal.pdf)). Several countries publish zone values instead, such as Germany's standard land values and Japan's published prices. **Mass appraisal** models, increasingly machine-learning ones, estimate whole-property values, and splitting out the land takes a separate step: Cook County's assessor uses a gradient-boosted model for totals but takes land from fixed rates per square foot set by its valuation staff ([CCAO model documentation](https://github.com/ccao-data/model-res-avm)). Quality is tested by **ratio studies**, comparing assessed values with sale prices: the standard accepts a typical deviation (the coefficient of dispersion, COD) of 5–10 or 5–15 for homes but 5–20 or 5–25 for vacant land, and a price-related differential between 0.98 and 1.03 as the test of whether cheap and dear homes are assessed alike ([IAAO 2025](https://www.iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Mass_Appraisal.pdf)). Australian valuers describe residual land values for complex investment property as "complex, inaccurate and prone to debate and challenge" ([Boyd & Boyd 2011](https://www.prres.org/uploads/727/1831/Boyd_Valuing_the_land_component.pdf)) [secondary], while advocates of land taxation argue that land is easier to value because location drives its price; the notes found no study measuring land-only and whole-property accuracy head to head, so the question is open. On how current values should be, the IAAO treats annual assessment as the goal, with physical reinspection every four to six years, and the OECD warned in 2022 that "many countries levy recurrent taxes on immovable property on significantly outdated property values", reducing both fairness and revenue ([IAAO 2025](https://www.iaao.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_on_Mass_Appraisal.pdf); [OECD 2022](https://www.oecd.org/tax/tax-policy/brochure-housing-taxation-in-oecd-countries.pdf)).

## Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Acquisition-value (base-year) assessment | Taxing a property on what was paid for it, indexed by a capped factor, instead of its current value (California). |
| Annual (rental) value | The yearly rent a property could fetch; the base of English-style rates. Also "rateable value" (Hong Kong) or "annual value" (Singapore). |
| Antecedent valuation date | The date at which values are estimated, often years before a valuation list takes effect. |
| Assessment cap | A legal limit on how fast a property's assessed value may rise (e.g. Florida's 3%). A rate cap limits the tax rate instead. |
| Automated valuation model (AVM), mass appraisal | Statistical models that value many properties at once from sales and property data, usually checked by valuers. |
| Banding | Placing properties in value ranges that each carry a fixed charge (council tax). |
| Betterment, development value | The rise in land value caused by planning permission, rezoning or public investment; taxes on it are betterment charges or levies. |
| Cadastre (matrikel, catasto) | The official register and map of land parcels, originally built for taxation. |
| Capital value, capital improved value | The value of land and buildings together: the likely sale price of the whole property. |
| Coefficient of dispersion (COD), price-related differential (PRD) | Ratio-study measures of how evenly assessments track sale prices, and whether cheap and dear properties are treated alike. |
| Ground lease (erfpacht), premium, canon | Public landowner leases land; a premium is paid up front, a canon or ground rent yearly. |
| Hartkorn | Danish notional measure of a farm's grain-producing capacity, the tax base from 1662 to 1903. |
| Increment tax | A tax on the rise in land value between two dates, usually charged at sale. |
| Indexation | Raising all values by a common percentage (inflation) without revaluing; preserves existing distortions. |
| Land-use right, land finance | China's time-limited rights to use state land, and local governments' reliance on selling them. |
| Quota (apportioned) tax | A tax where each area's total is fixed and shared among owners, so values stop mattering to the total. |
| Rates, rating | The British and Commonwealth name for local property tax. Site rating taxes land only; flat rating land and buildings together; composite rating both at different rates. |
| Realisation rate | South Korea's ratio of official value to market price. |
| Residual method, extraction | Deriving land value as property value minus the value of the building; errors in building cost pass into the land figure. |
| Revaluation | Re-estimating every value at a new date; it changes who pays what, which is why it is politically hard. |
| Self-assessment | Owners state their own values, checked by officials (Rome, Australia 1910, Ireland, France's IFI); Taiwan adds a state purchase right. |
| Split-rate (graded) tax | One property tax with a higher rate on land than on buildings; a pure land value tax sets the building rate to zero. |
| Standard land value (Bodenrichtwert), published land price, route price | Official zone or street values per square metre of land, set by committees or appraisers from sales (Germany, Japan). |
| Unimproved value, site value, land value | The value of a plot as if its buildings and other improvements did not exist; laws differ on whether works like clearing or levelling count. |
| Unit value (Einheitswert) | Germany's and Austria's single official tax value per property, fixed at a main assessment date (1935, 1964, 1973). |
| Wealth tax (net) | An annual tax on a person's assets minus debts; it usually borrows property valuations for real estate. |
