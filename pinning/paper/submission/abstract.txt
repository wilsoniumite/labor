We connect technological change to wages by jointly pricing labor’s machine substitute and workers’ outside options. Combining task assignment with a production network identifies two automation channels: improved machine capability and reduced labor requirements within machine production. Both can lower wages relative to rents on non-produced inputs. At an interior task margin, we derive bounds on wage purchasing power: embodied rent costs impose a ceiling, while feasible human production supplies a lower bound. Technological progress can expand productive capacity while reducing wage purchasing power over housing and other scarce services. The same prices connect participation to household or public support, living costs, and preferences. U.S. wage measures deflated by durables and shelter prices diverged by a factor of 4.8 over 1964–2024. Uniform transfers financed from pure rents redistribute purchasing power while preserving fixed-factor supply and avoiding benefit withdrawal upon employment.
